## Research: a real, openly licensed case study for the advanced-core capstone

**Question.** Find ONE real case study from a top university — free to the public and legally reusable — to serve as the capstone after the advanced core: solvable in Excel, SQL or Python, domain-neutral, with an open answer (several defensible decisions, graded on reasoning), and designed for the AI era (learners will use AI; the case and its reference answer are built for that).

**Method.** Five evidence clusters, all primary sources, accessed 2026-11-20, license lines quoted verbatim: MIT + Harvard; Stanford + Berkeley + CMU; open case collections; licensing compatibility + alternative datasets; AI-era assessment design. The top candidate was also tested live: its data file was downloaded, a ground-truth analysis computed independently, and the capstone question put to a current AI assistant (Muse Spark, 2026-11-20) whose answer was graded number by number. Detail in the evidence comments on this issue.

## Key findings

1. **No top-university case is both free and freely reusable in full.** The strongest university cases sit under NonCommercial and/or ShareAlike licenses (MIT OCW and CMU OLI: CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; Berkeley textbooks: CC BY-NC-ND 4.0; Berkeley course materials: CC BY-NC 4.0) or behind a paywall (Harvard Business School). The lawful pattern for every university winner is the same: **link to their case, host nothing of theirs, write our own brief** — and let learners download the data from the source.
2. **MIT OCW's license, verbatim, bars the obvious route.** OCW's terms name "Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)" and MIT adds that users "may not sell, profit from, or commercialize OCW materials or works derived from them." Under CC's own ShareAlike rule, an adaptation's license "must be a Creative Commons license with the same License Elements" — so OCW text or data can never be republished inside our CC BY 4.0 pages.
3. **HBS cases are excluded, on HBP's own words.** Cases are sold per copy (a current case lists at $11.95 with "purchase one copy per user" copyright permission), HBP's terms claim the contents as "the sole property of HBP or its licensors" with no copying or derivatives without written permission, and its permissions page states "we do not grant permission for any adaptation, edit, excerpt or summary of any case." Baker Library confirms active cases go only to enrolled HBS students; everyone else must purchase.
4. **The strongest case is MIT 15.071's "Understanding Why People Vote"** — a real randomized field experiment (Gerber, Green & Larimer, Michigan 2006): 344,084 voters, 8 columns, one clean CSV (6.2 MB), an everyday domain, built-in uncertainty, partial worked answers on the OCW page, and — decisive for the open-answer requirement — a decision with several defensible answers (the most effective mailing is also the most ethically uncomfortable one; the cheaper, gentler option is defensible on stated grounds).
5. **The runner-up is a journal case, not a course case.** Peterson & Ziegler's LEGO pricing case (*Journal of Statistics and Data Science Education*, 2021; 1,304 sets × 14 variables) has article text under CC BY 4.0 — the only candidate whose *text* we may adapt — but its data rides as a journal supplement with no separate license line, so the data route is still link + own brief.
6. **Berkeley's best case outsources its data problem to a county.** Data 100's Cook County property-assessment case (recurring, Fall 2026 and Spring 2026) has the richest decision (build a valuation model that is accurate *and* fair) and a published reference narrative — but Berkeley states no license for its notes or the data on the course page. The data originates with Cook County's open-data program; the capstone route is our own brief over the county's data, after verifying the county portal's license (the one open check in this package).
7. **Tested live, a current AI assistant gets this case's headline numbers right — and still leaves the capstone's real work undone.** Given the brief and the actual file with code execution (Muse Spark, 2026-11-20), the assistant's turnout rates, effects and confidence intervals all matched an independently computed ground truth. What it did not do is what the capstone grades: it fitted no model, ran no data-quality checks (it never mentioned that 342,517 of 344,084 rows are exact duplicates of another row — a structural feature of an 8-column file, not an error), headlined an age-targeting recommendation built on post-hoc subgroup slicing, and could not check household clustering because the file has no household identifier. Detail in §6.
8. **Assessment research supports designing *for* AI use, explicitly.** The AI Assessment Scale (Perkins et al., 2024) makes the permitted level part of the task — this capstone is a natural Level 4 "Full AI" task. Bearman et al. (2024) separate two assessable acts: judging AI *outputs* and judging AI *processes*. The OECD/EC AI Literacy Framework's Engage competency is, verbatim, "Evaluate whether AI outputs should be accepted, revised or rejected" — the verification log is that competency, not a house rule.
9. **Assistants fail measurably exactly where this capstone can be strong.** On Spider 2.0 (ICLR 2025), the same o1-preview agent scores 91.2% on clean single-query SQL but 21.3% on real enterprise workflows; on DABstep (2025), whose hard tasks require "iterative data filtering and cross-referencing," the best agent reaches 14.55%. Quirks visible only by running the data, and context that lives only in the brief, are the documented weak spot — UC Berkeley's and Western University's teaching centers recommend exactly those design moves.
10. **If the team ever wants a dataset it may host outright, the frictionless options exist** — UCI's Bike Sharing dataset (CC BY 4.0, 17,389 hourly rows, two grains), FiveThirtyEight's college-majors data (CC BY 4.0), NYC Open Data and NOAA (public-domain terms). None is a university *case*; all would need our own case written around them (§7).

## Licensing verdicts — what a CC BY 4.0 site may do

- **MIT OCW candidates (1, 5, 6): link only + write our own brief.** Linking triggers neither the NC term nor ShareAlike. Adapting OCW text, or hosting its CSVs, would force CC BY-NC-SA on the result — incompatible with our CC BY 4.0 pages. Learners download data from OCW. MIT's name may be credited, not used as branding.
- **LEGO (2): adapt the article's text with attribution (CC BY 4.0); link for the data.** The dataset is the article's supplement with no license line of its own; host nothing unless the authors confirm.
- **Berkeley CCAO (3): link to Berkeley's case study; build our brief on the county's data** once the Cook County portal's license is verified. Berkeley's own text: link only (textbook CC BY-NC-ND; notes state no license).
- **OpenIntro (4): link only.** Text is CC BY-SA 3.0 (an adaptation must stay BY-SA 3.0) and the data package is GPL-3; neither folds into CC BY 4.0 pages.
- **FiveThirtyEight (7): link, adapt, and host the data with attribution (CC BY 4.0)** — the only candidate with an unambiguous hostable data license; it is not a university case and its data are from 2010–2012.
- **Open Case Studies (9): link only** (CC BY-NC cannot be republished as CC BY). **Open Policing (10): the data is reusable under ODC-By with attribution**, but its scale (the site itself warns datasets are "too large for Excel") and sensitive domain rule it out here.
- **General rules (from CC's own texts):** CC0 and US federal public-domain material may be hosted and adapted with no conditions ("You can copy, modify, distribute and perform the work, even for commercial purposes, all without asking permission"). "Free for educational use" is not an open license — e.g. the *Introduction to Statistical Learning* site keeps its slides "All rights reserved" while allowing use "for non-profit educational purposes": link only.

## The capstone, sketched for #1

**The case (our own brief, linking OCW's assignment and data).** *The 40,000-letter decision.* "A civic-engagement campaign is preparing for a low-turnout primary. In 2006, researchers randomly assigned ~344,000 Michigan voters to no letter or one of four mailings of escalating social pressure (Civic Duty; Hawthorne; Self; Neighbors — descriptions in the linked source). The campaign can afford at most 40,000 letters. Decide: which mailing, if any, should it send — and should it target particular voters rather than mail at random? Defend the decision with numbers and uncertainty, state what would change your mind, and keep a verification log of every AI output you used."

**Why the answer is open.** The most effective mailing (Neighbors, +8.1 pp) is also the one that shows people their neighbors' records — a campaign may defensibly reject it on ethics/reputation grounds and choose Self (+4.9 pp) or a targeted Neighbors mailing; a campaign may also defensibly argue the 2006 primary does not transfer to its election and propose a pilot instead. All can earn full marks; a bare "send Neighbors, it has the biggest number" cannot.

**Deliverables.** (1) A cleaned analysis table (one row per voter, group as one column — the modeling step) with a short data-quality note; (2) one model or schema (e.g. a logistic model or a group×segment effect table) with a one-line justification; (3) 3–5 key numbers with intervals (turnout by group; each mailing's effect vs control; expected extra votes per 40,000 letters for the chosen plan); (4) one honest chart (effects with interval whiskers, zero visible); (5) a one-page decision memo (decision, evidence, risks, what would change it); (6) a verification log for all AI use (output → check performed → accept / revise / reject + reason); (7) an explain-back paragraph and a closing reflection (where AI helped, where it failed).

**Ground truth (computed for this research, 2026-11-20).** 344,084 rows; no missing values; every row carries exactly one group flag. Turnout: control 29.7% (n=191,243); Civic Duty 31.5% (+1.8 pp, 95% CI 1.3–2.3); Hawthorne 32.2% (+2.6, 2.1–3.1); Self 34.5% (+4.9, 4.3–5.4); Neighbors 37.8% (+8.1, 7.6–8.7). Neighbors ≈ 3,250 extra votes per 40,000 letters (3,040–3,460). The Neighbors effect is essentially identical by sex (+8.2 men, +8.1 women) and smaller for voters in their twenties (+3.7 pp) than at 60–69 (+10.8 pp); targeting ages 30–69 lifts the yield only ~8% (≈3,500). Data quirks: 342,517 rows are exact duplicates of another row (inevitable with 8 low-cardinality columns — different voters, not duplicate records; there is no ID column); 88 voters have yob before 1910; the file has no household identifier, so clustering cannot be checked.

**Rubric (grades reasoning, not one number).** 1) Question framed as a decision with a budget and a decision-maker. 2) Grain and modeling: one row per voter; group handled as one variable; duplicates correctly understood (not deleted). 3) Cleaning and data-quality checks actually run and reported (missingness, group exclusivity, implausible yob). 4) Numbers correct, with intervals, in percentage points (not % vs pp confusion). 5) Uncertainty and limits stated: one election/state/year; clustering uncheckable; subgroup claims hedged. 6) Chart honest (intervals shown, no truncated axis). 7) Decision follows from the evidence *and* addresses the ethics/cost trade-off; states what would change it. 8) Verification log complete and specific; explain-back in the learner's own words. **Time: about 4 hours, max 6.**

## AI-era design rules (each tied to its source)

1. **Print the AI rules on the brief: this is a Full-AI task (AIAS Level 4); the documentation is graded.** (Perkins et al. 2024; Berkeley CTL on stating permitted levels.)
2. **Decisive context lives only in the brief** — the budget, the decision-maker, the constraint — nowhere public; generic advice that ignores it is visibly wrong. (Western CTL: GenAI is "not able to draw on the specifics of these examples in their responses"; Berkeley CTL on course-specific relevance.)
3. **The dataset carries unannounced quirks visible only by running it** (here: the duplicate structure, the pre-1910 births, the missing household ID); finding them is the cleaning grade. Prefer real quirks; plant at most one, disclosed in teacher notes. [Recommendation] (DABstep/Spider 2.0 failure evidence.)
4. **Verification log: one row per AI output — output, check performed, accept / revise / reject + reason.** (OECD/EC Engage 3, Advanced: "Learners assess AI-generated outputs and justify decisions to accept, revise or reject them"; Bearman et al. 2024, focus 1.)
5. **The rubric grades the chain, not the answer** — several defensible decisions score full marks. (Western CTL: "nuanced real-world examples without a single correct answer"; Berkeley CTL: assess "how they got there"; TEQSA: assess "the process of learning.")
6. **Explain-back in the learner's own words**: method, why, and the one number they would defend. (Bearman et al. 2024, focus 2 — judging AI *processes*.)
7. **Closing reflection**: where AI helped, where it failed, one thing they will check by hand from now on — graded for specificity. (Berkeley CTL "reflective documentation"; Western CTL metacognitive element.)
8. **The reference answer ships an "the author knew you would use AI" section** (§6), re-verified against a current model at each review, with model and date stated. [Recommendation] (Berkeley CTL: GenAI outputs as "objects of critique.")

## The live AI test, and the draft reference-answer section

**Test protocol (2026-11-20).** The capstone question above was given, with the actual `gerber.csv`, to a current general-purpose AI assistant (Muse Spark) with code execution, in a fresh session with no hints about traps. Its full answer is preserved in the evidence for this package.

**What it got right.** Every headline number matched the independently computed ground truth: baseline 29.7%; effects +1.8 / +2.6 / +4.9 / +8.1 pp with intervals matching to rounding; ≈3,250 extra votes per 40,000 Neighbors letters; no sex targeting (+8.2 vs +8.1 pp); the age pattern (twenties +3.7 pp; 60–69 +10.8 pp; targeting 30–69 ≈ 3,500 per 40,000, "about 81%" of the electorate — verified 81.1%). It flagged the ethics of the Neighbors letter, the one-election limit, and the uncheckable clustering. It invented no variables and no sources — it correctly noted that past voting history, the usual targeting variable, is *not* in the file.

**What it skipped or got subtly wrong.** (1) It never mentioned the duplicate structure — it neither fell for it nor explained it, so a learner who asks a follow-up ("should I drop duplicates?") is unprotected. (2) It fitted no model and produced no schema — raw differences only (defensible for a randomized experiment, short of the capstone's deliverable). (3) It ran no reported data-quality checks (no missingness, exclusivity, or yob-plausibility check). (4) Its headline recommendation — "target ages 30–69" — rests on post-hoc subgroup slicing; only the youngest band's interval clearly separates, a caution it noted mildly but did not apply to its own headline. (5) It quoted tight intervals in the headline while its own caveat (possible household clustering) implies they may be understated.

**Draft — "The author knew you would use AI" (for the reference answer).** *The author knew you would use AI, so this section is not a warning; it is the answer key for the part AI does worst. When we ran this case past a current AI assistant (Muse Spark, 20 Nov 2026) with the data file, its main numbers were right — check yours against §5's ground truth, and if yours differ, find out why before you submit. Here is what it skipped, and the check that catches each skip. (1) It never looked at the rows: 342,517 of 344,084 rows are exact copies of another row. That is not dirty data — with only 8 columns, different voters must share rows, and there is no ID column. If your AI told you to "remove duplicates," it just deleted your electorate; the check is one question: what does one row mean here? (2) It fitted no model. Raw differences are a fine start in a randomized experiment, but your deliverable asks for one model or schema — an AI that stops at averages has done the easy half. (3) It recommended targeting voters aged 30–69 from slicing the data after seeing it. Only the youngest voters clearly respond less; the rest of the "gain" is about 8% and rests on differences the intervals barely support. If your memo recommends targeting, it must say which subgroup differences are actually established. (4) It could not check whether voters were assigned by household, because the file has no household column — and neither can you. A good memo says so, instead of quoting intervals as if they were exact. What a strong submission does differently: it runs the checks (row meaning, missingness, group exclusivity, odd birth years), it makes the ethics trade-off explicitly rather than letting the biggest number decide, and its verification log shows at least one AI output it revised or rejected — with the reason.*

## Alternatives, if no university case is chosen

Ranked for building our own case around openly licensed data:

1. **UCI Bike Sharing Dataset (CC BY 4.0)** — 17,389 hourly rows + 731 daily rows (two grains of one system), weather/season fields; decision: next season's fleet and rebalancing plan. The license line is the repository's standard: "This dataset is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license." (Confirm the line on the Bike Sharing page itself when linking; verified verbatim on a sibling UCI page.)
2. **NYC Open Data — 2015 Street Tree Census** — 666,134 trees × 45 columns; the City's Technical Standards Manual states datasets become "a public resource available to anyone, without restriction or licensing requirements." Decision: where to fund planting/stump removal. Quote the term; do not relabel it CC BY. Use a one-borough extract for Excel.
3. **NOAA Storm Events Database (public domain)** — federal works are outside copyright (17 U.S.C. §105); NWS: its information is "in the public domain… may be used without charge for any lawful purpose." Scope to a year/state subset; decision: compare severe-weather risk between two counties.
4. **Harvard Dataverse CC0 deposits** — the repository's terms publish datasets "with a default CC0 default public domain dedication waiver agreement," but depositors may override: verify the chosen dataset's own Terms tab before use. Named example: a 988-respondent survey experiment (Jankowski 2023, DOI 10.7910/DVN/QNOMXJ) — the cleanest small designed-experiment option.
5. **Our World in Data** — CC BY covers OWID's *own* production only; third-party series keep their providers' terms (OWID's FAQ says to check before redistributing). Use as a linked reading source, not a hosted dataset, unless the series is OWID-produced.
