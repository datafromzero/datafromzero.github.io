Synthesis of every platform studied in Waves 1–2, as verified in Wave 4. All figures were checked on 2026-11-15 against pages opened in the research sessions; where a vendor's own pages contradict each other, both versions are shown and neither is blended into a single total. See `00-README.md` §4 for the honesty rules that govern every number in this file.

How to read this map: §1 is the comparison at a glance. §2 profiles each platform or platform group. §3 compares paywall and free models. §4 states the cross-cutting structural findings. §5 names the gaps Data From Zero exploits, in priority order.

## 2. Platform profiles

### 2.1 DataCamp — the interactive subscription

**Positioning.** One subscription, every data role, learned by doing in the browser. Short videos alternate with interactive exercises; no local installs. The catalog is organized into career tracks, skill tracks, standalone courses, projects, assessments, and DataCamp's own certifications.

**Scale (checked 2026-11-15).** 810+ courses, 33 career tracks, and 118 skill tracks, confirmed verbatim on DataCamp's pricing and track-index pages in Wave 4. Track hour totals differ between DataCamp's own pages; per the honesty rules, this map does not present any single catalog-hour total for DataCamp.

**Price formulation (use exactly this shape, dated).** List price about $28 per month billed annually; the pricing page simultaneously promotes an $11 per month special price (checked 2026-11-15). A Teams tier is shown at the same $11 per user per month billed annually; Enterprise pricing is not public. A student plan is advertised at over 50% off.

**What it teaches best.** The beginner SQL ladder is the benchmark in this map: querying, joins, window functions, a PostgreSQL function toolkit, and database design inside the fundamentals track itself, in one dialect, with projects interleaved. Classical ML sequencing (supervised → unsupervised → trees → boosting → validation → tuning) is deep and well ordered, and the modern-data-stack bench (dbt, Airflow, Snowflake, Databricks, Kafka) is broader than any rival's. The Power BI line, co-created with Microsoft and aligned to the PL-300 exam, is the deepest single-BI-tool curriculum found. Its AI line runs literacy → API builder → LangChain and agents, with current vendor surface (OpenAI APIs, Hugging Face, Pinecone, Model Context Protocol).

**Where it is thin.** Almost everything past chapter 1 is paywalled, so no free full pathway exists. Deep learning is compressed relative to classical ML; math for ML is nearly absent (one linear-algebra course, in R); query optimization and indexing are not a visible pillar; dialect breadth beyond PostgreSQL is shallow. The same courses recycle across tracks, so a learner following two tracks re-takes identical content. Communication and storytelling are sold as a separate layer rather than woven into projects, and no interview-prep or portfolio-review layer is visible in the track structure.

**Paywall shape.** Free at the edges — first chapter of every course, cheat sheets, tutorials, blog, podcast, and a notebook workspace — with the core paywalled from chapter 2 onward. Certifications are bundled into the paid tier rather than sold per exam.

### 2.2 Coursera — the credential marketplace

**Positioning.** Career credentials carrying the Google, IBM, Meta, and university brands, with shareable certificates, Credly badges, and — for several programs — ACE and FIBAA recommendations worth up to 12 US college credits and 6 ECTS, subject to each receiving institution. Google certificates plug into an employer consortium (150+ US employers named on the page); IBM certificates plug into IBM's Talent Network.

**Scale and the contradiction finding.** Coursera's own pages disagree about its own programs, and Wave 4 re-verified that the disagreement is real and still present. It is a finding about the platform, not a research error:

- Google Data Analytics: Coursera's program page states 9 courses and over 180 hours; Google's own grow.google page lists 8 courses and about 240 hours; a tally of Coursera's own course cards sums to about 152 hours. Three official-ish numbers for one program.
- IBM Data Analyst: 11 courses in the current V3 version (older issued badges show 9 courses), about 151 learning hours. Use the versioned formulation; do not drop the version note.
- IBM Data Engineering: the current listing enumerates 16 courses ending with generative-AI and career-guide courses, while the badge criteria text attaches about 217 learning hours to a 13-course set, and older marketing pages describe 13 courses with timelines up to 12–15 months. State the count and the hours as two separately sourced, versioned facts — never as the single blended figure "16 courses / 217 hours."

**Price model.** About $49 per month per program on the Google and IBM program FAQs (some programs run $51–59), or Coursera Plus at $59 per month or $399 per year — with the caveat, noted by reviewers, that several IBM certificates are not included in Plus. The monthly meter means the slowest beginners pay the most for the same certificate; headline durations ("as little as 4 months") sit next to FAQ admissions of much longer realistic timelines (about 8 months for Python for Everybody; up to 12–15 months circulated for IBM Data Engineering).

**What it teaches best.** The Google Data Analytics certificate is the clearest beginner on-ramp on the platform: one organizing workflow frame (Ask → Prepare → Process → Analyze → Share → Act), a capstone case study, and a job-search course. Macquarie's Excel Skills for Business (4 courses, about 106–107 hours) is the most complete Excel curriculum on Coursera, with in-workbook practice. Michigan's PostgreSQL for Everybody goes deeper on how databases work than any analyst certificate goes on querying, and UC Davis teaches A/B testing in SQL. Duke's Statistics with R (inference, regression, a full Bayesian course, capstone) and Stanford's single-course statistics survey are the strongest statistics offers. DeepLearning.AI's Machine Learning and Deep Learning specializations have the cleanest concept sequencing in the ML/DL layer, and IBM AI Engineering is the only flagship that reaches LLM fine-tuning, RAG, and agents.

**Where it is thin.** Fragmentation and duplication: one vendor sells four overlapping data certificates sharing whole courses, with no guidance on which job each shared course serves. Statistics is structurally underweight where beginners actually are — the most-taken analyst certificate (Google Data Analytics) has no statistics course at all; hypothesis testing and experiments appear only in Meta's analyst program and in Google's advanced certificate. No flagship analyst certificate contains a deep SQL course: window functions, CTEs, and optimization are nobody's headline content. Python depth is fragmented — Michigan's roughly 79-hour general path teaches no pandas or NumPy, IBM's roughly 25-hour shared course is a syntax on-ramp, and Google's analyst Python is a single course. Prerequisite cliffs hide behind "beginner" labels (calculus and linear algebra noted as assets late in IBM Data Science; the Deep Learning Specialization assumes Python and linear algebra). Since August 2025 the free tier is a first-module preview, and enrolling in a single course that belongs to a certificate automatically subscribes the learner to the full certificate — a pattern admitted in Coursera's own FAQ.

**What a free site cannot copy, and should not pretend to.** Recognized verifiable credentials with credit recommendations, trusted graded assessment at scale, managed lab environments, employer distribution plumbing, and identity machinery. Data From Zero's counter is not a rival credential; it is interview-style checks, portfolio projects, and public progress artifacts (see §5).

### 2.3 Dataquest — sequencing as the product

**Positioning.** No videos at all. Every lesson is a short concept followed immediately by code written and checked in the browser against real datasets. Paths are fully sequenced so the learner never decides what comes next, and path pages state courses, projects, hours, and enrolled learners up front.

**Scale.** Eight career paths in the catalog listing: Junior Data Analyst (19 courses, 15 projects, 97 hours; Excel first, then SQL, then Python), Data Analyst in Python (27 courses, 19 projects, 144 hours), Data Analyst in R, Data Scientist in Python (38 courses, 27 projects, 171 hours), Data Engineer (30 courses, 14 projects, 81 hours), AI Engineer in Python (the most current AI curriculum among the interactive platforms: LLM APIs, prompt engineering, embeddings, vector databases, RAG, FastAPI deployment), and Business Analyst paths for Power BI and Tableau tied to vendor certifications. The homepage shows slightly different counts for two paths; the catalog figures above are the more detailed listing. Dataquest's own comparison article puts the full catalog at 109 courses across career and skill paths.

**Strengths.** The highest practice density of the paid interactive platforms; guided projects interleaved through each path rather than saved for the end (19 in the analyst path); honest time math that converts path length into months at 5 versus 10 hours per week; the clearest beginner information architecture in the UI audit (beginner clarity 5 of 5 — the only 5 awarded), with path cards carrying skill tags, counts, realistic durations, and plain tags such as "Best for Beginners." Its Junior Data Analyst order — Excel → SQL → Python — independently mirrors the order Data From Zero has committed to.

**Weaknesses.** Everything past the introductory content is paywalled, the certificate is a paid feature, and the price does not appear on the public pages opened — a transparency gap a free competitor can win on by stating its model plainly. Excel and BI coverage is real but thin and locked inside three paths, with no deep standalone spreadsheet track. Outside the R and BI paths it is a Python monoculture.

### 2.4 Codecademy — structure plus career tooling

**Positioning.** Interactive lessons, quizzes, and projects organized into career paths, skill paths, and courses, wrapped in career services: a job-readiness checker that scores skills against a job posting, an AI interview simulator, and interview-preparation skill paths.

**Architecture worth noting.** The three Data Scientist paths (Machine Learning, Analytics, Inference) share one foundations trunk — data literacy → SQL (at unit 3, before deep Python) → Python fundamentals → pandas → EDA → statistics → visualization → portfolio projects — and then branch into a specialty. The Inference specialty goes furthest on statistics of any platform in this trio: hypothesis testing, regression for inference, and causal inference, plus R. Path pages publish units, lessons, projects, quizzes, and total hours before signup (the Inference path: 24 units, 78 lessons, 59 projects, 53 quizzes, about 85 hours). Portfolio projects land mid-path, not only at the end.

**Weaknesses.** Career paths run roughly 50–95 hours — breadth and speed over depth compared with Dataquest's 81–200. The entire career-relevant catalog sits behind Plus/Pro; certificates are a paid-plan feature, and a skill-path page states plainly that starting requires upgrading the plan. There is no dedicated data-engineering career path, Excel appears only as a unit inside two paths, and the headline project counts are inflated by many small exercises rather than substantial builds. The identical trunk across three "specialist" paths also means the branding promises more differentiation than the first half delivers.

### 2.5 freeCodeCamp — free, but the data track is archived

**Positioning.** A donor-funded nonprofit where everything — lessons, projects, exams, and publicly verifiable certificates — is free. Certification means completing learning blocks plus five required projects with automated tests.

**The structural fact.** The current recommended curriculum is web-development-first. The data certifications most learners know (Data Analysis with Python, Machine Learning with Python, Scientific Computing with Python) have been moved to an archive: still available and claimable, flagged on every page as no longer updated. The maintained on-ramps are a general Python certification and a Relational Databases certification.

**Coverage.** The archived data-analysis certification covers Jupyter, NumPy, pandas, cleaning, and reading data, ending in five projects; visualization is Matplotlib and Seaborn. SQL lives only inside the developer-flavored Relational Database line (Bash and terminal first, then PostgreSQL and Git, in a real dev environment) — not taught as an analyst skill in sequence with Python. ML is a single TensorFlow-centric video course with no scikit-learn-first progression and no LLM content. There is no Excel, no BI tooling, no dedicated statistics, and no data-engineering path. The legacy data certifications also lean on long passive video, unlike the interactive-challenge model of the web-dev side.

**Why it still matters.** It is the trust benchmark for any free competitor: zero price including the credential, real projects as the certificate's evidence, and anti-hype honesty (its FAQ states plainly that mastery may take several years). Its UI audit scores make the design opening precise: syllabus transparency 5 of 5 and friction 5 of 5, but visual teaching aids only 3 of 5, delivered as an unadorned text list with no durations or difficulty signals.

### 2.6 Udemy — the marketplace long tail

**Positioning.** Anyone can publish; the market sorts by ratings and reviews. Learners either buy a course once with lifetime access (list prices roughly $20–$200, with near-constant sales bringing most courses to about $10–$20) or subscribe to a curated Personal Plan (26,000+ courses, advertised from about $13 per month). Certificates of completion are not accredited.

**Depth pockets no structured platform matches.** Spreadsheet craft as a profession: the flagship Excel course (about 23 hours) runs through Microsoft 365 dynamic-array functions, Power Query, PowerPivot and the data model, and macros/VBA with project work. Database design taught as its own subject, side by side across SQL Server and PostgreSQL. A flagship ML course that implements every topic three ways (Python, R, and a cloud track) and includes reinforcement learning and association rules that structured curricula omit or thin out. All-in-one analyst bootcamps (one opened example: about 89 hours) that bolt Python, statistics, SQL, Power BI, and a capstone into a single product — and keep bolting on new tool modules, including generative AI, as the market moves.

**Weakness.** The marketplace structurally cannot answer the beginner's first question — what order? Thousands of near-duplicate courses with instructor-varying quality is the opposite of a committed sequence. Data engineering has no dominant flagship at all, only tool-specific and exam-prep courses with no sequencing guidance between them.

### 2.7 edX — the university model

**Positioning.** Universities and companies supply stacked credentials: single courses → Professional Certificates → MicroMasters → online master's degrees. Most courses audit free; payment buys graded work and the verified certificate. MicroMasters sequences can convert into credit toward a partner university's master's.

**Depth pockets.** The deepest standalone statistics and math bench in this map: MIT's Statistics and Data Science MicroMasters runs semester-length probability (15–17-week courses) with a proctored capstone exam; Stanford's Statistical Learning sits alongside it. UC San Diego's Data Science MicroMasters ($1,260 for the program in the pages opened) and Georgia Tech's Analytics MicroMasters ($2,475; the same foundational courses as its online MS in Analytics) show the graduate end of the market. HarvardX's Data Science certificate teaches the whole discipline in R — a deliberate differentiator — and uniquely treats productivity tooling (Unix, Git and GitHub, reproducible documents) as first-class curriculum. Adelaide's Big Data MicroMasters ($985.50) leads with computational thinking and algorithm design rather than tools.

**Weakness.** Excel is thin and SQL is rarely a standalone headline; both live embedded inside broader programs. This is not where the market learns analyst-grade spreadsheet or querying depth, and semester pacing with instructor-paced cohorts is the opposite of self-serve reference content. Note also that MIT's MicroMasters enrollment has moved to MIT Learn and some older programs have been discontinued — catalog churn is part of the model.

### 2.8 Pluralsight — diagnostics and certification depth

**Positioning.** A curated, subscription-only tech library of vetted expert authors, organized into sequenced paths wrapped with Skill IQ adaptive assessments, hands-on labs and sandboxes on higher tiers, and certification practice exams. There is no per-course purchase; canceling ends access. The 2026 plan structure is modular (a core library plus stackable deep libraries, with a Complete plan); exact price points are region- and promotion-sensitive and the tier system was replaced in 2026, so this map records the structure rather than a single price.

**Depth pockets.** Certification-aligned engineering content written to vendor exam blueprints (Azure data engineering sequences, Microsoft Fabric, Snowflake operations taught as scaling, monitoring, and governance rather than just queries). An enterprise BI long tail — Qlik Sense, MicroStrategy, Alteryx — alongside Tableau and Power BI. Its own career guidance sequences analyst → data science → data engineering → MLOps as one lattice with SQL named as the baseline skill for every role.

**The idea worth stealing.** Assessment-first routing: a diagnostic score places the learner at the right level of a path instead of forcing a fixed lesson 1. No fixed-track rival in this map does this. Data From Zero's checkpoint-gated linear path is a different answer to the same problem — one committed order, with gates — but the underlying insight (placement beats assumption) is shared.

### 2.9 LinkedIn Learning — the workplace library

**Positioning.** In-house-produced, practitioner-taught video courses organized into career learning paths, with certificates that attach directly to the learner's LinkedIn profile — the profile hook is the real product. Individuals reach it through LinkedIn Premium (US pricing shown at $39.99 per month or $239.88 per year in the pages opened); employers buy per-seat licenses, and many public libraries provide it free.

**Distinctive coverage.** It productized analytics engineering as a named job title early: a dedicated course built around the data lifecycle, SQL for analytics engineering, Python and pandas for the role, and dbt as the centerpiece transformation tool. Its analyst path opens with non-technical skills — soft skills sequenced inside a technical path, not offered as blog advice — and covers Copilot-assisted Excel analysis. Its ML paths include explainable AI, causal inference, and no-code ML in KNIME and SPSS Modeler: the enterprise GUI toolchain that code-first platforms exclude entirely.

**Weakness.** Path assembly seams show: the analyst path ends with a Python data-cleaning course that assumes Python the path itself never teaches. Depth is workplace-upskilling grade — aimed at professionals improving in place — rather than career-switcher mastery, and there is no single flagship Python-for-data sequence comparable to the marketplace bestsellers.

### 2.10 Kaggle Learn and the practice layer — teach, drill, simulate

The free and practice-first ecosystem splits cleanly into three jobs, and no single resource does more than one of them well:

- **Teach.** Kaggle Learn offers 17 free micro-courses (Python, Pandas, visualization, cleaning, Intro and Advanced SQL against BigQuery datasets, an ML ladder from intro through intermediate, feature engineering, explainability, deep learning, and specialties). Each course is a handful of short tutorials paired with auto-checked exercise notebooks on real data, and Intro to ML exits directly into a competition submission. SQLBolt teaches SQL basics in 18 lessons — one concept, then in-browser queries on a single small dataset, no account, no paid tier — and stops exactly where analyst interviews start: no window functions, no CTEs. The Mode SQL Tutorial (now hosted by ThoughtSpot after acquisition; any published link must use the live destination and be re-checked) teaches analyst-style SQL anchored to business questions, up through window functions and session analysis, without auto-grading or progress tracking. W3Schools is the reference layer: short pages with a runnable example beside every explanation, free to read, with depth capped at light-intermediate.
- **Drill.** HackerRank's SQL and Python domains run judged challenges from Easy to Hard, and its free certifications publish the clearest public ladder in this layer — Basic (simple queries, relationships, aggregators), Intermediate (complex joins, unions, subqueries), Advanced (optimization, modeling, indexing, window functions, pivots). LeetCode's study plans package the drill: SQL 50, Advanced SQL 50, Introduction to Pandas (15 beginner problems), and 30 Days of Pandas — distinctively, the same logic problem can be solved in SQL or in pandas.
- **Simulate.** StrataScratch is a bank of real-company interview questions (SQL, Python/pandas, R, plus statistics, case, and product banks), filterable by company and round, with the bulk behind a paid tier and a newer mode built around debugging AI-generated code. Maven Analytics is the paid, BI-heavy guided-path option in this layer: video courses, guided projects on business datasets, and a public showcase portfolio.

**Stance for Data From Zero (from the underlying research).** Link out for hosted notebooks, datasets, competitions, drill volume, free third-party certificates, and company-tagged interview banks — none of these can or should be rebuilt on a static site. Absorb the patterns: the zero-setup concept-then-query loop extended past SQLBolt's ceiling; business-question framing; the micro-lesson that ends in an artifact; the same problem solved in SQL and then again in pandas; the runnable example beside every explanation; the guided project that ends in a public, showable piece; and the published Basic/Intermediate/Advanced topic ladders as cross-checks for our own checkpoint checklists.

### 2.11 roadmap.sh — the map that teaches nothing

**Positioning.** Free, community-created visual roadmaps: 62 skill roadmaps listed on the homepage, including Data Analyst, BI Analyst, Data Engineer, Machine Learning, AI and Data Scientist, SQL, Python for Data Analysis, and Power BI. Clicking a node leads out to scattered external resources.

**Why it spreads.** The developer-roadmap repository is the distribution case study of this whole map: a single visual artifact that is screenshot-able, printable, and forwardable, with share badges shipped in the README itself. The UI audit scores its visual teaching aids 5 of 5 and its syllabus transparency 5 of 5 — for the map. The same audit states the trade-off plainly: the chart tells the learner what and in what order, but teaches nothing by itself. There are no datasets, no drills, no time estimates, no difficulty gradient, and no non-English edition of substance.

**The gap it defines.** roadmap.sh owns "map, no teaching." DataCamp owns "teaching, paywalled, English-first." The open position is the intersection: an open map whose every node opens a real, visual, bite-size lesson in a set order. That is the position §5 builds on.

### 2.12 WorldQuant University — the free accredited pole

**Positioning.** A not-for-profit, fully online university in Washington, D.C., founded in 2015 and funded by the WorldQuant Foundation. Accredited by the Distance Education Accrediting Commission (DEAC, a US national accreditor — a real distinction from regional accreditation for credit transfer and some employers), with initial accreditation in 2021. The model is completely free, not merely tuition-free: no tuition, no application fee, no examination fees, and no fee for the degree, certificate, or verified credential itself. The only possible learner cost is an external English-proficiency test paid to its provider.

**Portfolio.** Two families. Graduate Programs are for-credit, cohort-based, and run on a fixed calendar: an MSc in Financial Engineering (39 credits, nine courses plus capstone, gated by a bachelor's degree, English proficiency, and a Quantitative Proficiency Test score of 75% or higher; 20–25 hours per week over about two years), a Graduate Certificate in Applied AI Fundamentals (15 credits, five courses, no coding — AI tools and dashboards for decision-makers), and an MSc in Strategic AI and Innovation (30 credits, ten courses) that stacks directly on the certificate, gated by an 80% program average. Labs are not for credit, are fully self-paced and project-based, and carry no pathway into the degrees: Data Foundations (8 projects total, 2 units of 4 — the FAQ figure governs; see `00-README.md` §4), Data Science (8 projects), Deep Learning Fundamentals (12 projects, PyTorch throughout), and Computer Vision (6 projects). Labs run an estimated 10–16 weeks at 10–15 hours per week where an estimate is published, and each unit earns a Credly badge.

**Design worth absorbing.** Project-first units: the syllabus is the project list, each project opens with a client-style request and works a real, geographically grounded dataset (Mexico City property, Nairobi air quality, Nepal earthquake damage, Dhaka traffic). Data Foundations projects close by re-running the same workflow on a second dataset to test whether the method transfers. Lab capstones end in deployment — a packaged model, an API, or a small web app — not a notebook screenshot.

**Limits that define our opening.** Selectivity filters out true beginners before they start (degree gates, entrance tests, admissions assessments for the Labs). The pathway assumes Python and statistics foundations; there is no Excel-first or SQL-first entry. The deepest offering is financial engineering; general analyst skills (spreadsheets, BI dashboards, business SQL) are barely present, and NLP/LLM content is effectively absent — text appears as sentiment analysis inside one finance course, and sequence models are taught on sensor data rather than language. Commitments are heavy and slow relative to self-serve reference content.

### 2.13 OSSU — the curate-a-degree pole

**Positioning.** The Open Source Society University creates no teaching material. Each GitHub repository is a curated, ordered list of free courses from universities and MOOC platforms, assembled to mirror an undergraduate degree and maintained by a named maintainer plus community pull requests. Learners fork the repo and tick off completed courses in their own copy — the fork count is simultaneously a user metric and a distribution artifact — and gather in Discord and GitHub issues. The Data Science path explicitly follows the American Statistical Association's undergraduate data-science curriculum guidelines.

**Scale (date-stamped, per the honesty rules).** The Computer Science repository stands at about 211K stars, November 2026 (211,134 stars read directly on 2026-11-15; the live counter had already drifted by +2 within roughly a day of the Wave 4 check, which is why the package uses the approximation). The Data Science repository stands at about 22K stars on the same basis. Brand spillover matters: the flagship CS repo lends credibility to the much smaller DS repo (144 commits versus the CS repo's 1,112 — the DS path is visibly the less-maintained sibling).

**Shape.** About 2 years at about 20 hours per week. Prerequisites are high school mathematics plus prior statistics. The Data Science path runs eleven sections: orientation, MIT's Python sequence, a four-course data-structures-and-algorithms block taught in Java, a databases section framed as warehousing and BI rather than analyst querying, single-variable calculus, linear algebra (intuition videos, then the full MIT course), multivariable calculus, statistics and probability, tools and methods, machine learning assembled from the DeepLearning.AI specialization plus electives, and a self-directed final project with no peer review described (the CS path's final project, by contrast, is explicitly peer-evaluated).

**Strengths of the model.** Best-of-breed teaching for free, an external academic standard as a defensible quality anchor, near-zero operating cost, and a legible finite promise — "a full degree's worth, in this order" — that answers the beginner's hardest question completely, which is why the repos accumulate stars and why creators repeatedly make videos walking viewers through them.

**Weaknesses.** The seams show: learners bounce across platforms with different accounts, pacing, pedagogies, and paywalls for graded work, and nothing verifies that one course's exit level matches the next course's entry assumptions. Curated courses rot, retire, and re-platform faster than volunteer maintenance repairs them. Above all it is degree-shaped, not job-shaped: no Excel anywhere, no analyst-grade SQL block, no dashboards, no portfolio or career layer, and months of calculus and Java algorithms before any data payoff — a motivation cliff for career switchers. Except for the CS final project, nobody reviews the learner's work.

**What Data From Zero absorbs and where it differs.** Absorb: a published progression graph with prerequisites declared at fine grain, a stated external anchor for ordering decisions, capstone expectations visible from day one, a forkable tick-off progress artifact, and an "extras" overflow area that keeps the main line clean. Differ deliberately: we write original lessons in one voice over one dataset through-line instead of linking other institutions' courses; our order is job-first (Excel → SQL → Python) rather than degree-first; our unit is the lesson, not the multi-week course; and checkpoints and project review are built into the path rather than outsourced to linked courses.

## 3. Paywall and free models compared

Five distinct models appear in this map, and the differences matter more than the prices:

1. **Sampling funnel (DataCamp, Coursera, Codecademy).** A deliberately incomplete free experience — first chapter, first module, or selected courses — designed to convert. The free tier is a demo, not a plan. Codecademy's version is the least candid: a course can be free to start while its projects, assessments, and certificate are revealed as paid in a block at the bottom of the course page.
2. **Intro-free, core-paid (Dataquest).** Introductory content of every path free with no credit card, stated plainly, with honest completion math — the most candid paid model in the map — but full paths, projects, and certificates still behind a subscription whose price the public pages do not show.
3. **Marketplace and library pricing (Udemy, edX, Pluralsight, LinkedIn Learning).** Per-course purchase with perpetual discounting; free audit with a paid verified track; modular subscription libraries with no single-course purchase; and bundling into a broader professional subscription, respectively. In all four, the beginner's problem is not only price but legibility: what to buy, in what order, and what it adds up to is genuinely hard to determine from public pages.
4. **Fully free with a credential (freeCodeCamp, Kaggle Learn, WQU).** The credential itself costs nothing. The three differ in everything else: freeCodeCamp's data track is archived, Kaggle's courses are deliberately shallow on-ramps to a larger platform, and WQU's depth is real but gated by selectivity and time. None offers a maintained, beginner-first, analyst-shaped free path.
5. **Free map, external costs (roadmap.sh, OSSU).** The organizing artifact is free and open; the learning it points to carries other platforms' paywalls, seams, and rot. The learner pays in coordination effort instead of money.

The structural conclusion: **free is common; free and deep and sequenced and beginner-shaped, in one place, does not exist in this map.** Every fully free option is shallow, stale, selective, or a pointer to elsewhere. Every deep, sequenced option charges. That empty quadrant is the market position, stated in §5.

A note on price legibility as a competitive axis in itself: the UI audit found no price anywhere on the DataCamp and Dataquest homepages opened, and Codecademy's full price discoverable only in page metadata. Coursera was the exception, stating $49 per month plainly — deep in an FAQ. A site with no price can weaponize the contrast by stating "free, all of it" exactly where rivals state nothing.

## 4. Cross-cutting structural findings

These findings recur across platforms and are stated once here rather than repeated per profile.

1. **Recycling creates repetition.** DataCamp reuses the same SQL, Python, and statistics courses across its analyst, data-science, and ML tracks; Coursera reuses the same IBM courses across four certificates, and its FAQ confirms a course completed once counts everywhere. Breadth on the shelf is partly the same content re-sequenced. A single canonical lesson graph — learn once, credit everywhere across the path and tracks — is a genuine design advantage, not a slogan.
2. **One course template, reused at scale, is the incumbents' unit economics.** DataCamp's roughly 4-hour course (four chapters, video alternating with in-browser exercise) is reused across 810+ courses; tracks are pure sequencing plus projects and assessments. Beating the incumbents does not require out-producing the catalog. It requires beating the template and the sequencing in the domains learners start with: Excel, SQL, and Python.
3. **Projects are the differentiator incumbents lean on — and the consensus finish line.** Flagship career tracks carry 2–9 projects on realistic datasets; freeCodeCamp requires five per certification; Dataquest interleaves up to 27 guided projects per path; WQU Labs are nothing but projects. Independently, hiring-side research in this package converges on the same standard: a portfolio of 3–5 finished projects beats a stack of certificates (with Data Engineering the deliberate exception at 2–3 deep projects). Any competing path needs equal or better project cadence, visible without a paywall.
4. **Statistics is missing where beginners actually are.** Three independent routes reach the same conclusion: the most-taken beginner analyst certificate has no statistics course; no beginner or associate certification in the certification map tests inferential statistics seriously; and OSSU — the outlier that treats statistics as a full discipline — buries it behind a calculus spine. Placement, not existence, is the failure. Data From Zero's advanced core puts statistics on the main line, after Python and before the tracks, where every track inherits it.
5. **SQL depth is nobody's headline inside analyst credentials — despite being the most-tested analyst skill.** Window functions, CTEs, and optimization appear in dedicated SQL products (DataCamp's ladder, UC Davis, the drill layer) but not as a deep, dedicated block inside the flagship analyst certificates. This is the direct justification for treating SQL as one complete block in the path rather than as survey slices spread across tools.
6. **Sequencing transparency is a feature learners can see.** The platforms that publish counts, order, and honest time math up front (Dataquest, Codecademy, freeCodeCamp, roadmap.sh) earn the audit's highest transparency scores; the platforms that lead with marketing and bury the syllabus (Coursera's certificate pages run about 90% persuasion before inspectable curriculum) earn the lowest. Transparency is cheap for a free site and expensive for a funnel, which is why it differentiates.
7. **Demand, stated honestly.** Across the named job-posting samples opened in this research, SQL and Excel are #1 or #2 in every sample, with the order depending on the sample: Excel first at 81% ahead of SQL at 60% in the AccioJob sample of 328 postings (a mixed analyst, business-analyst, engineer, and scientist sample); SQL at about 73% with Excel at 54% in the 365 Data Science analysis as reported by the Traecta transition guide, whose own page also reports a newer 365 Data Science outlook with lower shares — the figures are sample- and year-specific, not constants. Python sits third for analyst roles at about 40–50% in the named samples, and first for data-scientist roles. BI tools sit below the top three. AI is the loudest marketing topic on every incumbent homepage and a real hiring signal, but for the entry-level audience this site serves, SQL and Excel remain the largest hiring topics. The path order is validated twice over: by this demand pattern and by learning sequence (spreadsheet fluency is the fastest first win, SQL is the first real wall, Python is the multiplier).
8. **The AI-era layer is real but unevenly placed.** Dataquest's AI Engineer path and DataCamp's LangChain line are the most current builder curricula; IBM AI Engineering is the only flagship reaching fine-tuning and RAG; Google Cloud's data-workflow specialization teaches generative tasks inside BigQuery SQL (its FAQ recommends a baseline of SQL, BigQuery, and basic ML despite a "no experience" header, and its applied project includes multimodal retrieval). Anthropic's official interactive prompt-engineering tutorial — 9 chapters plus an appendix, confirmed from its GitHub README in Wave 4 — shows the vendor-direct end of the market. The pattern for Data From Zero, developed in the AI-era research, is to thread AI skills (assisted querying and coding, verification of AI output, RAG and agent basics) through the linear path rather than bolting on a separate AI track that repeats the foundations.

## 5. Gaps Data From Zero exploits

In priority order. Each gap names the structural reason incumbents cannot close it quickly.

### Gap 1 — The free + deep quadrant is empty

freeCodeCamp is free but its data track is archived and missing Excel, BI, statistics depth, and data engineering. Kaggle Learn is free but deliberately shallow. WQU is free and deep but selective, finance-tilted, and slow. roadmap.sh and OSSU are free maps to other people's paywalls. Dataquest is deep and well sequenced but paywalled; Codecademy is well structured but paid and light. A free roadmap with Dataquest-grade sequencing, Codecademy-grade structure, and full depth in Excel, SQL, Python, and statistics occupies a quadrant no incumbent currently holds. Incumbents cannot follow without cannibalizing the subscription the funnel exists to sell; the one nonprofit that could (freeCodeCamp) would have to un-archive and rebuild a data track it has deprioritized in favor of web development.

### Gap 2 — Visual teaching is the unclaimed differentiator

The UI audit's visual-teaching scores: roadmap.sh 5 of 5 for its map only (a navigation chart that teaches nothing); DataCamp, Dataquest, Codecademy, and Kaggle Learn at 4; freeCodeCamp at 3; Coursera at 2. No platform leads with concept-level visuals — a canonical diagram or interactive for joins, window functions, distributions, regression, or ML intuition — inside the lesson itself. Data From Zero's premise is exactly that: the map from roadmap.sh, the openness from freeCodeCamp, the card metadata from Dataquest, and a real visual lesson behind every node. The build catalog, costs, and priority order for those visuals are in the teaching playbook and UI lessons files.

### Gap 3 — Vietnamese scaffolding is a moat, not a translation feature

Wave 4 verified the incumbents' current position against their own official pages. DataCamp now offers Vietnamese through AI-plus-human-review translation, explicitly in beta, covering courses, assessments, practices, projects, and interface — with partner courses English-only and a lower AI-tutor-only tier whose wider platform stays in English. Coursera's auto-translation covers 4,000+ courses in 40 additional languages including Vietnamese, but its own support page lists what is **not** translated: downloadable files, externally hosted assignments, peer-graded submissions and feedback, hands-on labs and guided projects, and other learners' discussion posts. Dataquest and Codecademy are English-only. In other words, the translated layer stops exactly where the assessed, hands-on learning happens.

The moat is therefore not translation — the incumbents are closing that gap mechanically — but scaffolding built for Vietnamese learners from the start: a terminology rule and glossary that keep job vocabulary in English while explaining in Vietnamese; localized Vietnam-context examples, datasets, and salary framing (entry-level analyst pay in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi listed at 10–16 million VND per month in the Vietnamese career guide opened in this research, against US-dollar subscriptions priced for US and EU professionals); and format guidance for the traps that actually bite — Excel function names are not localized (learners type SUM and VLOOKUP in English on a Vietnamese install), while the list-separator and CSV locale behavior depends on the learner's own regional settings and must be taught as a diagnostic check. Retooling thousands of interactive exercises into native scaffolding is a rebuild, not a toggle, and Vietnam alone is too small a market to justify it at incumbent price points — while being exactly the right size to be a home market. All `/vi/` rules in this package come from the Vietnamese-learners research, which owns them canonically.

### Gap 4 — One committed order, with gates, against the catalog

The most repeated beginner question in the community evidence is not "what is SQL" but "what do I learn first, and in what order?" — and the most named drop-off pattern is tutorial hell: weeks jumping between courses with no order and no checkpoints. A marketplace structurally cannot answer the order question, because its business is the catalog; a track-selling platform cannot tell learners to ignore 99% of its catalog. Data From Zero's answer is one line — Excel → SQL (one complete block) → Python → advanced core → one of five tracks — with week-sized stages and a "you may proceed when you can do X" gate at each stage. The demand evidence (§4.7) validates the specific order; the learning-sequence argument (build on the spreadsheet fluency business-background switchers already have, instead of restarting them at HTML or generic code) validates it independently.

### Gap 5 — Job tasks and a portfolio as the finish line, localized

Incumbents sell certificates, so they cannot demote certificates below portfolios without devaluing their own product — yet the hiring-side evidence says portfolios are what employers screen for, and their examples, datasets, and salary data are US-market. Data From Zero ends every stage in workplace artifacts (a monthly sales report, a churn query, a dashboard a manager could read) and ends the path in 3–5 portfolio projects, with the Data Engineering exception of 2–3 deep projects, framed against the Vietnamese job market. External proof points are named and linked (free HackerRank certificates, LeetCode's SQL 50 and pandas plans, Kaggle competitions) rather than imitated.

### Gap 6 — An open, forkable roadmap the community can improve

The two open artifacts in this map — roadmap.sh's map and OSSU's fork-and-tick-off curriculum — are also its two proven distribution engines: a free, ordered, GitHub-native artifact is content other creators make videos about, and every learner's fork is a public progress tracker and an advertisement at once. Incumbent content lives inside closed platforms optimized for enrollment, not remixing; opening it would break their licensing and funnel metrics. Data From Zero publishes under CC BY 4.0, so teachers and creators may legally re-share and re-teach the material, and layers a named public challenge pattern (daily public progress, in the #100DaysOfCode mold) on top so learners distribute the path themselves. The full positioning moves and shareability mechanics are in `05-career-and-viral.md`.
