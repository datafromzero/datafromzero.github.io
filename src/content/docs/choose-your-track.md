After the advanced core you choose one of four tracks: Data Analyst, Data Engineer, Data Scientist or ML / AI Engineer.
A track is not more of the same. It works like a master's degree: **less coding practice, more knowledge and judgment**, and
the specialist skills of one career, which the core only touches. How much mathematics it takes depends on the track: the
Data Scientist and ML / AI Engineer tracks are math-heavy, the Data Analyst track needs friendly statistics, and the Data
Engineer track is about software and systems more than math. The table further down shows the detail.

Think carefully before you choose. The track decides what you study next, and the four careers lean on very different skills.

Each track page ends its lesson list with **What you finish with**: the hours from the first Excel lesson to the capstone, the
project you hand in, and what employers screen for. Want to build the tables a dashboard reads, with dbt? Analytics engineer is
not a fifth track, but there is an [analytics engineer route](/data-analyst/#analytics-engineer-route) from the Data Analyst
track into the dbt lessons of the Data Engineer track, and it says what it skips.

Not sure yet? [Take the career quiz](/career-quiz/) again. It asks ten questions about how you like to work and suggests a
direction. Then come back and compare it with the charts below.

The scores are a guide, **not a measurement.** They come from real job postings, the U.S. O*NET occupation database and two
official skills frameworks, and every source is linked at the bottom of the page. Real jobs differ from one company to the
next, and any role can lean toward another's skills. Knowledge of an industry (retail, health, finance) matters in every
career, but it depends on who hires you rather than on the track, so it is not on the chart. Use the charts to ask a
question: which of these skills do I want to be the strongest part of my working day?
