This lesson uses the same tables as *From a spreadsheet to SQL* (S1): the five tables of **cafe-sales**, the invented
three-cafe chain we use from Excel to Python. Each query runs in the editor on this page.

## The question

The owner of the cafes asks:

> How many orders did each cafe take, and how did customers pay at each one? Which cafes took at least 500 completed
> orders?

One `COUNT(*)` gives one number for the whole table. The owner wants one for each cafe.

Each order is one **row** of the `orders` table. Its store and payment method are **columns**. Four rows (the stores
are 1 Old Town, 2 Campus and 3 Harbor):

| order_id | store_id | status | payment_method |
|---:|---:|---|---|
| 1105 | 2 | completed | cash |
| 1106 | 1 | completed | card |
| 1107 | 1 | completed | mobile |
| 1108 | 1 | completed | card |

## The big idea

`GROUP BY` sorts your rows into **groups**, like buckets: one group for each different value of a column you pick.
Then it turns every group into a single row.

## Learn by doing

Five small steps. Each gives you one idea, then you try it in the editor.

### 1. One group per store

The column you pick is the **key**. `GROUP BY store_id` makes one group for each store, and every group becomes one row.
Say what that row stands for before you write the query: *one row per store*. That is the **grain**.

**Try it:**

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
GROUP BY store_id
ORDER BY store_id;`} />

Read it: *count the orders in each store's group*. `COUNT(*)` is an **aggregate**: it turns a group into one number.

Expected: 3 rows, 2 columns: store 1 has 729 orders, store 2 has 716 and store 3 has 355. *Excel:* a PivotTable (E15)
is the same machine: `store` in Rows is the key, and a count in Values is the aggregate.

*Why:* three stores, three groups, three rows. The counts add up to 1,800, every order, the canceled one included.

### 2. Two columns, one group per pair

Pick two columns and a group is one **pair**: store 1 with card is one group, store 1 with cash is another. To show a
column beside the count, it must be in `GROUP BY`. Any other column has many values in a group, so DuckDB stops with an
error saying the column "must appear in the GROUP BY clause".

**Try it.** Grain: *one row per store and payment method*. Before you run it: how many rows?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store_id, payment_method, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
GROUP BY store_id, payment_method
ORDER BY store_id, payment_method;`} />

Expected: 3 columns. *Excel:* `payment_method` in Columns, read as a list.

*Why:* 3 stores times 3 methods is 9, because every store has orders in all three. A pair with no orders gets no group,
so no row. Store 1 reads card 421, cash 91, mobile 217.

### 3. `WHERE` clears rows before the groups

`WHERE` runs before any group exists, so it can test only a row's own columns, such as `status`.

**Try it.** Grain: *one row per store, completed orders only*.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY store_id
ORDER BY store_id;`} />

Expected: 3 rows, 2 columns: 729, 715 and 355. *Excel:* `status` in Filters, set to completed.

*Why:* the canceled order 1461 never reaches a group. Campus, store 2, goes from 716 to 715.

### 4. `HAVING` clears groups after the count

`HAVING` runs after the groups are built, so it can test what a group holds, such as its size. A group that fails is
dropped whole.

**Try it:**

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY store_id
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 500
ORDER BY store_id;`} />

Expected: 2 rows, 2 columns: store 1 with 729 and store 2 with 715. *Why:* Harbor's group has 355 orders, so its row
is gone. That answers the owner: Old Town and Campus.

`WHERE` sees one order at a time and has no idea how many orders a store has. `HAVING` runs after the grouping, so it
sees the finished count per store and can drop a whole store at once.

### 5. Check that the groups add up

After any `GROUP BY`, add the groups back together. They should give the whole.

**Try it:**

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS completed_orders FROM orders WHERE status = 'completed';`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column: `1799`. *Why:* it equals 729 + 715 + 355 from unit 3, and the 1,799 completed orders the
till system reported in *A cleaning workflow you can defend* (E13). The groups lost no rows and counted none twice.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A key picks the groups, and every group becomes one row. Say the grain first.
- Show only the key and aggregates.
- `WHERE` filters rows before the groups; `HAVING` filters groups after the count.
- The order: `FROM`, `WHERE`, `GROUP BY`, `HAVING`, `SELECT`, `ORDER BY`.

**Common trap: "`HAVING` is just a second `WHERE`."** They run at different moments. `WHERE MAX(order_id) > 1500` fails,
because no maximum exists yet. `HAVING status = 'completed'` fails too, because a group holds many statuses, not one.
Ask what you are judging: a row or a group.

</Aside>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT customer_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
GROUP BY customer_id;
```

has 1 order. Now filter the groups:

```sql
SELECT customer_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
GROUP BY customer_id
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 20
ORDER BY orders DESC, customer_id;
```

34 customers placed at least 20 orders. Customers 54, 61 and 81 lead with 29 orders each, so the tie-breaker decides
their order.

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague wants the stores with more than 500 orders and writes this. It gives an error, not a table:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE COUNT(*) > 500
GROUP BY store_id;`} />

<Solution>

The error reads `WHERE clause cannot contain aggregates!`. `WHERE` runs right after
`FROM`, before any group exists, so there is no count to compare. A condition on a count belongs to `HAVING`:

```sql
SELECT store_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
GROUP BY store_id
HAVING COUNT(*) > 500;
```

Expected: 2 rows, 2 columns: store 1 with 729 orders and store 2 with 716 (all orders, canceled included). Harbor, with
355, is dropped.

</Solution>
