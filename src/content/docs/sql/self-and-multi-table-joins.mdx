Real questions need more than two tables, and sometimes the same table twice. Every query on this page runs in the editor
under it: press Run.

## The question

The owner of the cafes asks:

> Excel said Campus sold 112 lines of coffee in March, worth \$706.25, completed orders only. Can SQL rebuild that from
> the five tables? And some customers were referred by other customers: who referred customer 144?

The Campus coffee of *Conditional totals with SUMIFS and COUNTIFS* (E7) needs four tables. The referrals need one table
twice: customers 6 and 144 point at the customers 3 and 86 through `referred_by`, and 86 points at nobody:

| customer_id | loyalty_tier | referred_by |
|---:|---|---:|
| 3 | gold | NULL |
| 6 | bronze | 3 |
| 86 | bronze | NULL |
| 144 | bronze | 86 |

## Warm-up

Answer from memory before you open anything. Then click to check.

1. In *Join fan-out: aggregate before you join* (S22), what do you do before a join that would copy a per-order figure
   onto many lines?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Aggregate the repeating side first, with `GROUP BY` on the join key in a CTE, so that both sides have one row per key.

   </details>

2. In E7, four conditions picked the lines: Campus, coffee, completed and March. Which table of the database holds each
   one?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The store name is in `stores`, reached through `orders.store_id`. The category is in `products`. The status and the
   time, `status` and `ordered_at`, are in `orders`. The lines themselves are in `order_items`: four tables.

   </details>

## The big idea

## Learn by doing

Five small steps. Press Run on each query.

### 1. Chain joins, and ask what one row is

A **join chain** writes one `JOIN` after another, and each link works as in *INNER JOIN: pairing rows on a key* (S20): it
pairs the rows built so far with the next table wherever its `ON` is true. The `ON` may name any table already in the
chain, not only the one just before.

After each join, ask two questions: *is the key unique in the table I just added?* and *what is one row now?* The
counting rule of S20 and the fan-out of S22 answer them: the grain stays the same, or becomes finer.

**Try it**. Before you run it: start from `order_items` and add `orders`, `products` and `stores`, each
on a key that is unique on the added side. How many rows will you get?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS joined_rows
FROM order_items AS oi
INNER JOIN orders AS o ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
INNER JOIN products AS p ON oi.product_id = p.product_id
INNER JOIN stores AS s ON o.store_id = s.store_id;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column. *Why:* **3287**, the lines of `order_items`. `order_id` is unique in `orders`, `product_id` in
`products` and `store_id` in `stores`, so no join adds a line, and each line has a partner, so none drops. One row is
still one line, now with its store, status, category and price. Each box of the picture answers the two questions for one
link.

### 2. Rebuild E7: conditions run once, at the end

`WHERE`, `GROUP BY` and the rest run once, on the final wide table. So pile up the joins first, then state every
condition together.

*Excel:* in E7 the four conditions of the Campus coffee were four columns of one sheet, beside each line. A join chain
builds such a sheet: each `JOIN` brings the next table's columns next to every line.

**Try it**: Campus, coffee, completed orders, March.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS lines,
       SUM(oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd) AS sales_usd
FROM order_items AS oi
INNER JOIN orders AS o ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
INNER JOIN products AS p ON oi.product_id = p.product_id
INNER JOIN stores AS s ON o.store_id = s.store_id
WHERE s.store_name = 'Campus'
  AND p.category = 'coffee'
  AND o.status = 'completed'
  AND o.ordered_at >= DATE '2026-03-01'
  AND o.ordered_at < DATE '2026-04-01';`} />

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns: `112 | 706.25`. *Why:* the
rows are completed orders only. Canceled order 1461 is a Campus order from March 1 with a 5.00 coffee line: leave out the
status condition and you get 113 lines and 711.25. The extra-zero line of order 2075 is an Old Town order from May, so it
is not in this slice.

### 3. One table, two roles

A **self-join** is an ordinary join with one table named twice, once per role, each with its own alias. Here `c` is the
customer and `r` is the customer who referred them. Without aliases DuckDB cannot tell the two uses apart and stops with
an error.

Link the roles through the key: the foreign key of one against the primary key of the other (S18), so
`c.referred_by = r.customer_id`.

**Try it**: first, how many customers name a referrer?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(referred_by) AS referred_customers FROM customers;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column: `30`. *Why:* `COUNT(column)` skips empty values (S7). The other 120 of the 150 customers
have an empty `referred_by`.

**Try it**. Before you run it: each referred customer has one referrer, because `customer_id` is
unique. How many pairs will the join return?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS pairs
FROM customers AS c
INNER JOIN customers AS r ON c.referred_by = r.customer_id;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column. *Why:* **30**, one pair for each customer counted above. The 120 customers whose
`referred_by` is empty pair with nobody, so the inner join leaves them out.

### 4. Read the pairs

After a self-join each column name exists twice, so qualify every column with its alias (S20). The rest works as before,
join types included: a `LEFT` self-join keeps the unpaired rows, as in S21.

**Try it**: look at the pairs.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT c.customer_id AS customer,
       c.loyalty_tier AS customer_tier,
       r.customer_id AS referrer,
       r.loyalty_tier AS referrer_tier
FROM customers AS c
INNER JOIN customers AS r ON c.referred_by = r.customer_id
WHERE c.customer_id IN (6, 30, 34, 144)
ORDER BY c.customer_id;`} />

Expected: 4 rows, 4 columns. *Why:* customer 144 was referred by customer 86, as in the table of the question. That
answers the owner. Customer 24 is the referrer of both 30 and 34: one to many again, now inside a single table. The
picture shows the same rows.

### 5. Check by hand

Pick one customer whose lines you can add up yourself, and compare. Customer 144 has few orders. Start the chain at
`customers` this time.

**Try it**: the lines of customer 144.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT o.order_id, p.product_name, oi.quantity,
       oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd AS line_amount_usd
FROM customers AS c
INNER JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
INNER JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
INNER JOIN products AS p ON oi.product_id = p.product_id
WHERE c.customer_id = 144
ORDER BY oi.order_item_id;`} />

Expected: 4 rows, 4 columns. Add the four amounts by hand before you go on. *Why:* the grain is a line again: the first
join made it one row per order, the second made it one row per line. Now total the same chain in SQL:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT SUM(oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd) AS spend_usd
FROM customers AS c
INNER JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
INNER JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
INNER JOIN products AS p ON oi.product_id = p.product_id
WHERE c.customer_id = 144;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column: `17.25`. By hand: 4.50 + 4.25 + 5.00 + 3.50 = 17.25. Two orders, both completed.

The chain returned E7's 112 lines and 706.25, and customer 144's 17.25 matches the hand total. The 30 pairs of unit 3 equal
the 30 customers who name a referrer, so no pair was lost or doubled.

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why does the chain keep all 3,287 lines, while the self-join returns only 30 of 150 customers?</summary>

The 30 comes from `referred_by`: 120 customers hold `NULL` there, and a `NULL` finds no partner, so only the 30 who hold
an id get a pair. A `LEFT JOIN` (S21) would keep all 150, with `NULL` for the referrer.

</details>

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Each `JOIN` pairs the rows built so far with the next table. After each, ask: was the key unique, and what is one row now?
- `WHERE`, `GROUP BY` and the rest run once, on the final wide table.
- A self-join names the table twice with two aliases, linked foreign key to primary key.
- Qualify every column with its alias.

**Common trap: "a self-join needs a special join, or a copy of the table."** It is an ordinary join. A copied table is not
needed: the 30 pairs of unit 3 come from one `customers` table, named twice, with two aliases keeping the roles apart.

</Aside>

**What were the completed sales of each store?** Expected: 3 rows, 2 columns (the store name
and its sales), sorted by store name. The three amounts should add up to E21's Sales, 22,749.25.

Done for you: the grain is a line, and the first join is in place. This starter shows the units sold for each store

<RunSQL query={`SELECT o.store_id,

Now finish it: add the joins to `products` and `stores`, keep only the completed orders, and show the store name and the
sum of the line amounts in place of the store number and the units.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two joins are the ones from unit 1, and the `WHERE` is the status test from unit 2. `GROUP BY` as in *GROUP BY and
HAVING* (S8).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

```sql
...
INNER JOIN products AS p ON ... = ...
INNER JOIN stores AS s ON ... = ...
WHERE ...
GROUP BY ...
ORDER BY s.store_name;
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT s.store_name,
       SUM(oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd) AS sales_usd
FROM order_items AS oi
INNER JOIN orders AS o ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
INNER JOIN products AS p ON oi.product_id = p.product_id
INNER JOIN stores AS s ON o.store_id = s.store_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY s.store_name
ORDER BY s.store_name;
```

The result is Campus 8,998.75, Harbor 4,606.25 and Old Town 9,144.25. They add up to **22,749.25**, E21's Sales: the
completed orders only, with the extra-zero line of order 2075 (an Old Town order) still inside. With the canceled order 1461
kept, Campus would read 9,003.75.

<Exercise id="sql-self-and-multi-table-joins-3">

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague wants each referred customer next to the tier of the customer who referred them, and
runs this:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT c.customer_id AS customer,
       r.customer_id AS referrer,
       r.loyalty_tier AS referrer_tier
FROM customers AS c
INNER JOIN customers AS r ON c.customer_id = r.referred_by
ORDER BY c.customer_id;`} />

Expected from that query: 30 rows, 3 columns, as in unit 3. Its row for customer 3 reads `3 | 6 | bronze`. The picture
showed that customer 6 was referred by customer 3, who is gold. What went wrong, and what is the right query?

<Solution>

`c.customer_id = r.referred_by` says that `r` was referred by `c`, the opposite of the plan. The column called
`customer` holds the referrer, and `referrer_tier` holds the tier of the referred customer. The row count is 30 either
way, so the count cannot catch it; a pair you know can. Link the foreign key of `c`, the customer, to the key of `r`:

```sql
SELECT c.customer_id AS customer,
       r.customer_id AS referrer,
       r.loyalty_tier AS referrer_tier
FROM customers AS c
INNER JOIN customers AS r ON c.referred_by = r.customer_id
ORDER BY c.customer_id;
```

Expected: 30 rows, 3 columns, and the row for customer 6 reads `6 | 3 | gold`.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
