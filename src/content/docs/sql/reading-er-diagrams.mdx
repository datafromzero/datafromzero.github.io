## The question

A new teammate sends you a diagram of the cafe database and asks, before writing any SQL:

> If I join `orders` to `customers`, will I lose any orders? And if I join `orders` to `order_items`, how many rows will I have?

You can answer both from the diagram, then check. A join pairs the rows of two tables by a key: an INNER JOIN keeps only the pairs that match, and a LEFT JOIN also keeps the rows of its left table that have no match.

## Warm-up

From memory first, then click to check.

1. In *Tables, keys and grain* (S18), which column of `order_items` is its primary key, which are foreign keys, and what is one row?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `order_item_id` is the primary key. `order_id` and `product_id` are foreign keys. One row is one product line inside one order.

   </details>

2. Write the query that shows `store_id` is not a key of `orders`.

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `SELECT COUNT(*), COUNT(DISTINCT store_id) FROM orders;`. A key has two equal counts; here the second is far smaller, because a store repeats on many orders.

   </details>

### 1. Read one line twice

**Idea.** A line joins a primary key (PK) to the foreign key (FK) that names it, and carries a verb phrase such as *places*. Read it from each table in turn, with the verb: "each customer places zero or many orders; each order is placed by zero or one customer." The mark nearest a table says how many of that table's rows go with one row at the other end. Two bars mean exactly one, a bar and a circle zero or one, a bar and a three-pronged foot one or many, and a circle and a foot zero or many.

**Predict:** the diagram says each order has exactly one store. How many orders will have an empty `store_id`, and how many different stores will the column use?

**Try it:**

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS orders,
       COUNT(store_id) AS with_store,
       COUNT(DISTINCT store_id) AS stores_used
FROM orders;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 3 columns. *Why:* the result is `1800 | 1800 | 3`. "Exactly one" means never empty, and no row breaks it. All three stores have orders, so the diagram shows one or many. That is what the rows hold today: a store opened tomorrow would have none, and only a constraint makes a mark a rule.

### 2. A circle means "may be empty"

**Idea.** A circle beside a bar sets the minimum to zero: the foreign key may be empty, or a parent may have no children. A bar alone sets the minimum to one.

On the `orders` and `customers` line the diagram shows zero or one at the customers end and zero or many at the orders end: each customer places zero or many orders, and each order is placed by zero or one customer.

**Predict:** how many orders have an empty `customer_id`? And do all 150 customers appear in `orders`?

**Try it:** the orders with no customer.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT * FROM orders WHERE customer_id IS NULL;`} />

Expected: 6 columns. *Why:* the empty `customer_id` is the walk-in order 1335 of *Bring in a value by key with XLOOKUP* (E8), a visit without a loyalty card. That is the circle at the customers end.

**Try it:** the other end.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS customers_who_ordered FROM orders;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column. *Why:* compare it with the 150 rows of `customers`: the difference is the circle at the orders end, customers with no order at all.

### 3. Many on both sides needs a middle table

**Idea.** One order holds several products, and one product is on many orders. A single column cannot hold that, so a table in the middle, a **link table**, holds one row for each pair. Its foreign keys point to both parents. Read each line of the link table separately: the diagram shows `order_items` with one order and one product per row.

The kinds of link, from their marks:

| Marks at the two ends | Kind | Where the foreign key sits |
|---|---|---|
| single and single | one-to-one | the child's foreign key is also its primary key or unique; cafe-sales has none |
| single and many | one-to-many | on the many side: `stores` to `orders` |
| many on both sides, through a table | many-to-many | in the link table: `orders`, `order_items`, `products` |
| a table with itself | self-reference | in the same table: `customers.referred_by` |

**Predict:** the diagram says each order contains one or many lines. What are the fewest and the most lines one order has?

**Try it:**

<RunSQL query={`WITH per_order AS (
  SELECT order_id, COUNT(*) AS lines
  FROM order_items
  GROUP BY order_id
)
SELECT MIN(lines) AS fewest, MAX(lines) AS most, COUNT(*) AS orders_with_lines
FROM per_order;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 3 columns. *Why:* the minimum of one holds (every order has a line), and orders with lines add up to all 1,800. The maximum is the largest count today. The `*` in the diagram sets no upper limit, so it is a design rule, not a measurement.

*Excel:* a link table is the long list you get when you give each order and product pair its own row, the tidy layout of *Rows, columns and tidy layout* (E1).

### 4. A table can point at itself

**Idea.** A self-reference holds a key of the same table. On the diagram it is a line that leaves a box and comes back to it. `customers.referred_by` holds the `customer_id` of the customer who made the referral. Read it the same way: each customer has zero or one referrer, and each customer refers zero or many others.

**Predict:** how many customers have no referrer?

**Try it:**

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS without_referrer FROM customers WHERE referred_by IS NULL;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column. *Why:* that is the circle at the referrer end (each customer is referred by zero or one customer), a row with no parent. The other customers each name one parent, a customer of the same table.

### 5. Plan a join before you write it

**Idea.** Before a join, read the line three times. Which two keys match? What is one row afterwards? And does an optional end matter? An INNER JOIN keeps only pairs that match, so a row whose key is empty or has no partner disappears. A LEFT JOIN keeps every row of the table on its left. The DuckDB documentation: "A join that is not OUTER is INNER (only rows that get paired are returned)."

The join syntax comes in the next lessons (*INNER JOIN: pairing rows on a key*, S19, and *LEFT JOIN and finding rows with no match*, S20). Here you only run the four queries and read their row counts.

**Predict** from what the first four units showed. Write four numbers before you run anything: (a) `orders` INNER JOIN `customers`; (b) `orders` LEFT JOIN `customers`; (c) `customers` LEFT JOIN `orders`; (d) `orders` INNER JOIN `order_items`.

**Try it:**

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows_a
FROM orders AS o
INNER JOIN customers AS c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id;`} />

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows_b
FROM orders AS o
LEFT JOIN customers AS c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id;`} />

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows_c
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id;`} />

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows_d
FROM orders AS o
INNER JOIN order_items AS i ON i.order_id = o.order_id;`} />

Expected: each 1 row, 1 column. *Why:* (a) loses the walk-in order, so 1,799. (b) keeps all 1,800: its missing customer comes back empty. (c) starts from 150 customers and keeps the 5 who never ordered once each, next to the 1,799 orders that have a customer: 1,804. (d) gives one row per child, which is one row per line, 3,287, because the child end is many. *Excel:* an `XLOOKUP` that finds no key returns `#N/A` (E8); an INNER JOIN drops that row, a LEFT JOIN keeps it with an empty cell.

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: what does each join do to a row whose foreign key is empty?</summary>

An INNER JOIN drops it, because nothing pairs with an empty key. A LEFT JOIN keeps it, with empty columns from the other table, when the row is on the left.

</details>

<Aside type="note" title="Other diagrams: recognition only">

You will meet two other notations, and each is separate: never put both on one diagram. **Chen notation** draws entities as boxes, relationships as diamonds and attributes as ovals. A **UML class diagram** is a software-design drawing; it writes multiplicities as numbers at the line ends and an association name in the middle of the line. The UML specification gives the form `lower..upper`, where `*` is an unlimited upper bound and a lone `*` stands for `0..*`.

| In UML | What it looks like | In tables |
|---|---|---|
| Multiplicity `1`, `0..1`, `1..*`, `*` | numbers at the ends of a line | the four counts that crow's foot draws as two bars, bar and circle, bar and foot, circle and foot |
| Association | a plain line | a foreign key (one-to-many) or a link table (many-to-many) |
| Aggregation | a hollow diamond at the whole's end; "shared" parts | an ordinary foreign key; the specification leaves the exact meaning to the application area |
| Composition | a filled diamond | a foreign key on the part's table, usually required; the specification says parts go when the whole is deleted, which you must write as a rule |
| Inheritance (generalization) | a hollow triangle pointing at the general class | one table with a type column, or one table for each subtype: see *Designing tables: a workflow* |

You will not draw a UML diagram here.

</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Read each line twice, with its verb: "each customer places zero or many orders; each order is placed by zero or one customer".
- A bar means one, a circle means zero, a foot means many. The mark nearest a table counts that table's rows.
- Many-to-many always has a link table in the middle, with a foreign key to each parent.
- Before a join: which keys match, what one row is afterwards, and whether an optional end makes INNER and LEFT disagree.

**Common trap: "the symbols are rules the database enforces."** Here they come from the rows and the design. All three stores have orders today, so the line says one or many, yet a store opened tomorrow has none; only a constraint (S31) makes the database refuse a row that breaks a mark. Check a mark with a count, and do not read a count as a rule.

</Aside>

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague reads the loop on `customers` and says "each customer refers zero or many others, so counting `referred_by` counts the customers who referred someone". The query gives 30 and the report says "30 referrers".

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(referred_by) AS referrers FROM customers;`} />

Expected from that query: 1 row, 1 column: `30`. What does the 30 count, and what is the right query?

<Solution>

`COUNT(referred_by)` counts the rows whose `referred_by` is filled: customers who *have* a referrer, which is the zero-or-one end of the loop. The question is about the other end, customers who referred someone, and a customer who referred two others is counted twice. Count the different values:

```sql
SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT referred_by) AS referrers FROM customers;
```

Expected: 1 row, 1 column: `29`. Customer 24 referred two customers, 30 and 34, so 30 customers have a referrer and 29 customers referred someone. Count the end you were asked about.

</Solution>
