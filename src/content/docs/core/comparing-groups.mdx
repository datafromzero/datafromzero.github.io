## The question

The product manager of a budgeting app asks:

> The new paywall, `paywall_v2`, ran for eight weeks in `app-events`, a fictional app. Did it lift purchases, and by how much? And iOS people seem more active than Android people: is that real, and does it matter?

| comparison | group A | group B |
|---|---|---|
| bought Plus within 14 days of signing up | control: 343 of 2,706 (12.7%) | variant: 415 of 2,713 (15.3%) |
| `session_start` and `add_expense` events in the first 14 days | Android: 6,508 people, mean 2.86 | iOS: 7,196 people, mean 3.30 |

By the end you can pick the test for each, run it, report the gap and an interval beside the p-value, say which gap matters, and write the verdict in two sentences.

## The big idea

A p-value says whether a gap is real; the effect size says how big it is, and an interval says how sure you are: report all three.

## Learn by doing

About 35 minutes, 4 new ideas: the test of two proportions, the test of two means, the size of a gap next to its p-value, and the verdict for a manager. The setup cell gave `assign` and `full`, the 20,177 people who signed up by June 16 and so have 14 days of follow-up, with their `platform` and `activity`: the `session_start` and `add_expense` events in those 14 days, every recorded event, retried duplicates included.

<OpenInColab />

Which test? It depends on what you compare:

| what you compare | test | SciPy |
|---|---|---|
| two shares (buyers, clicks) | z test of two proportions, or the chi-square test of a table of counts | `stats.chi2_contingency(table, correction=False)` |
| two means (events, dollars) | Welch's t-test | `stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)` |
| two skewed samples, when a mean is the wrong summary | Mann-Whitney U test | `stats.mannwhitneyu(a, b)` |

Three or more groups need ANOVA (means) or chi-square (counts), not covered here.

### 1. Two proportions

**Idea.** For two shares, H0 says both groups share one rate. If it is true, the best estimate pools both groups, and the gap between the two sample rates wobbles by a standard error with a formula. Then z and the p-value go as in C8. The other common choice is the chi-square test of a table of counts; the SciPy documentation says `correction=False` turns off the continuity correction it applies to 2 by 2 tables. The interval of the difference uses each group's own rate.

**Try it.** Test `paywall_v2` by hand, as a table of counts, and with an interval for the difference:

```python
pw = assign[assign["experiment"] == "paywall_v2"]
ctrl = pw.loc[pw["variant"] == "control", "converted"]
var = pw.loc[pw["variant"] == "variant", "converted"]
n0, n1 = len(ctrl), len(var)
c0, c1 = int(ctrl.sum()), int(var.sum())                             # buyers
p0, p1 = c0 / n0, c1 / n1                                            # conversion rates
print(n0, n1, c0, c1, round(p0, 4), round(p1, 4))
pooled = (c0 + c1) / (n0 + n1)
se = np.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / n0 + 1 / n1))
z = (p1 - p0) / se
p_value = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))
print(round(float(pooled), 4), round(float(se), 4), round(float(z), 2), round(float(p_value), 3))
table = [[c1, n1 - c1], [c0, n0 - c0]]
chi2 = stats.chi2_contingency(table, correction=False)
print(round(float(chi2.statistic), 2), round(float(z ** 2), 2), round(float(chi2.pvalue), 3))
diff = p1 - p0
se_diff = np.sqrt(p0 * (1 - p0) / n0 + p1 * (1 - p1) / n1)
low, high = diff - 1.96 * se_diff, diff + 1.96 * se_diff
print(round(float(100 * diff), 2), round(float(100 * low), 2), round(float(100 * high), 2), round(float(100 * diff / p0), 1))
```

**Predict:** `paywall_v2` gave 12.7 and 15.3 percent. Will the p-value be above or below 0.05? Will the chi-square test of the table agree with the z test? And will the 95 percent interval of the difference stay above 0, with its low end above 1 point?

*Why:* the gap is +2.62 points, z is 2.78 and p is 0.005, below 0.05. The chi-square statistic, 7.74, is $$z^2$$, and its p-value is the same. The interval of the difference runs from +0.78 to +4.47 points: it stays above 0, and its low end is under 1 point. In relative terms, the variant has 20.7 percent more buyers than control.

In symbols, the test statistic for two proportions is

$$
z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_0}{\sqrt{\hat{p}\,(1 - \hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_0} + \frac{1}{n_1}\right)}}, \qquad \hat{p} = \frac{c_0 + c_1}{n_0 + n_1}
$$

where $$\hat{p}_0$$, $$\hat{p}_1$$ are the rates of control and variant, $$n_0$$, $$n_1$$ their sizes, $$c_0$$, $$c_1$$ their buyers and $$\hat{p}$$ the pooled rate. For `paywall_v2`, $$\hat{p} = 0.1399$$ and the denominator is 0.00942, so $$z = (0.1530 - 0.1268) / 0.00942 \approx 2.78$$. The interval of the difference is

$$
(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_0) \pm 1.96 \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_0(1-\hat{p}_0)}{n_0} + \frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1}}
$$

For `paywall_v2`, the square root is 0.00942, so the interval is

$$
0.0262 \pm 1.96 \times 0.00942 \approx 0.0262 \pm 0.0185 = [0.0078,\ 0.0447]
$$

### 2. Two means

**Idea.** For two means, H0 says both groups have the same mean. Welch's t-test divides the gap of the means by a standard error that keeps each group's own spread, so it needs neither equal spreads nor equal sizes; the NIST handbook calls its degrees of freedom robust for unequal sizes and variances. Activity counts are skewed, but with thousands of people the means behave as in lesson C6. SciPy's `ttest_ind(..., equal_var=False)` runs Welch's test, and its result has a `confidence_interval` method for the difference.

**Try it.** Compare the activity of iOS and Android people by hand and with SciPy, then try a test that does not use the means:

### 3. The size of the gap, next to the p-value

**Idea.** To say how big a gap is, report it in the data's own units with its interval, as in units 1 and 2. When the units do not tell the reader how big that is, add Cohen's d: the gap in units of the pooled standard deviation. Sullivan and Feinn give 0.2 as small, 0.5 as medium and 0.8 as large, ballpark categories to read in context.

**Try it.** Print $$s_p$$ and $$d$$ for iOS against Android, then repeat the test with 50, 200, 800 and 3,200 people picked at random from each platform (seed 8):

```python
def cohens_d(a, b):
    """The difference of the means in units of the pooled standard deviation."""
    pooled_sd = np.sqrt(((len(a) - 1) * a.var(ddof=1) + (len(b) - 1) * b.var(ddof=1)) / (len(a) + len(b) - 2))
    return (a.mean() - b.mean()) / pooled_sd


sd_pooled = np.sqrt(((len(ios) - 1) * ios.var(ddof=1) + (len(android) - 1) * android.var(ddof=1)) / (len(ios) + len(android) - 2))
print(round(float(sd_pooled), 2), round(float(cohens_d(ios, android)), 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
for size in (50, 200, 800, 3200):
    a = rng.choice(ios, size=size, replace=False)
    b = rng.choice(android, size=size, replace=False)
    print(size, f"{stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False).pvalue:.2g}", round(float(cohens_d(a, b)), 2))
```

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Shares: z or chi-square. Means: Welch. Skewed samples: Mann-Whitney.
- Report together the p-value (is the gap real?), the gap with its interval (how big, how sure) and, when units do not tell, Cohen's d.
- The p-value shrinks as groups grow, and the gap does not: a small p-value is not a big gap. Judge size against a bar from the decision.
- A verdict has two sentences: what happened, in business units with an interval, and what to do, with the caveat.
- A gap between groups nobody assigned describes the groups; it does not show an effect.

**Common trap:** reading "significant" as "important": iOS against Android has p = 2.2e-11 and d = 0.11.

</Aside>
