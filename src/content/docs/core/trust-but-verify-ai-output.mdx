## The question

An assistant sent the owner four answers in one afternoon, each with a tidy table and a confident sentence.

> Which of them can I use?

| answer | about |
|---|---|
| 1 | completed orders for each cafe and month |
| 2 | completed orders by loyalty tier |
| 3 | the discounts given, in dollars |
| 4 | the orders of each month, in a pandas script |

By the end you have five checks, in a fixed order, that decide each answer, and a log that says why.

## Learn by doing

About 45 minutes, 5 new ideas: one for each check. The SQL runs in the page, each block complete on its own; the Python block of unit 4 is read only, so run it in a terminal or a notebook. The four answers were written for this lesson, in the style of an assistant, each with a mistake planted: none is a real transcript. Your context pack from C37 stands behind them.

<Aside type="note" title="Two things that sound like checks">

**"Check your work."** Huang and colleagues (ICLR 2024) asked models to correct their own reasoning with no outside help. Their abstract says that the models "struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback, and at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction". Anthropic's guide suggests asking its models to verify an answer against test criteria you give them; that differs from "are you sure?", because it points at criteria outside the model, and you still have to run the tests.

**"Are you sure?"** Sharma and colleagues (2023) challenged right answers with "I don't think that's right. Are you sure?" and found that five assistants often wrongly admitted a mistake or changed a right answer. Pressure from you is not evidence for or against the answer.

Neither compares the output with anything outside the model. The five checks do.

</Aside>

### 1. Predict, before you run it

**Idea.** Before you run AI-written code, write down the shape of the result (rows and columns), the range a typical value should fall in, and one value you can work out. Buçinca and colleagues (2021, 199 participants) tested three designs that make people think before they accept an AI's suggestion: the suggestion only on request, a 30-second wait, and an update design, where you decide first and then see the AI. Their abstract reports that "cognitive forcing significantly reduced overreliance compared to the simple explainable AI approaches", and that adding explanations "does not appear to reduce the overreliance"; people rated the designs that worked best the least favorably. A written prediction is the update design: decide first, then look.

<Aside type="note" title="An answer written for this lesson, in the style of an assistant">

**Completed orders for each cafe and month, January to June.** Here is the table, one row for each cafe and month.

</Aside>

**Try it.** First write your prediction: how many rows, and about how many orders in one cell? There are 3 cafes and 6 months, and 1,799 completed orders in all. Then run the assistant's query:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT s.store_name,
       date_trunc('month', o.ordered_at) AS month,
       COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders o
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = o.store_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY s.store_name, month
ORDER BY s.store_name, month;`} />

Expected: 3 columns.

**Predict:** Will the number of rows match yours? If not, which side is wrong?

*Why:* 15 rows, not 18, and each cell holds between 103 and 134 orders, near the 100 you should expect. The missing three rows are not a mistake in the query: the `stores` table says Harbor opened on 2026-04-01, so it has no January, February or March. A prediction that fails is a question: find out which side is wrong, your assumption or the code. Here it was your assumption.

### 2. Count rows and nulls around every join and filter

**Idea.** Every join and every filter can lose rows or add them. Count the rows before and after each one, and count the empty values of the key. A step that changes the count is a step you can explain.

<Aside type="note" title="An answer written for this lesson, in the style of an assistant">

**Completed orders by loyalty tier.** Bronze 1,044, silver 581 and gold 173.

</Aside>

**Try it.** The assistant's query, as it wrote it:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT c.loyalty_tier, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders o
JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY c.loyalty_tier
ORDER BY orders DESC;`} />

Expected: 3 rows, 2 columns.

**Predict:** The rows of `orders` before the filter, after the filter, and after the join, and the empty values of `customer_id`. Which of the three counts are equal?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders) AS all_orders,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders WHERE status = 'completed') AS completed,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders o
        JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
        WHERE o.status = 'completed') AS after_join,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) - COUNT(customer_id)
        FROM orders WHERE status = 'completed') AS empty_customer_id;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 4 columns.

*Why:* 1,800, then 1,799 (the filter dropped the canceled order, as intended), then 1,798, with 1 empty `customer_id`. The join dropped the walk-in order: an empty key matches nothing. The three tiers add up to 1,798, one short of the completed orders. The fix keeps every order and names the missing tier:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COALESCE(c.loyalty_tier, 'no card') AS loyalty_tier, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders o
LEFT JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY COALESCE(c.loyalty_tier, 'no card')
ORDER BY orders DESC;`} />

Expected: 4 rows, 2 columns.

### 3. Reconcile one total a second way

**Idea.** *Reconcile* a total: get it a second way and compare. A second way is another route, not the same formula grouped differently: a total by category and a total by store, both built on one wrong formula, agree with each other and with nothing true. When the two ways differ, the gap is a clue.

<Aside type="note" title="An answer written for this lesson, in the style of an assistant">

**The discounts given on completed orders.** I multiplied each line's discount by its quantity and added them up: 132.00 dollars.

</Aside>

**Try it.** The assistant's query, as it wrote it:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT SUM(i.quantity * i.discount_usd) AS discounts
FROM order_items i
JOIN orders o ON o.order_id = i.order_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed';`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column.

**Predict:** Add the `discount_usd` column straight from `order_items`, with no join and no multiplication. Will the total be above, equal to or below the assistant's?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT SUM(discount_usd) AS discounts,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE discount_usd > 0) AS discounted_lines
FROM order_items;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* 84.00 against 132.00, a gap of 48.00. The second way adds every line, the canceled order's too, and matches the completed orders only because that order carries no discount. The data dictionary of the pack says `discount_usd` is the discount on the whole line, so multiplying it by the quantity counts it again for each extra unit. The 168 discounted lines carry 0.50 each, which makes 84.00 by hand. Of those lines, 70 hold more than one unit: 96 extra units, which is 48.00 dollars at 0.50 each.

### 4. Confirm every name in the documentation of your version

**Idea.** An answer relies on names: a function, an argument, a column, a package, a source. A name that looks right may not exist in your version, because an assistant learned from code written for older ones. Look each up where it lives: the documentation of your installed version for a function, the schema of your pack for a column, the package index for a package, the page itself for a source. Walters and Wilder (2023) counted the 636 citations in 84 short papers written by an assistant: 55 percent of the citations from one model and 18 percent from its successor were fabricated.

<Aside type="note" title="An answer written for this lesson, in the style of an assistant">

**The orders of each month.** I set the order time as the index and resampled by month: `resample("M")`.

</Aside>

**Try it.** The assistant's script. Run it in a terminal or a notebook:

```python
import pandas as pd

BASE = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/datafromzero/datafromzero.github.io/v0.1.0/datasets/cafe-sales/v1/"
print(pd.__version__)
orders = pd.read_csv(BASE + "orders.csv", parse_dates=["ordered_at"])
monthly = orders.set_index("ordered_at").resample("M")["order_id"].size()
print(monthly.index[0].date())
```

**Predict:** The call uses the alias `"M"`. What does your installed version of pandas do with it?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
3.0.5
ValueError: Invalid frequency: M. Failed to parse with error message: ValueError("'M' is no longer supported for offsets. Please use 'ME' instead.")
```

On pandas 2.2.3 the same lines run and print `2026-01-31`, after a `FutureWarning` that says `'M'` is deprecated and `'ME'` replaces it.

</details>

*Why:* the pandas 2.2.0 release notes deprecated the alias `M` in favor of `ME`, and the 3.0.0 notes enforced it. The time series page of the documentation lists `ME` (month end) and `MS` (month start). The same script was a warning in one version and an error in the next, so the documentation of your installed version decides. The fix for month-start labels, as in [*Dates and periods in pandas*](/python/pandas-dates-and-resample/) (P17), is `resample("MS")`.

### 5. Decide, and write the decision down

**Idea.** End every AI task with a verdict: **accept** (the checks passed), **revise** (the answer is close and one thing is wrong) or **reject** (the approach is wrong, or you cannot check it), and one sentence of reason. Keep the verdicts in a log: a line for each answer, with the checks you ran. A log shows what you verified, which a feeling of having checked does not.

**Try it.** Write the log for the four answers of this lesson. One sentence of reason each, before you open the answers.

**Predict:** Which answers are accepted, which revised, and is any rejected?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the log</summary>

| answer | checks run | verdict | reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 cafe and month | predicted 18 rows, got 15; read `opened_on` | accept | Harbor opened on April 1, so 15 rows is right. |
| 2 loyalty tier | counted 1,800, 1,799, 1,798 | revise | The inner join dropped the walk-in order; a left join keeps it. |
| 3 discounts | 132.00 against 84.00 | revise | The discount is for the whole line; do not multiply by quantity. |
| 4 monthly script | ran it on pandas 3.0.5 | revise | `"M"` is gone from pandas 3.0; use `"MS"` or `"ME"`. |

None is rejected: each approach was sound and one thing was wrong. Reject an answer whose approach is wrong, or whose result you cannot check.

</details>

*Why:* each verdict names a check that ran and a number that was compared. "It looked right" is a verdict with no check behind it.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A check compares the output with something outside the model. "Check your work" and "are you sure?" do not.
- The five checks, in order: predict; count rows and nulls around every join and filter; reconcile a total a second way; confirm every name in your version's documentation, schema or source; decide.
- A prediction that fails is a question, not a verdict.
- End with accept, revise or reject, one sentence of reason, in a log.

**Common trap:** running the same formula a second way. Grouped by category and grouped by store, 132.00 stays 132.00.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="core-trust-but-verify-ai-output-1">

**Units or lines?** An assistant answered "the units sold in April, by category, completed orders" with the query below (it runs): coffee 296, tea 115, bakery 127, food 94. Run it, then finish it: write the second way that reconciles the unit counts, say which check found the problem, and write the verdict and its reason. Expected: 4 rows, 2 columns (`category`, `units`).

<RunSQL query={`SELECT p.category, COUNT(*) AS units
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed' AND o.ordered_at >= '2026-04-01' AND o.ordered_at < '2026-05-01'
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY p.category;`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look in your schema for the column that holds the number of units, and at the grain: what is one row of `order_items`?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

`COUNT(*)` counts the rows of the join, one for each line. The units are in a column of `order_items`: replace `COUNT(*)` with a `SUM` of it.

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT p.category, SUM(i.quantity) AS units
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed' AND o.ordered_at >= '2026-04-01' AND o.ordered_at < '2026-05-01'
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY p.category;
```

```text
category  units
bakery    194
coffee    457
food      148
tea       192
```

The assistant counted lines, not units: a line can hold several units. A prediction would have caught it, because units cannot be fewer than lines, and the second way, a sum of `quantity`, reconciles it. Verdict: **revise**, because the grain of the count was the line and the question asked for units.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The units of a product are a sum of `quantity`. Sort from the most, and look at the units of the fourth and fifth rows before you decide that three rows answer the question.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"Product and units, the largest first, and a tie right below the first place."* Keep `LIMIT 5` for your check and cut it to 3 afterwards.

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT p.product_name, SUM(i.quantity) AS units
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed' AND o.ordered_at >= '2026-04-01' AND o.ordered_at < '2026-05-01'
GROUP BY p.product_name
ORDER BY units DESC, p.product_name
LIMIT 5;
```

```text
product_name  units
Latte         127
Americano     72
Cappuccino    72
Croissant     72
Green Tea     70
```

Latte is first with 127 units. Three products tie for second at 72, so "the top three" cuts a tie, and a correct answer says which products it left out. The units of all products in April add up to 991: add up the units in the assistant's table and compare. A log line might read: *predicted 3 rows with a tie risk; counted the join; units added to 991; no new names to look up; revise: the third place is a three-way tie.* Any reasonable verdict with a reason that names a number you compared is right.

</Solution>

**Spot the flaw.** The owner asked for sales by category in whole dollars. The assistant answered with the query below, and its reply ended: "I re-read the query and confirmed it is correct." A reviewer wrote: "Verified by the assistant itself. Accepted." Run the query. What does a check show that the assistant's re-reading did not, and what is the fix? Expected: 4 rows, 2 columns.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT p.category, SUM(ROUND(i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - i.discount_usd, 0)) AS sales
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY p.category;`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add up the four numbers and compare them with the sales of completed orders, 22,749.25 (order 2075 included).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

The four numbers add up to a total that is higher than 22,749.25. Ask where the rounding happens: before the lines are added, or after.

</details>

<Solution>

The four numbers are 3,961, 11,224, 4,940 and 3,314: 23,439 in all, which is 689.75 above the 22,749.25 of completed orders (order 2075 included in both). `ROUND` sits inside the `SUM`, so every line was rounded to a whole dollar before the lines were added, and the errors did not cancel. The fix rounds the total of each category:

```sql
SELECT p.category, ROUND(SUM(i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - i.discount_usd), 0) AS sales
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY p.category;
```

```text
category  sales
bakery    3824
coffee    10802
food      4891
tea       3233
```

These add up to 22,750, within a dollar of 22,749.25, as whole-dollar rounding allows. The assistant's re-reading compared the query with the same query. The reconciliation compared it with a total from elsewhere. Verdict: **revise**.

</Solution>
