## The question

The owner asks:

> Last meeting you said that about a quarter of orders are paid by mobile and that an order is worth about 12.80 dollars, from a sample of 200 orders. How much can I trust those two numbers? Give me a range I can plan with.

The first rows of that sample, 200 orders drawn at random with replacement from the 1,800 (an order can come up twice):

| order_id | store_id | ordered_at | payment_method | value |
|---:|---:|---|---|---:|
| 1161 | 2 | 2026-01-22 13:44:00 | card | 14.25 |
| 2394 | 1 | 2026-05-27 10:52:00 | cash | 13.00 |
| 2179 | 2 | 2026-05-10 13:33:00 | card | 24.50 |
| 1790 | 3 | 2026-04-06 14:40:00 | cash | 13.75 |

By the end you can give the owner a range for a share, a mean and a median, say what the 95 percent promises, and finish Part 1 with a one-page report.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Several metrics in one pass*](/sql/conditional-aggregation/) (S9), a share is the rows that pass a test divided by all the rows. `(sample["payment_method"] == "mobile").mean()` is its twin: `True` counts as 1, so the mean of the column is the share.

**Diverge.** A SQL query returns one number for a share. An interval needs a second number, how far off the first could be. Unit 1 builds it.

</Aside>

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   `8.62 / 100 ** 0.5`, which is 0.862: the SD divided by the square root of n.

   </details>

2. In [*Samples and the ways they mislead*](/core/sampling-and-bias/) (C5), suppose a convenience sample were made ten times larger, so that its interval for the mean becomes very narrow. Does the narrow interval fix its bias?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   No. More orders shrink the luck of the draw, not a bias.

   </details>

### 1. Estimate plus and minus a margin

**Idea.** A confidence interval is an estimate plus and minus a margin: $$\text{estimate} \pm \text{multiplier} \times \text{SE}$$. For a share the SE is the square root of $$\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n$$ and the 95 percent multiplier is 1.96, as in the OpenIntro textbook, which asks for at least 10 successes and 10 failures. For a mean the SE is $$s/\sqrt{n}$$ from lesson C6, and the multiplier $$t^{*}$$ comes from the t distribution with $$n-1$$ degrees of freedom, because s is itself an estimate; `stats.t.interval` leaves equal areas out on each side, as the SciPy documentation puts it. We draw with replacement, so the draws are independent and the formulas apply.

**Try it.** Draw 200 orders with replacement, and build the interval for the share paid by mobile:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
pick = rng.integers(0, len(orders), size=200)             # 200 order positions, drawn with replacement
sample = orders.iloc[pick]                                # the sample of 200 orders
n = len(sample)
p_hat = (sample["payment_method"] == "mobile").mean()     # the share paid by mobile in the sample
se = np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)                     # the standard error of a share
print(n, round(float(p_hat), 3), round(float(se), 3))
print(round(float(p_hat - 1.96 * se), 3), round(float(p_hat + 1.96 * se), 3))
print(round(float((orders["payment_method"] == "mobile").mean()), 3))   # the truth for all 1,800 orders
```

**Predict:** a sample of 200 orders gives a share of mobile payments. Will the interval around it contain the share of all 1,800 orders, printed last? And how wide will it be: a few percentage points or tens?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text

0.199 0.321
0.303
```

</details>

*Why:* the sample share is 0.26 with a standard error of 0.031, so the margin is 1.96 × 0.031 and the interval runs from 0.199 to 0.321. The share of all 1,800 orders, 0.303, is inside. The interval is about 12 percentage points wide: 200 orders do not pin a share down.

In symbols, the interval for a share is

$$
\hat{p} \pm 1.96 \times \mathrm{SE}, \qquad \mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}
$$

where $$\hat{p}$$ ("p hat") is the share in the sample, $$n$$ is the sample size and the part after the ± is the margin (the margin of error). In the sample, 52 of the 200 orders were paid by mobile, so $$\hat{p} = 0.26$$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.26 \times 0.74}{200}} \approx 0.031 \\
\text{interval} \approx 0.26 \pm 1.96 \times 0.031 \\
\approx 0.26 \pm 0.061 = [0.199,\ 0.321]
\end{gathered}
$$

**Try it.** Build the interval for the mean order value of the same sample, by hand and with SciPy:

```python
x = sample["value"].to_numpy()                    # the 200 order values
m, s = x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1)
sem = s / np.sqrt(n)                              # the standard error of the mean, estimated from the sample
t_star = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n - 1)             # the t multiplier for 95 percent
print(round(float(m), 2), round(float(sem), 2), round(float(t_star), 3))
print(round(float(m - t_star * sem), 2), round(float(m + t_star * sem), 2))
low, high = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=n - 1, loc=m, scale=sem)    # the same interval from SciPy
print(round(float(low), 2), round(float(high), 2), round(float(mu), 2))
```

**Predict:** for a mean, the multiplier is $$t^{*}$$ instead of 1.96. Will $$t^{*}$$ for 200 orders be above or below 1.96? Will SciPy's interval agree with the hand-made one, and will it contain the true mean, printed last?

*Why:* $$t^{*}$$ is 1.972, a little above 1.96, and the interval, 11.80 to 13.81, is the same by hand and from SciPy. The true mean, 12.64, is inside it.

In symbols, the interval for a mean is

$$
\bar{x} \pm t^{*} \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

where $$\bar{x}$$ is the sample mean, $$s$$ is the sample SD (dividing by $$n-1$$), $$n$$ is the sample size and $$t^{*}$$ is the multiplier for $$n-1$$ degrees of freedom; the part after the ± is the margin. The sample has $$n = 200$$ orders and, to three decimals, $$\bar{x} = 12.804$$ and $$s = 7.196$$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathrm{SE} = \frac{7.196}{\sqrt{200}} \approx 0.509 \\
\text{interval} \approx 12.804 \pm 1.972 \times 0.509 \\
\approx 12.804 \pm 1.004 = [11.80,\ 13.81]
\end{gathered}
$$

### 2. What the 95 percent means, and what makes an interval narrower

**Idea.** A confidence level belongs to the recipe, not to one interval, as the Seeing Theory page puts it. So the way to test a recipe is to run it many times: draw a sample, build the interval the same way, check it against the truth, and repeat.

**Try it.** Build the interval from 1,000 different samples of 200 orders and count how often it holds the true mean:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
low_miss = high_miss = 0
spans = []                                        # the first 20 intervals, for the picture
for k in range(1000):
    xs = values[rng.integers(0, len(values), size=200)]
    lo, hi = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=199, loc=xs.mean(), scale=xs.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(200))
    low_miss += hi < mu                           # the interval lies below the true mean
```

*Why:* the width is twice the multiplier times $$s/\sqrt{n}$$. Each row is about half as wide as the one above it (4.90, 2.40, 1.20 at 95 percent): four times the orders halve the width. Along a row a higher level is wider: more certainty, less precision.

In symbols:

$$
\text{width} = 2 \times t^{*} \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

With $$s = 8.62$$, the SD of all the orders dividing by $$n-1$$, and the 95 percent multiplier, which is $$t^{*} = 1.972$$ for $$n = 200$$ and $$t^{*} = 1.963$$ for $$n = 800$$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
n = 200: \quad  2 \times 1.972 \times \frac{8.62}{\sqrt{200}} \approx 2.40 \\
n = 800: \quad  2 \times 1.963 \times \frac{8.62}{\sqrt{800}} \approx 1.20
\end{gathered}
$$

Since $$\sqrt{800} = 2\sqrt{200}$$ and $$t^{*}$$ hardly changes, the width halves.

### 3. A bootstrap interval for a median

**Idea.** The median has no simple formula for its SE from the data alone. The bootstrap builds the sampling distribution from the sample itself: resample it with replacement to the same size, take the median of each resample, and use the middle 95 percent of the medians. Seeing Theory describes it as resampling with replacement from the empirical distribution; SciPy's `bootstrap` has a `percentile` method for it.

**Try it.** Bootstrap the median of the 200 orders of unit 1:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
boot = np.array([np.median(rng.choice(x, size=len(x))) for _ in range(2000)])    # 2,000 resamples of the sample, with replacement
print(round(float(np.median(x)), 2), round(float(boot.std(ddof=1)), 2))
b_low, b_high = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])                                 # the middle 95 percent of the 2,000 medians
print(round(float(b_low), 2), round(float(b_high), 2), round(float(np.median(values)), 2))
res = stats.bootstrap((x,), np.median, confidence_level=0.95, method="percentile", rng=np.random.default_rng(7))
print(round(float(res.confidence_interval.low), 2), round(float(res.confidence_interval.high), 2))
```

The cell builds two ranges for the next order, one from a formula that assumes a bell shape and one from the sample's own 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, which assumes none, and checks each against all 1,800 orders.

*Why:* the range for one order is about 28 dollars wide, against 2 for the mean: no sample size shrinks it below the spread of single orders. The NIST handbook explains, for regression, that an interval for a new measurement is wider because it adds the uncertainty of the new measurement to that of the estimate. The bell formula is $$\bar{x} \pm t^{*} \times s \times \sqrt{1 + 1/n}$$, and NIST calls it fairly tolerant of non-normal data unless the sample is small or strongly skewed. Its low end is below zero, which no order can be, but its range holds 95.2 percent of all orders: the low end fails, not the coverage, and that 0.952 is a share of orders, not the 952 of 1,000 intervals of unit 2. The percentile range, 3.25 to 31.51, holds 96.3 percent; we can check that only because all 1,800 orders are in hand.

With the sample of unit 1 ($$n = 200$$, $$\bar{x} = 12.804$$, $$s = 7.196$$, $$t^{*} = 1.972$$), the factor $$\sqrt{1 + 1/n}$$ adds the spread of the new order to the uncertainty of the mean:

$$
\begin{gathered}
12.804 \pm 1.972 \times 7.196 \times \sqrt{1 + \tfrac{1}{200}} \\
\approx 12.804 \pm 14.226 = [-1.42,\ 27.03]
\end{gathered}
$$

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- An interval is $$\text{estimate} \pm \text{multiplier} \times \text{SE}$$: 1.96 for a share, $$t^{*}$$ for a mean.
- The 95 percent describes the recipe: in the long run about 95 of 100 such intervals hold the parameter.
- Four times the sample halves the width; a higher level widens it.
- The bootstrap, resampling with replacement, gives an interval when no formula fits.
- An interval for the mean is not a range for one order: that one is far wider.

**Common trap:** treating an interval as a repair for a bad sample: it measures the luck of the draw, not a bias.

</Aside>
