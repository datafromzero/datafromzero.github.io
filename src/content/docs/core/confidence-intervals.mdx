The owner asks:

> Last meeting you said that about a quarter of orders are paid by mobile and that an order is worth about 12.80 dollars, from a sample of 200 orders. How much can I trust those two numbers? Give me a range I can plan with.

The first rows of that sample, 200 orders drawn at random from the 1,800:

| order_id | store_id | ordered_at | payment_method | value |
|---:|---:|---|---|---:|
| 1161 | 2 | 2026-01-22 13:44:00 | card | 14.25 |
| 2394 | 1 | 2026-05-27 10:52:00 | cash | 13.00 |
| 2179 | 2 | 2026-05-10 13:33:00 | card | 24.50 |
| 1790 | 3 | 2026-04-06 14:40:00 | cash | 13.75 |

By the end you can give the owner a range for a share, a mean and a median, say what the 95 percent promises, and finish Part 1 with a one-page report.

**Match.** In *Several metrics in one pass* (S9), a share is the rows that pass a test divided by all the rows. `(sample["payment_method"] == "mobile").mean()` is its twin: `True` counts as 1, so the mean of the column is the share.

**Diverge.** A SQL query returns one number for a share. An interval needs a second number, how far off the first could be. Unit 1 builds it.

## The big idea

An estimate on its own hides how far off it could be, so you report it with a margin made from its standard error.

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 4 new ideas: the interval for a share and a mean, the reading of the 95 percent, the bootstrap, the prediction interval. The setup cell gave `values`, the 1,800 order values, and `mu`, their mean. The 1,800 orders play the population, so each interval can be checked against the truth.

### 1. Estimate plus and minus a margin

**Idea.** A confidence interval is an estimate plus and minus a margin: estimate ± multiplier × SE. For a share, the SE is the square root of p̂(1 − p̂)/n and the multiplier for 95 percent is 1.96, as in the OpenIntro textbook; it asks for at least 10 successes and 10 failures in the sample. For a mean, the SE is s/√n from lesson C6, and the multiplier `t*` comes from the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom, because s is itself an estimate. `stats.t.interval` gives the interval that leaves equal areas out on each side, as the SciPy documentation puts it.

**Try it.** Draw 200 orders with replacement, and build the interval for the share paid by mobile:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
pick = rng.integers(0, len(orders), size=200)             # 200 order positions, drawn with replacement
sample = orders.iloc[pick]                                # the sample of 200 orders
n = len(sample)
p_hat = (sample["payment_method"] == "mobile").mean()     # the share paid by mobile in the sample
se = np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)                     # the standard error of a share
print(n, round(float(p_hat), 3), round(float(se), 3))
print(round(float(p_hat - 1.96 * se), 3), round(float(p_hat + 1.96 * se), 3))
print(round(float((orders["payment_method"] == "mobile").mean()), 3))   # the truth for all 1,800 orders
```

**Predict:** a sample of 200 orders gives a share of mobile payments. Will the interval around it contain the share of all 1,800 orders, printed last? And how wide will it be: a few percentage points or tens?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
200 0.26 0.031
0.199 0.321
0.303
```

</details>

*Why:* the sample share is 0.26 with a standard error of 0.031, so the margin is 1.96 × 0.031 and the interval runs from 0.199 to 0.321. The share of all 1,800 orders, 0.303, is inside. The interval is about 12 percentage points wide: 200 orders do not pin a share down.

**Try it.** Build the interval for the mean order value of the same sample, by hand and with SciPy:

```python
x = sample["value"].to_numpy()                    # the 200 order values
m, s = x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1)
sem = s / np.sqrt(n)                              # the standard error of the mean, estimated from the sample
t_star = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n - 1)             # the t multiplier for 95 percent
print(round(float(m), 2), round(float(sem), 2), round(float(t_star), 3))
print(round(float(m - t_star * sem), 2), round(float(m + t_star * sem), 2))
low, high = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=n - 1, loc=m, scale=sem)    # the same interval from SciPy
print(round(float(low), 2), round(float(high), 2), round(float(mu), 2))
```

**Predict:** for a mean, the multiplier is `t*` instead of 1.96. Will `t*` for 200 orders be above or below 1.96? Will SciPy's interval agree with the hand-made one, and will it contain the true mean, printed last?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
12.8 0.51 1.972
11.8 13.81
11.8 13.81 12.64
```

</details>

*Why:* `t*` is 1.972, a little above 1.96, and the interval, 11.80 to 13.81, is the same by hand and from SciPy. The true mean, 12.64, is inside it.

### 2. What the 95 percent means, and what makes an interval narrower

**Idea.** The 95 percent describes the recipe, not one interval: draw a sample, build the interval the same way, and repeat, as the Seeing Theory page puts it. Read one interval as the range of values for the mean that the data do not rule out. It is not a range that holds 95 percent of the orders, nor a 95 percent chance that this interval holds the mean. Hoekstra and colleagues (2014) gave 120 researchers and 442 students six false statements about a confidence interval; on average they accepted more than three.

**Try it.** Build the interval from 1,000 different samples of 200 orders and count how often it holds the true mean:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
low_miss = high_miss = 0
spans = []                                        # the first 20 intervals, for the picture
for k in range(1000):
    xs = values[rng.integers(0, len(values), size=200)]
    lo, hi = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=199, loc=xs.mean(), scale=xs.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(200))
    low_miss += hi < mu                           # the interval lies below the true mean
    high_miss += lo > mu                          # the interval lies above it
    if k < 20:
        spans.append((float(lo), float(hi)))
print(1000 - low_miss - high_miss, low_miss, high_miss)
print(sum(1 for lo, hi in spans if lo <= mu <= hi), "of the first 20 contain the true mean")
```

**Predict:** how many of the 1,000 intervals will contain the true mean? Of those that miss it, will more lie below it or above it?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
952 41 7
17 of the first 20 contain the true mean
```

**Idea.** The width of the interval is the multiplier times s/√n. The next cell holds s fixed at the SD of all orders and changes n and the confidence level.

**Try it.** Print the width of the interval, from low end to high end:

```python
s_all = values.std(ddof=1)                        # the spread of single orders, kept fixed

```

### 3. A bootstrap interval for a median

**Idea.** The median has no simple formula for its SE. The bootstrap builds the sampling distribution from the sample itself: treat the sample as a stand-in for the population, resample it with replacement to the same size, compute the median of each resample, and take the middle 95 percent of the medians. Seeing Theory describes it as resampling with replacement from the empirical distribution; SciPy's `bootstrap` has a `percentile` method for the same idea.

**Try it.** Bootstrap the median of the 200 orders of unit 1:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
boot = np.array([np.median(rng.choice(x, size=len(x))) for _ in range(2000)])    # 2,000 resamples of the sample, with replacement
print(round(float(np.median(x)), 2), round(float(boot.std(ddof=1)), 2))
b_low, b_high = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])                                 # the middle 95 percent of the 2,000 medians
print(round(float(b_low), 2), round(float(b_high), 2), round(float(np.median(values)), 2))
res = stats.bootstrap((x,), np.median, confidence_level=0.95, method="percentile", rng=np.random.default_rng(7))
print(round(float(res.confidence_interval.low), 2), round(float(res.confidence_interval.high), 2))
```

**Predict:** the cell resamples the 200 orders 2,000 times and takes the median of each resample. Will the interval contain the sample's median? Will it contain the median of all 1,800 orders, printed last?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
12.5 0.66
10.75 13.0 11.75
10.75 13.0
```

</details>

*Why:* the sample median is 12.5 and the interval, 10.75 to 13.0, holds both it and the true median, 11.75. The SD of the 2,000 medians, 0.66, is the bootstrap standard error. SciPy's `bootstrap` gives the same ends here.

### 4. An interval for the mean and an interval for the next order

**Idea.** An interval for the mean says where the parameter probably sits. A prediction interval says where one new value will probably fall, and the NIST handbook explains, for regression, why it is wider: it includes the uncertainty of the new measurement as well as that of the estimate. The results below show the same here. Under a bell-shape assumption it is x̄ ± `t*` × s × √(1 + 1/n), and the NIST reference says that this formula is fairly tolerant of non-normal data unless the sample is small or the data strongly skewed. The sample's own 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are a rough alternative that assumes no bell.

**Try it.** Build both ranges for one new order, and check them against all 1,800 orders:

```python
half = t_star * s * np.sqrt(1 + 1 / n)            # the bell-shape formula for the next single order
pi_low, pi_high = m - half, m + half
q_low, q_high = np.percentile(x, [2.5, 97.5])     # the middle 95 percent of the sample itself
print(round(float(pi_low), 2), round(float(pi_high), 2))
print(round(float(q_low), 2), round(float(q_high), 2))
share = lambda lo, hi: round(float(((values >= lo) & (values <= hi)).mean()), 3)    # the share of all 1,800 orders inside
print(share(pi_low, pi_high), share(q_low, q_high), float(values.min()))
```

**Predict:** the interval for the mean of the sample was 11.80 to 13.81 dollars. How much wider will an interval for one single new order be? Can the formula's lower end be a real order value, when the cheapest order of all is printed last?

<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.952 0.963 3.0
```

*Why:* the range for one order is about 28 dollars wide, against 2 for the mean: no sample size shrinks it below the spread of single orders. The formula's low end is below zero, which no order can be: the values lean right and are not bell-shaped. The percentile range, 3.25 to 31.51, holds 96.3 percent of all orders; we can check that only because all 1,800 are in hand.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- An interval is the estimate ± multiplier × SE: 1.96 for a share, `t*` for a mean.
- The 95 percent describes the recipe: in the long run about 95 of 100 such intervals hold the parameter.
- Four times the sample halves the width; a higher level widens it.
- The bootstrap, resampling with replacement, gives an interval when no formula fits.
- An interval for the mean is not a range for one order: that one is far wider.

**Common trap:** reading "95 percent" as "95 percent of the orders lie in it" or as "this interval holds the mean with 95 percent chance".

</Aside>
