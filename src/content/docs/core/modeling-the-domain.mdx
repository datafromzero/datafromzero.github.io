## Can you skip Part 3?

Part 3, Shapes of data, has six lessons, C16 to C21, and builds a star schema of the cafe data. Try these six questions without looking anything up; the numbers are made up. If all six answers are right, go on to Part 4, which starts with *Your laptop setup and the terminal*. If not, start here.

1. A shop has `orders` (1,000 rows) and `order_items` (2,400 rows, one for each product on an order). You join them on `order_id` and add up an amount that sits on each order row. Is the total too big, too small or right?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Too big. An order with three items appears three times in the join, so its amount is added three times.

   </details>

2. A course has many students and a student takes many courses. Each student gets a grade in each course. How many tables do you need, and which one holds the grade?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Three: students, courses, and a table with one row for each student and course. The grade belongs to the pair, so it sits in that third table.

   </details>

3. A table has one row for each cafe and day, with the day's sales. Name a question it cannot answer.

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Anything finer than a day, such as the busiest hour or which products sell together. A coarse table cannot be split back into the detail it was added from.

   </details>

4. A model that predicts which new signups will buy a paid plan scores 92 percent on the rows it was built from. What do you compare it with before you believe it?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   A trivial baseline, such as always answering the most common outcome, on rows the model has never seen. The score on the rows it learned from says little.

   </details>

5. A model that predicts who will cancel uses a column `cancellation_date` and scores almost perfectly. What is wrong?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Leakage. The column is filled only after a customer has canceled, so it holds the answer. A feature must be known at the moment of the prediction.

   </details>

6. Only 1 card payment in 100 is fraud. A model that says "not fraud" every time is right 99 percent of the time. Which number shows that it is useless?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Recall: it catches none of the fraud cases. Accuracy hides rare events, so weigh each kind of error by what it costs.

   </details>

## The question

> Before anyone writes a query, tell the new person what we keep track of, how the pieces connect, and what one row of each stands for.

This is how the owner describes the business:

> We have three cafes. Some customers join the loyalty program, and a customer can be referred by another customer. ### 1. Entities and attributes

**Idea.** An *entity* is a kind of thing the business keeps facts about, such as a store. An *attribute* is one fact about a thing, such as the city of a store. A noun is an entity when the business keeps facts of its own about it; a noun that is only one fact about something else is an attribute. Each entity needs a *key*, a value that no two things share. Peter Chen proposed describing a business as entities and relationships, drawn as a diagram, in 1976.

**Try it.** List the tables of the cafe database with their columns:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT table_name,
       COUNT(*) AS columns,
       string_agg(column_name, ', ' ORDER BY ordinal_position) AS column_names
FROM information_schema.columns
WHERE table_schema = 'main'
GROUP BY table_name
ORDER BY table_name;`} />

**Predict:** the owner's description names these nouns: store, customer, order, line, product, payment method, category, city and loyalty tier. Which of them would you give a table of its own?

Expected: 3 columns (the table, its number of columns, their names).

*Why:* five tables: `stores`, `customers`, `products`, `orders` and `order_items`. Payment method, category, city and loyalty tier are columns: the business keeps no facts of its own about them. The line is the surprise. The owner never calls it a thing, yet it has a table, because it carries facts of its own (a quantity and a discount). A noun can move from attribute to entity: if each category had a manager and a margin target, `category` would earn a table.

### 2. Relationships and cardinality

**Idea.** A *relationship* says how two entities connect, in a sentence with a verb: a store takes orders. Its *cardinality* says how many of one side go with one of the other: one store takes many orders, which is *one-to-many*. A one-to-many relationship is stored on the many side, in a *foreign key*, a column that holds the key of the one side. Each end can also allow none: a link may be optional.

**Try it.** Count the orders of each customer, and the orders that have no customer:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS customers,
       MIN(n) AS fewest_orders,
       MAX(n) AS most_orders,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE n = 0) AS never_ordered,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders WHERE customer_id IS NULL) AS orders_without_customer
FROM (SELECT c.customer_id, COUNT(o.order_id) AS n
      FROM customers AS c
      LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
      GROUP BY c.customer_id);`} />

**Predict:** one customer can have many orders. What is the fewest orders a customer has, and can an order have no customer?

Expected: 1 row, 5 columns.

*Why:* of 150 customers, 5 never ordered and the most orders by one customer is 29. One order has no customer, a walk-in. So an order has zero or one customer, and a customer has zero or many orders. The diagram below says the same in pictures.

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why does <code>orders</code> hold the key of <code>stores</code>, and not <code>stores</code> the key of its orders?</summary>

A store has hundreds of orders (Old Town has 729) and a cell holds one value. With the key on `orders`, each order row names its one store, and any number of orders can name the same store.

</details>

### 3. A link table for many to many

**Idea.** In a *many-to-many* relationship each side can have many of the other: an order holds many products, and a product is on many orders. No column on either table can store that. The link becomes a table of its own, with one foreign key for each side. A fact about the pair, such as how many units, belongs to neither side alone, so it sits in the link table. The PostgreSQL manual builds the same shape from `products`, `orders` and a third table, `order_items`.

**Try it.** `order_items` is the link table of the cafe database. Count its lines, the orders and products they point at, and the different pairs of order and product:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS lines,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders,
       COUNT(DISTINCT product_id) AS products,
       COUNT(DISTINCT (order_id, product_id)) AS pairs
FROM order_items;`} />

**Predict:** will the number of pairs equal the number of lines? If it does, which two columns could be the primary key of the table?

Expected: 1 row, 4 columns.

*Why:* 3,287 lines, 1,800 orders, 15 products and 3,287 pairs. The pairs equal the lines, so no product appears on two lines of one order, and the pair (`order_id`, `product_id`) could be the key. It states a rule of the business, one line for each product on an order, where a second unit goes into `quantity`. The data also carries `order_item_id`, a key of its own. `quantity` and `discount_usd` describe the pair.

**Try it.** The owner now wants to record which cafe stocks which product, and the day it started selling it. That is another many-to-many link. The tables you query declare no keys, and a foreign key needs a key to point at, so the block declares two first. Run it once; Reset data under the block starts again.

**Predict:** the last statement repeats the first pair with another date. What happens?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT tc.constraint_type, kcu.column_name, kcu.ordinal_position AS position
FROM information_schema.table_constraints AS tc
JOIN information_schema.key_column_usage AS kcu
  ON kcu.constraint_name = tc.constraint_name AND kcu.table_name = tc.table_name
WHERE tc.table_name = 'store_menu'

*Why:* two foreign keys, one for each side, and a primary key on the pair. `listed_on` is not in the key: it describes the pair. DuckDB requires a foreign key to point at a primary key or a unique column of another table, which is why the two keys came first.

### 4. The grain, and what a wrong one costs

**Idea.** The *grain* of a table is what one row stands for, said in one sentence: one row for each order line. State it before you write `CREATE TABLE`, because every key, join and total depends on it. The pages of Kimball Group on dimensional modeling use the word for fact tables: the grain says exactly what one row stands for. This idea is the first step of the next lesson, *Grain, facts and dimensions*.

**Try it.** The total of each order, one row for each order, added up:

<RunSQL query={`WITH order_totals AS (
  SELECT i.order_id, SUM(i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - i.discount_usd) AS order_total
  FROM order_items AS i
  JOIN products AS p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
  GROUP BY i.order_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows, ROUND(SUM(order_total), 2) AS total
FROM order_totals;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns: the 1,800 orders and their total, 22,754.25 dollars, canceled order and order 2075 included.

**Try it.** The same totals, joined to the lines of each order:

<RunSQL query={`WITH order_totals AS (
  SELECT i.order_id, SUM(i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - i.discount_usd) AS order_total
  FROM order_items AS i
  JOIN products AS p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
  GROUP BY i.order_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows, ROUND(SUM(t.order_total), 2) AS total
FROM orders AS o
JOIN order_items AS i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN order_totals AS t ON t.order_id = o.order_id;`} />

**Predict:** every line has its order, so the join is correct. Will the total stay the same, grow a little, or grow a lot?

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* 3,287 rows and 48,443.50 dollars, 2.1 times the real total. The join result has one row for each line, but the number belongs to the order, so an order with three lines is added three times. Nothing raised an error. You met this total in *Join fan-out* (S21); now you can name the cause: a number measured at the grain of the order was added up at the grain of the line. Declare the grain of each table, and of each join result, before you add anything up.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A noun the business keeps facts about is an entity; a noun that is one fact about something is an attribute.
- A one-to-many link is a foreign key on the many side. Ask each end whether it allows none.
- A many-to-many link is a table of its own, with a key for each side and the facts about the pair.
- Say what one row stands for before `CREATE TABLE`.
- A number measured at one grain and added up at another is counted once for every row it is repeated on.

**Common trap:** "if I join the tables I need, the numbers will be right." The join of `orders` to `order_items` turns 22,754.25 dollars of sales into 48,443.50.

</Aside>
