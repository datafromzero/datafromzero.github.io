### 1. The comparison picks the chart

**Idea.** Before you draw, write the comparison the reader must make as one sentence: the sentence picks the chart.

Cleveland and McGill ranked graphical tasks by how accurately people judge them: a position along a common scale is read more accurately than an angle or an area. The comparison decides which task the chart asks of the eye.

**Try it:** draw the weekly conversion of control and variant, and then the gap of each week.

```python
print(weekly.round(1).to_string())
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
weekly[["control", "variant"]].plot.bar(ax=ax, color=[GREEN, PURPLE])        # the default: two bars for each week
ax.set_title("Conversion by week")
print(len(ax.patches))
fig2, ax2 = plt.subplots()
ax2.bar(weekly.index, weekly["gap"], color=BLUE)                              # the comparison itself: one bar for each week
ax2.set_title("Lift by week")
print(len(ax2.patches), int(weekly["gap"].idxmax()), round(float(weekly["gap"].max()), 1))
plt.close("all")
```

**Predict:** a reader must see how the lift changed from week to week. Which chart gets them there faster: two bars for each week, or one bar for each week showing the gap? And in which week was the gap biggest?

*Why:* the first chart has 16 bars, and the reader pairs them and subtracts eight times in their head: two bars ask for a comparison of lengths and then a subtraction. The second has 8 bars and shows the gap itself, one reading each: week 1 had the biggest gap, +6.6 points, and week 7 was below zero, which in the first chart is a small difference between two bars. When the level matters too, keep both charts: the gap chart does not show that conversion sits between 11 and 19 percent. Five comparisons and the chart each one calls for:

| the reader must see | chart |
|---|---|
| which of a few categories is biggest | bars from zero, sorted |
| how something moves in time | a line, or bars in time order |
| how big the gap between two groups is | the gap itself, one bar for each period |
| how values spread | a histogram |
| how an estimate compares with a bar, with its uncertainty | a dot with its interval and a line for the bar |

Cleveland and McGill wrote that graphs "should employ elementary tasks as high in the ordering as possible", the order being how accurately people judge each task. A chart asks the reader's eye for some of those tasks, and the comparison decides which ones.

### 2. Take away what does not help

**Idea.** Every mark either carries data or helps the reader read it; any other mark is ink to remove. The test for a mark is whether the reader loses anything when it goes.

**Try it:** start from a cluttered chart of the gap by week, then take away what does not help and write each value on its bar.

```python
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
bars = ax.bar(weekly.index, weekly["gap"], color=GRAY)
for side in ax.spines.values():                                              # start from a cluttered chart: a frame on all four sides and gridlines
    side.set_visible(True)
ax.grid(True)
print(sum(side.get_visible() for side in ax.spines.values()), ax.yaxis.get_gridlines()[0].get_visible(), ax.yaxis.get_visible(), len(ax.texts))
for name in ("top", "right", "left"):                                        # take away: the frame, except the line the bars stand on
    ax.spines[name].set_visible(False)
ax.grid(False)                                                               # the gridlines
ax.yaxis.set_visible(False)                                                  # the y axis: every bar carries its value
ax.bar_label(bars, labels=[f"{value:+.1f}" for value in weekly["gap"]], fontsize=9)
ax.set_title("Lift by week")
print([name for name, side in ax.spines.items() if side.get_visible()], ax.yaxis.get_gridlines()[0].get_visible(), ax.yaxis.get_visible(), len(ax.texts))
plt.close("all")
```

### 4. The decision memo

**Idea.** A memo is a page for someone who may stop reading after one line. Its parts, in order: the *decision*, what to do and by when; the *context*, what was tested and what was planned; the *tension*, what keeps the decision from being obvious; the *evidence*, one chart or number for each claim; the *recommendation*, the next step and what would change it. The American Statistical Association says a p-value does not measure the size of an effect, and that business decisions should not rest only on whether it passes a threshold, so the memo gives sizes with their range.

- The comparison picks the chart: for a gap, plot the gap.
- Take away the marks that carry nothing; keep the zero line of a bar chart.

- A memo runs decision, context, tension, evidence, recommendation, with sizes and ranges in business words.
- The method and the caveats go in one line below, never before the decision.
