## The question

The owner asks:

> What does a typical order cost, and how much do orders differ from one another?

The total of an order is its lines added up. The first completed orders, from 1,799 in all:

| order_id | store_id | ordered_at | total |
|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 1001 | 2 | 2026-01-01 08:02:00 | 14.00 |
| 1002 | 1 | 2026-01-01 15:38:00 | 12.50 |
| 1003 | 2 | 2026-01-01 15:47:00 | 12.50 |
| 1004 | 1 | 2026-01-01 16:18:00 | 6.50 |

By the end you can answer in two sentences, and say which of your numbers one odd order can move.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** `=AVERAGE(...)` in *Your first formulas* (E2), `AVG(...)` in *Counting and summing: aggregate functions* (S7) and `Series.mean()` give the same number: the mean.

**Diverge.** Two tools can both say standard deviation and not agree. Unit 2 shows where.

</Aside>

### 1. Three centers

**Idea.** The *mean* is the sum divided by the count. The *median* is the middle value once the column is sorted. The *mode* is the value that occurs most often (NIST calls all three "typical values").

**Try it.** The three centers of all the totals:

```python
print(round(totals.mean(), 2), totals.median(), totals.mode().tolist())
```

**Predict:** will the mean of the order totals be above or below the median? And what is the mode?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
12.65 11.75 [4.5]
```

</details>

*Why:* the mean is above the median, and the mode, 4.5, is the single most common total and says nothing about the rest. Orders are bounded below by one cheap item and stretched above by big orders, so the long tail is on the right.

**Try it.** The 11 orders of May 1 hold one extra-zero order of 197.00 (the dataset README names it). Drop that order:

```python
day = sales.loc[sales["ordered_at"].str.startswith("2026-05-01"), "total"]
rest = day.drop(day.idxmax())
print(len(day), round(day.mean(), 2), day.median())
print(round(rest.mean(), 2), rest.median())
print(round(totals.mean() - totals.drop(totals.idxmax()).mean(), 2), totals.median(), totals.drop(totals.idxmax()).median())
```

**Predict:** how far do the mean and the median of the day move when the order is dropped? And how far do the mean and the median of all 1,799 orders move when the same order is dropped?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text

### 2. Three spreads

**Idea.** The *range* is the largest value minus the smallest. The *quartiles* cut the sorted column into four equal parts; the *IQR* (interquartile range) is the third quartile minus the first, the width of the middle half. The *standard deviation* is the square root of the average squared distance from the mean, the variance (NIST; Seeing Theory defines the variance the same way for a random variable).

**Try it.** The three spreads of all the totals:

```python
q1, q3 = totals.quantile(0.25), totals.quantile(0.75)
print(totals.max() - totals.min(), q3 - q1, round(totals.std(), 2))
```

**Predict:** what are the range, the IQR and the standard deviation of the 1,799 totals?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
194.0 9.5 8.62
```

</details>

**Try it.** The same three, without the order of 197.00:

```python
rest = totals.drop(totals.idxmax())
r1, r3 = rest.quantile(0.25), rest.quantile(0.75)
print(rest.max() - rest.min(), r3 - r1, round(rest.std(), 2))
```

**Predict:** which of the three barely moves?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
65.25 9.5 7.44
```

</details>

*Why:* the IQR did not move: it looks only at the middle half. The range looks at two values and moved the most; the standard deviation squares every distance, so it follows the extremes too.

**Try it.** pandas and NumPy each have a standard deviation:

```python
print(round(day.std(), 2), round(np.std(day), 2))
print(round(totals.std(), 4), round(np.std(totals), 4))
```

**Predict:** on the 11 orders of May 1, will `day.std()` and `np.std(day)` agree? And on all 1,799 orders?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
56.33 53.71
8.6161 8.6137
```

</details>

*Why:* pandas divides the sum of squares by n − 1, as NIST does for a sample, and NumPy by n unless you pass `ddof=1`; the pandas page on `std` says `ddof=0` gives NumPy's behavior. The gap is large for 11 orders and small for 1,799. Use `Series.std()` for the rows of a sample; *Samples and the ways they mislead* (C5) says why n − 1.

### 3. Percentiles and the box plot

**Idea.** A *percentile* is a value with that share of the sorted column at or below it, so the 25th percentile has a quarter of the values at or below it. The quartiles are the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. A *box plot* draws a box from the first to the third quartile with a line at the median; its whiskers reach the most extreme values within 1.5 × IQR beyond the box, and each value past the whiskers is a dot (Tukey's rule, the default of matplotlib and the one OpenIntro describes).

**Try it.** Five percentiles of the totals:

```python
print(totals.quantile([0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9]).tolist())
```

**Predict:** what are the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles of the totals?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[4.0, 7.0, 11.75, 16.5, 22.5]
```

</details>

**Try it.** Draw the box plot, and count the dots above the fence at the 75th percentile plus 1.5 times the IQR:

```python
fence = q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1)
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
bp = ax.boxplot(totals)
ax.set_title("Order totals, January to June 2026")
ax.set_ylabel("Order total (USD)")
print(fence, len(bp["fliers"][0].get_ydata()), (totals > fence).sum())
```

**Predict:** where is the fence, and how many dots will there be?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
30.75 50 50
```

### 4. Say it in two sentences

**Idea.** Report a skewed column by its median and the middle half, then say why the mean differs and what deserves a check. One sentence for the center and spread, one for the shape.

**Try it.** Write the summary from variables, so that every number comes from a cell:

```python
q1, med, q3 = totals.quantile([0.25, 0.5, 0.75])
summary = (f"Half of the {len(totals):,} completed orders cost between {q1:.2f} and {q3:.2f} dollars, and the middle order costs {med:.2f}. "
           f"The mean, {totals.mean():.2f}, sits above the median because a few large orders pull it up; the largest, {totals.max():.2f}, is worth a check.")
print(summary)
```

**Predict:** which two numbers will the summary give for the center and the spread of a column like this one?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
Half of the 1,799 completed orders cost between 7.00 and 16.50 dollars, and the middle order costs 11.75. The mean, 12.65, sits above the median because a few large orders pull it up; the largest, 197.00, is worth a check.
```

</details>

*Why:* the median and the two quartiles describe the typical order; the mean is named, with its reason. The largest order is flagged, not deleted: *Distributions, z-scores and outliers* (C3) investigates it.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- The mean follows extreme values; the median does not.
- The range and the standard deviation lean on the extremes; the IQR measures the middle half.
- `Series.std()` divides by n − 1, `np.std` by n.
- A box plot shows the middle half; a dot beyond 1.5 × IQR is unusual, not wrong.
- For a skewed column, give the median with the quartiles, and say why the mean differs.

**Common trap:** reporting the mean of a skewed column alone.

</Aside>

campus = sales[sales["store_id"] == 2]["total"]  # given: the totals of the Campus orders

Use the fence of the box plot: the 75th percentile of the Harbor totals plus 1.5 times the IQR of the Harbor totals, and count the Harbor orders above it. Harbor is store 3. Put the count in `answer_2`. Write the code in the empty cell of the notebook. Expected: `answer_2` is a whole number.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Take the Harbor totals first; every number after that, the quartiles, the fence and the count, comes from them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"One whole number, smaller than the 50 orders above the fence for all three stores."*

```python
harbor = sales[sales["store_id"] == 3]["total"]
h1, h3 = harbor.quantile(0.25), harbor.quantile(0.75)
fence_harbor = ...
```

</details>

<Solution>

```python
harbor = sales[sales["store_id"] == 3]["total"]
h1, h3 = harbor.quantile(0.25), harbor.quantile(0.75)
fence_harbor = h3 + 1.5 * (h3 - h1)
answer_2 = int((harbor > fence_harbor).sum())
```

Harbor's quartiles are 7.50 and 17.00, so its fence is 31.25, a little above the 30.75 of all orders together; 10 of the 355 Harbor orders are above it. Counting the orders of all three stores above that fence gives 44, which the check cell reports.

</Solution>

store_shares = sales.groupby("store_id")["total"].apply(lambda s: (s > 20).mean())  # the colleague's three store shares
