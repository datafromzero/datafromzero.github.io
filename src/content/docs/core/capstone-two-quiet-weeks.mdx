## The question

You run a city bike-share. Next year one third of your bikes must come off the street for two weeks in a row, for a safety refit. Which two weeks do you choose, how many rides and how much revenue will you lose, and what would change your mind?

There is no single right answer. Several decisions earn full marks, and a decision with no reasoning behind it earns none. This is the capstone of the core: real data, an open answer, and AI allowed.

## How this capstone works

**Time.** About 4 hours, 6 at most. At 6 hours, stop and hand in what you have, and say what is missing.

**Any tool.** Excel, SQL with DuckDB, Python, or a mix.

**AI is allowed at every step.** Using it is free; using it without checking is what costs points. You start from a context pack, run the five checks of [*Trust, but verify: checking AI output*](/core/trust-but-verify-ai-output/) (C38) on every output, and log a verdict for each (deliverables 1 and 6).

**What is graded.** The chain from data to decision, not the number at the end, by the eight rubric items below.

**Who marks it.** This site stores nothing and has no accounts: mark yourself with the rubric, or ask a mentor or study partner.

**When to stop.** If the same check fails twice, stop and decide yourself, as in [*What stays human*](/core/what-stays-human/) (C42).

## The brief

### The case

**The operator, the refit and every number about them in this section are invented for this exercise.** The ride data are real: Capital Bikeshare in Washington, DC, for 2011 and 2012.

You are the data analyst at a city bike-share operator. The head of operations asks which two weeks of 2013 to choose for the refit, and what it will cost. The data end on December 31, 2012.

### The rules of the case

Treat these as facts. Where they leave a gap, you decide, and you say what you decided.

1. **Weeks and window.** A week runs Monday to Sunday. The refit takes two consecutive weeks, both entirely inside 2013 between February 1 and November 30.
2. **The event week.** The city's Riverfront Spring Festival runs Monday, March 25 to Sunday, March 31, 2013. No refit week may touch it.
3. **The members' promise.** Members were promised "no more than one week of reduced service per season". A week belongs to the season in which its Monday falls. The seasons of 2013 begin on March 20, June 21, September 22 and December 21. A window whose two weeks fall in the same season breaks the promise. The operator may break it, but the head of operations wants any break named and priced. Finance puts the cost of a break between 8,000 and 25,000 revenue units (RU, rule 4) in lost renewals and cannot narrow it.
4. **Revenue.** A casual (non-member) ride earns about 3 times a member ride. Count a registered ride as 1 RU and a casual ride as 3 RU. Here "registered" means member.
5. **How much is lost.** A pilot showed that taking a third of the bikes off the street loses about 20% of the rides that would otherwise have happened, because riders find another bike. Use 20% for casual and registered rides alike. You may show how your answer changes if the pilot was off.

**Decide:** which two consecutive weeks; how many rides and how many RU the operator should expect to lose, with an honest range; and what would change your decision.

The calendar of 2013 is not in the data. How you line up its weeks with 2011 and 2012 is part of your method, and so is the choice of year or years to forecast from.

## Tools and routes

**Excel.** Use Get Data, then Text/CSV, and choose Transform Data or Load in the Navigator (Microsoft Learn, Text/CSV connector); [*Repeatable cleaning with Power Query*](/excel/power-query-basics/) practices Transform Data. The page warns that type inference can be wrong: check that `dteday` arrives as a date. `ISOWEEKNUM(date)` returns the ISO week number, with weeks that begin on Monday.

**SQL (DuckDB).** `read_csv` works out the configuration and the column types for you. Check what it decided. Run the statements through the `duckdb` package, as in [*Warehouses, data lakes and what things cost*](/core/warehouses-lakes-and-cost/) (C35).

```sql
CREATE VIEW hour AS SELECT * FROM read_csv('hour.csv');
CREATE VIEW day  AS SELECT * FROM read_csv('day.csv');

SELECT count(*) AS hour_rows, min(dteday) AS first_day, max(dteday) AS last_day
FROM hour;
```

The date-part functions `isoyear(date)` and `week(date)` return the ISO year and week; the documentation calls the second "ISO Week".

**Python (pandas).** `pd.read_csv("hour.csv", parse_dates=["dteday"])` parses the date column. `df["dteday"].dt.isocalendar()` returns the ISO year, week and day of each date.

## Rubric

Eight items, scored 2 (full), 1 (partial) or 0 (none), 16 in all. The rubric grades the chain from data to decision: any decision in the reference answer can score 16, and so can another that your reasoning supports.

### 1. The decision and its words

- **Full:** you state the decision, who it is for, and all five rules with a yes or a no for your window; you define in one sentence each the words that move the count: at least a ride, a week and a lost ride ([*Defining a metric before you compute it*](/core/defining-metrics/), C12; C42).
- **Partial:** the decision is stated, but a rule or a definition is missing.
- **None:** you start computing without saying what is decided.

### 2. Grain and model

- **Full:** for each table you use, you say what one row is (hour, day, week) before you use its columns ([*Grain, facts and dimensions*](/core/grain-facts-and-dimensions/), C17); you define the week and say how you lined 2013 up with 2011 and 2012; casual and registered stay separate until you compute RU; one line says why this table or model.
- **Partial:** a sound table, but the grain or the week alignment is unstated.
- **None:** the numbers come from a table whose row you cannot describe.

### 3. Data checks

- **Full:** at least four checks reported, each with what it found and what you did, including "left as it was, because"; at least one counts rows around a step, and at least one reconciles a total a second way (C38).
- **Partial:** one to three checks, or checks with no action.
- **None:** no checks are reported, or the note says the data were clean.

### 4. The forecast basis

- **Full:** you name the base year or years and why; you say how you handle the difference between the levels of 2011 and 2012; and you look at the daily values inside your base weeks for something tied to a date that will not repeat in 2013.
- **Partial:** a base year is chosen, but the level or the special days are not examined.
- **None:** the years are pooled without saying so, or no basis is stated.

### 5. Numbers and ranges

- **Full:** three to five numbers with units, and two of them are the rides and the RU lost; each has a range from a method someone else can repeat (a bootstrap, percentiles of past gaps, a stated scenario for the level; [*Sampling variability and the Central Limit Theorem*](/core/sampling-variability-clt/), C6, and [*Confidence intervals*](/core/confidence-intervals/), C7); one sentence says what the range is a range of and what it leaves out.
- **Partial:** ranges exist but the method is unstated, or rides and RU are mixed.
- **None:** single numbers, or an interval that answers another question, such as a bootstrap of the daily counts given as the range for 2013.

### 6. The chart

- **Full:** bars start at zero, the range is drawn, the axis has units, the title states the takeaway, and the chart makes one point.
- **Partial:** readable, but a range or a unit is missing, or a cut axis is explained.
- **None:** it misleads: a cut axis nobody mentions, two axes, no units.

### 7. The decision and its trade-offs

- **Full:** the decision follows from your numbers; the promise, the festival week and finance's range are named; the price of keeping the promise is set against that range, as [*Choosing a metric: accuracy, precision, recall and error costs*](/core/metrics-and-error-costs/) (C21) prices each error before choosing; the choice is tied to ranges, not to one value; the memo separates what changes the numbers from what changes the decision and names at least two things that would change it.
- **Partial:** a plausible decision, but a rule is unaddressed, the choice rests on one value (such as the middle of finance's range), or what would change it is generic.
- **None:** the decision does not follow from the evidence, or it breaks a rule without saying so.

### 8. AI use, explain-back and reflection

- **Full:** a context pack with its four parts in order; a log row for every AI output that fed a result (asked, output, checks run, verdict, one sentence of reason), your name and what you did not check; at least one revise or reject, or a sentence on why none; an explain-back in your own words that names one number; a reflection that names one specific check.
- **Partial:** a log with verdicts but no reasons or no named checks, or an explain-back that could describe any project.
- **None:** no log, or a list of prompts with no checks.

A total of 13 or more is strong. From 8 to 12 is solid with gaps: redo the items that scored 0 or 1. Below 8, read the reference answer's ground truth and redo the work, not the writing.

Your numbers can differ in the last digits if your conventions differ: lining the weeks up by calendar date (February 4 to 17 in 2012) instead of by ISO week moves the February window's 2012 count from 47,999 to 46,771 rides (-2.6%).

**The table.** The question is about two-week windows, so the table that answers it has one row for each ISO year and week, with rides, casual rides, registered rides, RU (registered plus 3 × casual) and the number of hour rows it holds. **The conventions of this reference.** Weeks are ISO weeks, which start on Monday. In 2013, ISO week 6 begins on Monday, February 4, and ISO week 47 ends on Sunday, November 24, the last week that ends by November 30. By the season rule, the windows whose two weeks fall in different seasons start on March 18, June 17 and September 16. The March one touches the festival, so two keep the promise: **June 17 to 30** and **September 16 to 29**. ### How the range is built

The range is a planning range, not a 95% interval. It has two parts, and only one of them is the kind of thing a confidence interval measures.

1. **The level of 2013.** The data cannot say how long the growth of 2012 lasts; the system may have changed in ways the file does not show. We allow 2013 to be 10% below 2012 and up to 30% above it, a little under half of the +65% of 2012. This is our choice, and a reader may choose another. No interval can measure it from two years: it is a possible shift of the whole base, the kind of error that [*Confidence intervals*](/core/confidence-intervals/) (C7) says an interval does not repair.
2. **Weather and noise.** Compare each two-week window of 2012 that starts in weeks 7 to 44 with the mean of the windows that start two weeks before and two weeks after it: 38 gaps, with a median of 5.6% and a 90th percentile of 13.6% (15.0% for 2011). We use ±14%. Like the percentile range for the next order in C7, it takes the percentiles of past values and assumes no bell shape; the windows overlap, so treat 14% as a planning number and not as 90% coverage.

Multiplying the two gives factors of 0.90 × 0.86 = 0.774 at the low end and 1.30 × 1.14 = 1.482 at the high end. For the February window: 47,999 × 0.774 = 37,200 and 47,999 × 1.482 = 71,100 rides, so 20% of those is 7,400 to 14,200 rides lost. If the pilot's 20% were really 15% or 25%, every loss in the table would scale by 0.75 or 1.25. The range of a gap between two windows applies the same level factors to both windows, and the weather factors either to both (same weather) or to each (independent weather).

**Why 2012 and not an average of both years.** 2013 resembles 2012 much more than 2011: the whole of 2012 had 65% more rides, and pooling would drag the forecast toward a year the system has left behind. The mean of the two years' February windows is 36,900 rides, 23% below the 2012 count, and it lands on the bottom edge of the range as if it were the middle. Scaling 2011 by the yearly ratio of 1.65 gives 42,700, 11% below, which is inside the range and a good cross-check.

**Why not a confidence interval of the days.** A bootstrap of the 14 daily counts of the 2012 window ([*Confidence intervals*](/core/confidence-intervals/), C7, unit 3: 20,000 resamples of 14 days, seed 1; NumPy 2.1.3 and 2.5.3 give the same hundreds) gives about 41,600 to 53,700 rides. It says how much the total of these 14 days would move with another draw of days, which is the sampling variability of C6. It says nothing about the level of 2013, and the same weeks of 2011 had 25,884 rides, far outside it. The uncertain thing is next year, not the draw.

### Pricing the promise

Two numbers decide between a window that breaks the promise and one that keeps it. The first is the price of keeping it: June 17 to 30 loses about 13,600 RU more than February 4 to 17 (10,500 to 20,200 RU). The second is what a break costs, and the case gives only a range: finance puts it between 8,000 and 25,000 RU and cannot narrow it.

The price of keeping it is the break-even. February wins only if a break really costs less than about 13,600 RU, and that value sits inside finance's range, a third of the way up from the bottom. As in C21, write what each choice costs before you choose; then there are several fair readings, each a stance on risk:

- **On average.** If every value in the range were equally likely, a break would cost 16,500 RU on average, about 2,900 RU more than the price of keeping the promise. That is a small edge for June, small next to the spread of both numbers.
- **By chance.** Under the same assumption a break is the cheaper choice only for the third of the range below 13,600 RU, about one time in three.
- **By regret.** Keeping the promise when a break would have cost only 8,000 RU overpays by about 5,600 RU. Breaking it when the cost turns out to be 25,000 RU overpays by about 11,400 RU. To avoid the larger overpayment, keep the promise; to gamble for the saving, break it.

None of these is the one right reading. The memo must say which stance it takes, and it must not replace finance's range by a single value.

### A worked decision

**Decision A: June 17 to 30, keep the promise, and say how close the call is.**

> **To:** Head of operations. **Re:** Refit window for 2013.
>
> **Recommendation.** Refit during June 17 to 30, 2013. This keeps the promise to members: the weeks fall in spring and summer.
>
> **Cost.** In 2012 the same weeks carried about 89,300 rides (124,100 RU). With 20% lost, we should expect to lose about **17,900 rides (plausible range 13,800 to 26,500)** and about **24,800 RU (19,200 to 36,800)**, of which about 3,500 are casual rides.
>
> **Why not February.** February 4 to 17 is cheaper by about 13,600 RU (10,500 to 20,200; 7,700 to 24,200 if the weather differs between the two windows), but both weeks fall in winter and break the promise. Finance prices a break between 8,000 and 25,000 RU. The break-even sits a third of the way up that range, so this is a close call and not a finding. We keep the promise because the average break cost (16,500 RU) is above the break-even and because the larger possible overpayment (about 11,400 RU, against about 5,600) is on the side of breaking. A different view of risk would choose February.
>
> **Risks.** The loss is largest in a warm, dry two weeks, at the top of the range. The 2013 level is the largest unknown: it moves every number but not the ranking of the windows.
>
> **What would change this.** (1) Finance narrowing its range: below about 13,600 RU, choose February 4 to 17; above it, keep June. Asking finance is the cheapest piece of information we can buy. (2) A way to soften a break, such as early notice and a credit, if finance can price it. (3) A pilot measured in another season: ask when it ran, because 20% may not hold in June. (4) A new fixed event in the June weeks.

The decision follows from the numbers, it is tied to the ranges and not to one value, each number has a range, the promise is set against finance's estimate, and the memo separates what changes the numbers from what changes the decision.

**Decision B, just as defensible: Feb 4 to 17, break the promise on purpose, and tell members.** The argument is that the cost of a break lies in the lower part of finance's range, because fewer members are affected: the 2012 February window has about 44,000 registered rides against about 72,000 in June, 39% fewer. This is an argument and not data, so the memo must show what happens at the top of the range: February costs 11,200 RU of lost rides plus a break of up to 25,000 RU, 36,200 in all, against 24,800 RU for June. It must also say that the break-even is 13,600 RU, and what finance would have to say for the choice to hold. A memo that picks February because it is the cheapest window, with no mention of the promise, does not earn full marks.

**Decision C, also defensible: Feb 11 to 24.** This is a planned one-week buffer: if the crew is not ready on February 4, this is where you land. It breaks the promise like Decision B and costs about 800 more rides (1,400 more RU). Starting one, two or three weeks late costs about 800, 900 and 1,900 more rides. The memo must state that trade and reason about the promise as Decision B does.

**What does not earn full marks as it stands:**

- A window in November "because the winter is quiet". By every count in this file it costs more than February (14,900 rides after repairing Thanksgiving, against 9,600) and it breaks the promise just as February does, so the quiet is not a reason. It earns full marks only with a reason the data cannot give, such as a crew or weather risk that the memo states and prices; the case gives none.
- A window that touches the festival week, or that ignores the promise without saying so, or a choice that rests on one value for the break cost.
- One number with no range, a range from an interval of daily counts, averaging both years without saying so, or taking the cheapest weeks of 2012 without looking at why they are cheap.

### The author knew you would use AI

The author of this case knew you would use AI, so this is not a warning: it is the answer key for the places where a shortcut an assistant may take can fail on this data. The author planted nothing. Every trap below is in the file as published, on the pages it comes with, or in the brief as written, and each has a check that catches it. These are shortcuts an assistant may take, not a report on any one assistant. A context pack copied from the Readme carries trap 1 into every prompt and says nothing of traps 5, 8 or 9: write the pack from the files as well.

1. **"Season 1 is spring."** An assistant that relies on the Readme may label it "1:springer". The data say otherwise: season 1 runs from January 1 to March 20 and from December 21 to 31, with a mean of 12.3 °C in the hourly rows, and the UCI page lists it as winter. *Check:* take the first and last date of each season value (C38, check 3).
2. **Predicting rides from casual and registered.** `cnt` equals `casual + registered` in every row, so a model that uses them to predict `cnt` is given the answer ([*Data leakage*](/core/data-leakage/), C20). Computing revenue from them is correct, and that is the one place they belong. *Check:* ask whether you would know the column before the period you forecast, and add the two columns once to see `cnt`.
3. **Quoting 0.5 degrees.** `temp` is divided by 41, so 0.5 is 20.5 °C, and `atemp` is divided by 50, so 0.5 there is 25 °C. A memo that says "mean temperature 0.28 in February" never turned the column back into degrees ([*Data types, units and how data was collected*](/core/study-design-and-data-types/), C1). In the ISO-aligned February window (February 6 to 19, 2012) the mean is 11.6 °C. *Check:* multiply by the Readme's divisor and compare with the weather you know.
4. **Averaging both years without saying so.** It is the quickest forecast, and an assistant may offer it. It understates 2013 by about 23% for the February window, because 2012 had 65% more rides than 2011. *Check:* total the rides by year before you pool anything.
5. **Never noticing the 165 missing hours.** An assistant that sums `cnt` may get the right totals and never mention the gaps; one that averages per row, or "fills the gaps", may invent rides. There are 76 days with fewer than 24 rows, and 2012-10-29 has one. *Check:* count rows per day against 24, and compare the daily sums with `day.csv` (C38, checks 2 and 3).
6. **Trusting the page's row count.** The UCI page says 17389 instances. The file has 17,379 rows. *Check:* count the rows yourself (C38, check 2).
7. **Taking the cheapest weeks at face value.** Sorting 2012 by weekly rides puts the week of Sandy and the week of Thanksgiving among the cheapest. Neither will fall on the same dates in 2013, and November looks cheaper than it is. *Check:* look at the daily values and the `holiday` flag in each cheap week, and in the weeks you keep: the June and September base weeks each hold one odd day.
8. **Treating humidity zero and wind zero as measurements.** All 22 hour rows with `hum` equal to 0 are on 2011-03-10. The 2,180 hour rows of zero wind (12.5%) sit below a gap: the next reading is 6 on the original scale. A weather model that reads them as "calm and dry" may learn from gaps. *Check:* look at the value counts, and ask which values are possible.
9. **Making a category of three rows.** `weathersit` 4 occurs in 3 hour rows. A model with a coefficient for it has no evidence behind that coefficient. *Check:* count rows per category before you model.
10. **A confidence interval of the days.** A bootstrap of the daily counts of two weeks is real arithmetic and answers the wrong question: it says nothing about next year's level, and 2011 sits far outside it. *Check:* ask what the interval is an interval of.
11. **The brief-only rules.** The festival week, the season promise, finance's range for a break, the 3× weight and the 20% loss appear in no public page, so generic advice about "the quietest weeks" may ignore them. An assistant may use one third where the brief says 20%, or use the dataset's `season` column for a promise that the brief defines by date. *Check:* write each rule next to your window with a yes or a no, before you read the AI's answer.
12. **One value for a range.** An assistant may replace finance's 8,000 to 25,000 RU by its middle, 16,500 RU, and announce that the choice is settled. The break-even of 13,600 RU lies inside the range, so the data do not settle it. *Check:* ask which values of the break cost would flip the choice, and say how likely they are.

**What a strong submission does differently.** It runs the checks above and reports them, including the ones that changed nothing. It sets the price of the promise against finance's range instead of letting the lowest number decide, and it ties the choice to the ranges. It separates what moves the numbers from what moves the decision. And its log shows at least one AI output that was revised or rejected, with the reason.
