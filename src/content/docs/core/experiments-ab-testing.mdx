## The question

The product manager of a budgeting app writes:

> Two tests finished this month: `paywall_v2`, a redesigned paywall, and `signup_flow_v2`, a new signup screen. Both are significant on the dashboard. Can I ship both?

In each test a coin, tossed at signup, put the new person in control or in the variant. "Bought" means a purchase of Plus within 14 days. The data are the tests of `app-events`, a fictional app.

| test | control | variant | gap | p |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `paywall_v2` | 12.7% | 15.3% | +2.6 points | 0.005 |
| `signup_flow_v2` | 9.5% | 11.2% | +1.8 points | 0.032 |

By the end you can write the plan of a test before it starts, size it, run the checks that come before any result, and read the result against the rule you wrote first.

### 1. The plan is written before the data

**Idea.** A plan fits on five lines. The *hypothesis* says what changes and for whom. The *primary metric* is the one number the test is judged on. A *guardrail* is a second number that must not get worse. The *randomization unit* is what the coin is tossed for. The *ship rule* says in advance which results mean ship, drop or keep running.

| line | `paywall_v2` |
|---|---|
| hypothesis | The redesigned paywall makes more new people buy Plus |
| primary metric | bought Plus within 14 days of signing up |
| guardrail | of the buyers, the share who cancel within 30 days |
| randomization unit | the person, one coin toss at signup |
| ship rule | **ship** if the lift is at least 2 points, its interval stays above 0 and the guardrail's interval stays below +5 points; **drop** if even the top of the lift's interval is below 2 points; otherwise **keep running** |

The bar of 2 points, the limit of 5 points and the rule are assumptions of this example: in a real test the people who pay for the change set them.

**Try it:** check what the plan relies on, that each person is tossed once and at signup, and that the person is the right unit.

```python
print(int(assign.groupby("user_id").size().max()), assign["user_id"].nunique(), len(assign))      # most rows for one person, people, rows
lag = assign.merge(users[["user_id", "signup_ts"]], on="user_id", validate="many_to_one")
seconds = (lag["assigned_at"] - lag["signup_ts"]).dt.total_seconds()                                  # from signup to the coin toss
print(int(seconds.min()), int(seconds.max()))
pw = assign[assign["experiment"] == "paywall_v2"]
views = events[events["event_name"].isin(["view_paywall", "paywall_viewed"]) & events["user_id"].isin(pw["user_id"])]
views = views.drop_duplicates(["user_id", "event_ts"])                                                # a client retry wrote some events twice
per_person = views.groupby("user_id").size()                                                          # paywall views of each person who saw it
print(len(pw), len(per_person), int((per_person >= 2).sum()), round(float((per_person >= 2).mean()), 3))
```

**Predict:** `paywall_v2` enrolled 5,419 people. Of those who saw the paywall at least once, what share saw it more than once: about 1 in 20, 1 in 5 or 1 in 2?

*Why:* the 15,868 rows belong to 15,868 different people, and the coin was tossed 1 to 3 seconds after signup, before anything else could happen. So the person is the unit: Kohavi and colleagues prefer randomizing by user so that the user gets a consistent experience throughout the test.

### 2. How many people, and for how long

**Idea.** The *minimum detectable effect* (MDE) is the smallest true lift the test is built to catch. It comes from the decision, the smallest lift worth shipping, and not from the data. Power, from C8, is the chance of catching it. For two shares the people needed in each group follow from four inputs: the baseline rate, the MDE, the bar for p and the power. Divide the total by the people enrolled each day to get days, then round up to whole weeks: Kohavi and colleagues advise a week or two, then multiples of a week, so that day-of-week effects can be analyzed.

**Try it:** the baseline is 12.4 percent, an assumption. Find the people each group needs for a true lift of 1, 2 and 3 points at power 0.80 and the bar 0.05, then check what the test as run could do.

```python
def people_per_group(p0, lift, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """People needed in EACH group to catch a true lift of `lift` (0.02 means 2 points) from a baseline `p0`."""
    p_bar = p0 + lift / 2                                           # the average of the baseline and the baseline plus the lift
    z_alpha = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)                         # 1.96 for a two-sided bar of 0.05
    z_power = stats.norm.ppf(power)                                 # 0.84 for 80 percent power
    return int(np.ceil(2 * p_bar * (1 - p_bar) * (z_alpha + z_power) ** 2 / lift ** 2))


baseline = 0.124                                                    # an assumption: the share who buy Plus within 14 days, as in lesson C8
days_run = (pw["assigned_at"].max().normalize() - pw["assigned_at"].min().normalize()).days + 1
per_day = len(pw) / days_run                                        # people paywall_v2 enrolled each day
print(days_run, round(float(per_day), 1))
for lift in (0.01, 0.02, 0.03):
    n = people_per_group(baseline, lift)
    days = 2 * n / per_day
    print(f"lift +{100 * lift:.0f}: {n:,} per group, {2 * n:,} in all, {days:.0f} days, {int(np.ceil(days / 7))} whole weeks")
print(round(16 * baseline * (1 - baseline) / 0.02 ** 2))            # the rule of thumb 16 * variance / lift squared, for 2 points
```

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
runs = 100_000
n_control, n_variant = int((pw["variant"] == "control").sum()), int((pw["variant"] == "variant").sum())
for lift in (0.01, 0.02, 0.03):                                     # the true lift of each simulated world
    buyers0 = rng.binomial(n_control, baseline, size=runs)
    buyers1 = rng.binomial(n_variant, baseline + lift, size=runs)
    caught = two_prop_p(buyers0, n_control, buyers1, n_variant) < 0.05
    print(f"lift +{100 * lift:.0f}: {n_control:,} and {n_variant:,} people catch it in {caught.mean():.2f} of {runs:,} tests")
n_plan = people_per_group(baseline, 0.02)
buyers0 = rng.binomial(n_plan, baseline, size=runs)
buyers1 = rng.binomial(n_plan, baseline + 0.02, size=runs)
print(n_plan, round(float((two_prop_p(buyers0, n_plan, buyers1, n_plan) < 0.05).mean()), 2))
```

**Predict:** the plan says 2 points. A stricter bar is the 1 point of C9's example: about how many people would each group need to catch a true lift of exactly 1 point four times in five, about 5,000, 10,000 or 20,000? And `paywall_v2` had 2,706 and 2,713 people: if the true lift had been exactly 2 points, in about how many of 100 such tests would p come out below 0.05?

The simulation agrees with the plan: at 4,555 in each group, 0.80 of the tests catch a 2-point lift. The test as run, 8 weeks, reached p below 0.05 in 0.58 of the tests for a true lift of 2 points, and in 0.89 for 3 points. The simulated shares are for seed 14 and 100,000 tests each; another NumPy version can move the last digit.

The size of the test is a decision made before launch: a bigger MDE, a longer run, or a test that may miss.

In symbols:

$$
n \approx \frac{2\,\bar{p}\,(1-\bar{p})\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{\delta^2}
$$

where $$n$$ is the people in each group, $$\delta$$ the MDE as a share (0.02 for 2 points), $$\bar{p}$$ the average of the baseline and the baseline plus $$\delta$$ (0.134), and the two $$z$$ values the multipliers for the bar $$\alpha = 0.05$$ (1.96) and the power $$1 - \beta = 0.80$$ (0.84). Then $$n \approx 2 \times 0.134 \times 0.866 \times 2.80^2 / 0.02^2 \approx 4{,}555$$. Kohavi and colleagues give the rule of thumb $$n = 16\sigma^2/\Delta^2$$ for 95 percent confidence and 80 percent power, with $$\sigma^2$$ the variance of the metric, which is $$p(1-p)$$ for a share; it gives 4,345 here, a little lower because it uses the baseline's variance alone.

### 3. Count the groups before you read anything

**Idea.** A fair coin gives each person a chance of one half of each group, so the two counts differ only by luck. A *sample ratio mismatch* (SRM) is a split that luck does not explain: the groups are not the ones the plan describes. The check is a chi-square test of the counts against the planned split, run before any result is read. The Microsoft Research page on SRM names an alarm bar of p < 0.0005 and calls it deliberately strict; this lesson uses it.

**Try it:** count the people in each group of the three experiments and test the counts against a fair 50/50 split.

```python
for name, g in assign.groupby("experiment"):
    n_control = int((g["variant"] == "control").sum())
    n_variant = int((g["variant"] == "variant").sum())
    srm = stats.chisquare([n_control, n_variant])                   # observed counts against equal counts, the default
    print(name, n_control, n_variant, round(float(srm.statistic), 2), round(float(srm.pvalue), 4))
```

**Predict:** for which of the three experiments will the split look wrong, and what will its p-value be: about 0.3, about 0.01 or below 0.001?

$$
\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} = \frac{(2878 - 2740.5)^2}{2740.5} + \frac{(2603 - 2740.5)^2}{2740.5} \approx 13.8
$$

where $$O$$ is an observed count and $$E$$ the expected one. With two groups that gives p = 0.0002, below the bar of 0.0005. The other two splits are fine, with p of 0.92 and 0.32.

`stats.chisquare` takes counts and expects equal counts unless told otherwise, and its documentation warns that it is invalid when counts are very small, which is not the case here. A mismatch voids the test: do not read its result, find the cause.

### 6. The readout against the rule

**Idea.** The readout answers in order: was the split fair, what is the lift and does it last, did the guardrail move, and what does the rule written first say. The rule reads intervals, not a significance label: the American Statistical Association says that business decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a threshold. A guardrail needs the same care. "Not significant" says little about a small group of buyers; the question is whether its interval rules out a harm the team cannot accept.

**Try it:** the guardrail is the share of buyers who canceled within 30 days of starting. Compute its gap and interval for `paywall_v2`, then apply the rule of unit 1.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Write the plan before the data: hypothesis, primary metric, guardrail, randomization unit and ship rule.
- Halving the lift you want to catch needs four times the people; turn people into whole weeks.
- Count the groups first: a split that luck does not explain (here p < 0.0005) voids the test, so find who is missing.
- Cut the lift by week: a bump in the first week overstates what lasts.
- Read the primary metric and the guardrail as intervals against the rule written first.

**Common trap:** reading the dashboard's win before counting the groups: 9.5 against 11.2 percent, p = 0.032, from a test whose true effect is zero.

</Aside>
