## The question

The product manager of a budgeting app writes:

> Two tests finished this month: `paywall_v2`, a redesigned paywall, and `signup_flow_v2`, a new signup screen. Both are significant on the dashboard. Can I ship both?

In each test a coin, tossed at signup, put the new person in control or in the variant. "Bought" means a purchase of Plus within 14 days. The data are the tests of `app-events`, a fictional app.

| test | control | variant | gap | p |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `paywall_v2` | 12.7% | 15.3% | +2.6 points | 0.005 |
| `signup_flow_v2` | 9.5% | 11.2% | +1.8 points | 0.032 |

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Hypothesis tests and p-values, honestly*](/core/hypothesis-testing/) (C8) you saw that power grows with people and that every extra look adds a chance of a false alarm. In [*Comparing two groups: tests and effect sizes*](/core/comparing-groups/) (C9) you wrote the verdict for `paywall_v2`. Here the same numbers go into a plan made before the data.

**Diverge.** C8 and C9 assumed that the coin was fair and that one number covered the whole run. Units 3 to 5 test both.

</Aside>

### 1. The plan is written before the data

**Idea.** A plan fits on five lines. The *hypothesis* says what changes and for whom. The *primary metric* is the one number the test is judged on. A *guardrail* is a second number that must not get worse. The *randomization unit* is what the coin is tossed for. The *ship rule* says in advance which results mean ship, drop or keep running.

| line | `paywall_v2` |
|---|---|
| hypothesis | The redesigned paywall makes more new people buy Plus |
| primary metric | bought Plus within 14 days of signing up |
| guardrail | of the buyers, the share who cancel within 30 days |
| randomization unit | the person, one coin toss at signup |
| ship rule | **ship** if the lift is at least 2 points, its interval stays above 0 and the guardrail's interval stays below +5 points; **drop** if even the top of the lift's interval is below 2 points or the bottom of the guardrail's interval is already above +5; otherwise **keep running** |

The bar of 2 points, the limit of 5 points and the rule are assumptions of this example: in a real test the people who pay for the change set them.

**Try it:** check what the plan relies on: that each person is tossed once, and at signup.

```python
print(int(assign.groupby("user_id").size().max()), assign["user_id"].nunique(), len(assign))      # most rows for one person, people, rows
lag = assign.merge(users[["user_id", "signup_ts"]], on="user_id", validate="many_to_one")
seconds = (lag["assigned_at"] - lag["signup_ts"]).dt.total_seconds()                                  # from signup to the coin toss
print(int(seconds.min()), int(seconds.max()))
pw = assign[assign["experiment"] == "paywall_v2"]
print(len(pw))                                                                                         # people in paywall_v2
```

**Predict:** what is the most rows one person has in the experiment table, and how long after signup is the coin tossed: within 1 second, 1 to 3 seconds or minutes?

*Why:* the 15,868 rows belong to 15,868 different people, one row each, and the coin was tossed 1 to 3 seconds after signup, before anything else could happen. A person can see the paywall more than once, so a coin tossed at every view could show them both versions, and "bought within 14 days" belongs to the person. So the person is the unit: Kohavi and colleagues prefer randomizing by user so that the user gets a consistent experience throughout the test.

### 2. How many people, and for how long

**Idea.** The *minimum detectable effect* (MDE) is the smallest true lift the test is built to catch. It comes from the decision, the smallest lift worth shipping, and not from the data. Power, from C8, is the chance of catching it. For two shares the people needed in each group follow from four inputs: the baseline rate, the MDE, the bar for p and the power. Divide the total by the people enrolled each day to get days, then round up to whole weeks: Kohavi and colleagues advise a week or two, then multiples of a week, so that day-of-week effects can be analyzed. The outcome "bought within 14 days" also needs 14 days after the last signup.

**Try it:** the baseline is 12.4 percent, an assumption. Find the people each group needs for a true lift of 1, 2 and 3 points at power 0.80 and the bar 0.05, then check what the test as run could do.

```python
def people_per_group(p0, lift, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """People needed in EACH group to catch a true lift of `lift` (0.02 means 2 points) from a baseline `p0`."""
    p_bar = p0 + lift / 2                                           # the average of the baseline and the baseline plus the lift
    z_alpha = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)                         # 1.96 for a two-sided bar of 0.05
    z_power = stats.norm.ppf(power)                                 # 0.84 for 80 percent power
    return int(np.ceil(2 * p_bar * (1 - p_bar) * (z_alpha + z_power) ** 2 / lift ** 2))


baseline = 0.124                                                    # an assumption: the share who buy Plus within 14 days, as in lesson C8
days_run = (pw["assigned_at"].max().normalize() - pw["assigned_at"].min().normalize()).days + 1
per_day = len(pw) / days_run                                        # people paywall_v2 enrolled each day
print(days_run, round(float(per_day), 1))
for lift in (0.01, 0.02, 0.03):
    n = people_per_group(baseline, lift)
    days = 2 * n / per_day
    print(f"lift +{100 * lift:.0f}: {n:,} per group, {2 * n:,} in all, {days:.0f} days, {int(np.ceil(days / 7))} whole weeks")
```

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
runs = 100_000
n_control, n_variant = int((pw["variant"] == "control").sum()), int((pw["variant"] == "variant").sum())
for lift in (0.01, 0.02, 0.03):                                     # the true lift of each simulated world
    buyers0 = rng.binomial(n_control, baseline, size=runs)
    buyers1 = rng.binomial(n_variant, baseline + lift, size=runs)
    caught = two_prop_p(buyers0, n_control, buyers1, n_variant) < 0.05
    print(f"lift +{100 * lift:.0f}: {n_control:,} and {n_variant:,} people catch it in {caught.mean():.2f} of {runs:,} tests")
```

**Predict:** the plan says 2 points. A stricter bar is the 1 point of C9's example: about how many people would each group need to catch a true lift of exactly 1 point four times in five, about 5,000, 10,000 or 20,000? And `paywall_v2` had 2,706 and 2,713 people: if the true lift had been exactly 2 points, in about how many of 100 such tests would p come out below 0.05?

```text
lift +1: 17,638 per group, 35,276 in all, 365 days, 53 whole weeks
lift +2: 4,555 per group, 9,110 in all, 94 days, 14 whole weeks

lift +1: 2,706 and 2,713 people catch it in 0.19 of 100,000 tests
lift +2: 2,706 and 2,713 people catch it in 0.58 of 100,000 tests
```

*Why:* a lift of 2 points needs 4,555 people in each group, 94 days at 96.8 people a day: 14 whole weeks of signups, then 2 more weeks for the last signup to have its 14 days. A lift of 1 point needs 17,638 in each group, four times as many, and 53 weeks: halving the lift quadruples the people.

The test as run, 8 weeks, reached p below 0.05 in 0.58 of the tests for a true lift of 2 points, and in 0.89 for 3 points. The simulated shares are for seed 14 and 100,000 tests each; another NumPy version can move the last digit. The size of a test is a decision made before launch: a bigger MDE, a longer run, or a test that may miss.

In symbols:

$$
n \approx \frac{2\,\bar{p}\,(1-\bar{p})\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{\delta^2}
$$

where $$n$$ is the people in each group, $$\delta$$ the MDE as a share, $$\bar{p}$$ the average of the baseline and the baseline plus $$\delta$$ (0.134), and the $$z$$ values the multipliers for the bar $$\alpha = 0.05$$ and the power $$1 - \beta = 0.80$$ (1.96 and 0.84). Then $$n \approx 2 \times 0.134 \times 0.866 \times 2.80^2 / 0.02^2 \approx 4{,}555$$. The formula assumes equal groups, a z-test of two shares with large counts, a known baseline, one look at the planned size and steady enrollment.

### 3. Count the groups before you read anything

**Idea.** A fair coin gives each person a chance of one half of each group, so the two counts differ only by luck. A *sample ratio mismatch* (SRM) is a split that luck does not explain: the groups are not the ones the plan describes. The check is a chi-square test of the counts against the planned split, run before any result is read. The Microsoft Research page on SRM names an alarm bar of p < 0.0005 and calls it conservative, to avoid false alarms; this lesson uses it.

**Try it:** count the people in each group of the three experiments and test the counts against a fair 50/50 split.

```python
for name, g in assign.groupby("experiment"):
    n_control = int((g["variant"] == "control").sum())
    n_variant = int((g["variant"] == "variant").sum())
    srm = stats.chisquare([n_control, n_variant])                   # observed counts against equal counts, the default
    print(name, n_control, n_variant, round(float(srm.statistic), 2), round(float(srm.pvalue), 4))
```

**Predict:** for which of the three experiments will the split look wrong, and what will its p-value be: about 0.3, about 0.01 or below 0.001?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
paywall_v2 2706 2713 0.01 0.9242
signup_flow_v2 2878 2603 13.8 0.0002
welcome_tips 2449 2519 0.99 0.3206
```

</details>

*Why:* only `signup_flow_v2` fails: 2,878 and 2,603 people, where a fair coin would give about 2,740.5 each. The statistic adds up how far each count is from its expected value:

$$
\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} = \frac{(2878 - 2740.5)^2}{2740.5} + \frac{(2603 - 2740.5)^2}{2740.5} \approx 13.8
$$

where $$O$$ is an observed count and $$E$$ the expected one. With two groups that gives p = 0.0002, below the bar of 0.0005. The other two splits are fine, with p of 0.92 and 0.32.

`stats.chisquare` takes counts and expects equal counts unless told otherwise. A mismatch voids the test: do not read its result, find the cause.

### 4. Who is missing

**Idea.** A mismatch has a cause, and the usual one is that people are lost on the way to the assignment table, so the groups lose different people. To find them, compare everyone who could have been enrolled, here the signups of the test window in `users`, with the experiment table, and ask what the missing people have in common.

**Try it:** list the people who signed up while `signup_flow_v2` ran and have no row in the experiment table.

### 5. Does the lift last

**Idea.** Kohavi and colleagues write that newness and its opposite, primacy, imply that "some experiments need to be run for multiple weeks". The check is to cut the lift by the week of signup and to test the difference of the two gaps, not only to look at them. Their other remedy, computing the metric for new users only, does not apply here: everyone in `paywall_v2` is a new signup.

**Try it:** split `paywall_v2` by signup week into week 1 (January 12 to 18) and weeks 2 to 8, compute the gap and its interval in each part, then test the difference of the two gaps.

```python

tables = {}
for label, g in parts.items():
    t = gap_table(g.loc[g["variant"] == "control", "converted"], g.loc[g["variant"] == "variant", "converted"])
    tables[label] = t
    print(f"{label}: {t['n0']:,} and {t['n1']:,} people, {100 * t['rate0']:.1f}% and {100 * t['rate1']:.1f}%, gap {100 * t['gap']:+.1f} points ({100 * t['low']:.1f} to {100 * t['high']:.1f}), p = {t['p']:.3f}")
first, later = tables["week 1"], tables["weeks 2 to 8"]
```

### 6. The readout against the rule

**Idea.** The readout answers in order: was the split fair, what is the lift and does it last, did the guardrail move, and what does the rule written first say. The rule reads intervals, not a significance label: the American Statistical Association says that business decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a threshold. For a guardrail the question is whether its interval rules out a harm the team cannot accept. This guardrail is measured among buyers, who are chosen after the coin by the test itself, so a rise or a fall can come partly from who buys.

**Try it:** the guardrail is the share of buyers who canceled within 30 days of starting. Compute its gap and interval for `paywall_v2`, then apply the rule of unit 1.

```python
latest = subs.sort_values("updated_at").groupby("subscription_id").tail(1)                    # the current row of each subscription

def verdict(lift, lift_low, lift_high, cancel_low, cancel_high, bar=0.02, limit=0.05):
    """The rule written before launch: ship, drop or keep running."""
    if lift_high < bar or cancel_low > limit:
        return "drop"                                               # even the best case is below the bar, or the harm is already clear
    if lift >= bar and lift_low > 0 and cancel_high < limit:
        return "ship"
    return "keep running"


main = tables["all eight weeks"]
```

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Write the plan before the data: hypothesis, primary metric, guardrail, randomization unit, ship rule.
- Halving the lift you want to catch needs four times the people; turn people into whole weeks and add the follow-up.
- Count the groups first: a split that luck does not explain (here p < 0.0005) voids the test.
- Cut the lift by signup week; call a change a fade only after testing the difference of the two gaps.
- Read the primary metric and the guardrail as intervals against the rule written first.

**Common trap:** reading the dashboard's win before counting the groups: 9.5 against 11.2 percent, p = 0.032, from a test whose true effect is zero.

</Aside>
