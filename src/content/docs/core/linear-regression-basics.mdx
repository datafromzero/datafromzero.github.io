## The question

The owner of the cafes asks:

> When an order has one more item in it, how many dollars does its total grow by? And what can that tell us about orders we have not seen?

An order's *units* are the sum of the quantities of its lines; its total is in dollars. The first completed orders, from 1,799 in all:

| order_id | units | total |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1001 | 4 | 14.00 |
| 1002 | 2 | 12.50 |
| 1003 | 3 | 12.50 |
| 1004 | 2 | 6.50 |

By the end you can read a fitted line in dollars, say what its misses show, say what R² means and does not, hold a second thing fixed, and say where a line stops being safe.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In *Correlation is not causation* (C10) you compared a gap inside each channel. A line with a second variable makes that comparison for you: its coefficient is read holding the other variable fixed.

**Diverge.** A line is fitted to the rows you have. Unit 5 shows what it does where it has no rows.

</Aside>

### 1. A line in the units of the data

**Idea.** A *fitted line* predicts y from x. Its *slope* is how much the prediction rises when x rises by 1, in units of y per unit of x. Its *intercept* is the prediction at x = 0, in units of y. *Least squares* picks the line whose squared misses add up to the smallest sum (NIST: it minimizes "the sum of the squared deviations between the data and the model"). NumPy's `polyfit(x, y, 1)` fits it and returns the slope first.

**Try it.** Fit the total of an order on its units, over all 1,799 completed orders:

```python
units, total = sales["units"], sales["total"]
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(units, total, 1)
print(round(slope, 2), round(intercept, 2))
print(round(intercept + slope * 3, 2), round(total[units == 3].mean(), 2), units.min())
sxy = ((units - units.mean()) * (total - total.mean())).sum()
sxx = ((units - units.mean()) ** 2).sum()
print(round(sxy, 2), round(sxx, 2), round(units.mean(), 4), round(total.mean(), 4), round(sxy / sxx, 4))
```

**Predict:** Fit the line of the order total on the number of units, over all 1,799 orders. How many dollars will each extra unit add (the slope), and what total will the line give at 0 units (the intercept)?

For 3 units the line says 13.28 and the real 3-unit orders average 13.11: close, not equal, because a line gives an average, not each order.

In symbols, the fitted line is $$\hat{y} = a + b\,x$$, where $$\hat{y}$$ ("y hat") is the predicted total in dollars, $$x$$ the units, $$b$$ the slope in dollars per unit and $$a$$ the intercept in dollars. Least squares gives them from the data, with $$x_i$$ and $$y_i$$ the units and the total of order $$i$$, $$n$$ orders, and $$\bar{x}$$ and $$\bar{y}$$ the means:

$$
\begin{gathered}
b = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2} \\
a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}
\end{gathered}
$$

The top of $$b$$ is the sum that made the correlation of C10 (units and dollars move together); the bottom is how much the units spread. With the sums and means printed above:

$$
\begin{gathered}
b = \frac{21{,}795.84}{4{,}509.04} \approx 4.83 \\
a = 12.6455 - 4.8338 \times 2.8688 \approx -1.22
\end{gathered}
$$

### 2. Residuals

**Idea.** A *residual* is the actual total minus the line's prediction, in dollars. A positive residual is an order that cost more than the line says. Residuals show what a line misses: a good fit leaves misses with no pattern, and a miss that stands out is an order to look at (NIST).

**Try it.** The smallest and largest residual of this line, and what they add up to:

```python
residual = total - (intercept + slope * units)
worst = sales.assign(residual=residual).sort_values("residual")
print(round(residual.min(), 2), round(residual.max(), 2), abs(round(residual.sum(), 6)))
top = worst.tail(1)
print(top[["order_id", "store_id", "units", "total", "residual"]].round(2).to_string(index=False))
print(round(float(intercept + slope * top["units"].iloc[0]), 2))
```

### 3. R-squared

**Idea.** *R²* ("R squared") says how much of the spread of y around its mean the line accounts for: one minus the squared misses of the line divided by the squared distances of y from its mean. 0 means the line does no better than the mean, 1 means no misses. It has no units. For a line with one x it equals the square of the correlation $$r$$ of C10 (SciPy's page for `linregress` says that the square of `rvalue` is the coefficient of determination).

**Try it.** R² for the 1,798 orders:

```python
ss_res = (miss ** 2).sum()
ss_tot = ((clean["total"] - clean["total"].mean()) ** 2).sum()
print(round(ss_res, 1), round(ss_tot, 1), round(1 - ss_res / ss_tot, 3))
```

**Predict:** R² compares the squared misses of the line with the squared distances from the mean total. Will it be close to 0.2, 0.5 or 0.8 for the 1,798 orders?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
19792.6 99474.0 0.801
```

</details>

*Why:* 0.801: the line accounts for about 80% of the squared spread of the totals around their mean. It does not say that the line is 80% right, that units cause the total, or that the line suits every order; NIST warns that a high R² does not guarantee that the model fits the data well.

In symbols, with $$e_i$$ the residuals and $$\bar{y}$$ the mean total:

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2} = 1 - \frac{19{,}792.6}{99{,}474.0} \approx 0.801
$$

**Try it.** The same line, fitted to small orders only:

```python
small = clean[clean["units"] <= 2]
slope3, intercept3 = np.polyfit(small["units"], small["total"], 1)
r2_small = np.corrcoef(small["units"], small["total"])[0, 1] ** 2
print(len(small), round(slope3, 2), round(r2_small, 3))
```

**Predict:** Now fit the line only to orders of 1 or 2 units. Will the slope change a lot, and will R² stay the same?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text

```

</details>

*Why:* the slope barely moves (4.5 against 4.41), yet R² falls from 0.801 to 0.546. R² depends on how spread out x is in the sample as well as on the relationship: the same price rule looks "less explained" among small orders. Do not compare R² between samples with different ranges as if it measured the strength of one rule.

### 4. Holding the other fixed

**Idea.** A line can have more than one x: $$\hat{y} = a + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2$$. Each coefficient is read holding the other variable fixed: it is the change in the prediction when its own variable rises by 1 and the other stays where it is. NumPy's `linalg.lstsq` fits it from a table with a column of ones for the intercept.

**Try it.** Add the units of food in the order as a second variable, $$x_2$$, next to the units $$x_1$$:

```python
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(clean)), clean["units"], clean["food_units"]])
coef = np.linalg.lstsq(X, clean["total"], rcond=None)[0]
print([round(float(c), 2) for c in coef])
miss2 = clean["total"] - X @ coef
print(round(1 - (miss2 ** 2).sum() / ss_tot, 3))
print(round(float(coef @ [1, 3, 0]), 2), round(float(coef @ [1, 3, 1]), 2))
```

**Predict:** Add the units of food in the order as a second variable. Will the coefficient on units stay near 4.41 or move? And how many dollars will one more food unit add, holding the number of units fixed?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[-0.01, 4.0, 3.0]
0.9
11.99 14.99
```

</details>

*Why:* the coefficients are −0.01 for the intercept, 4.00 for units and 3.00 for food units, and R² rises to 0.9 from 0.801. Holding the units fixed, one food unit in place of another kind of unit adds 3.00 dollars (a 3-unit order with no food unit is predicted at 11.99, with one food unit at 14.99). The slope of units fell from 4.41 to 4.00: food units are counted in the units too, so the first line gave the units credit for part of what food was doing.

**Try it.** Look the coefficients up in the menu:

**Predict:** Check the two coefficients against the menu: what does the average unit cost after discounts, for units that are not food and for units that are food?

<details>


</details>

*Why:* the average unit that is not food cost 4.0 dollars after discounts, as the coefficient says, and the average food unit 6.97, which is the 4.0 plus about 3: the fit found the menu again. A coefficient with a meaning like this one can be checked; most real ones cannot, which is why "holding the other fixed" describes the model and is not an experiment: the regression compares orders, it does not change them.

### 5. Extrapolation

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- The slope is in units of y per unit of x; the intercept can sit where no real case does.
- A residual is actual minus predicted: look at the biggest one and at the pattern of all of them.
- R² is the share of squared spread the line accounts for. It is not accuracy, not cause, and it moves with the range of x.
- A coefficient read "holding the other fixed" describes the fit; the regression compares orders, it does not change them.
- Do not carry a line beyond the data it saw.

**Common trap:** reading a high R² or a clean coefficient as proof that the line is right outside the data, or that x causes y.

</Aside>
