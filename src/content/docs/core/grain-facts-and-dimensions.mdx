### 1. Choose the grain

**Idea.** The *grain* of a fact table says what one row stands for, and it comes before the columns. A *fact table* holds the numbers an event produced, one row for each event. Kimball Group's page on grain says that different grains must not be mixed in the same fact table. Choose the finest grain the questions need.

**Try it.** Build the sales at three grains: one row for each line, for each order and for each cafe and month. The second and third are added up from the first.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE sales_line AS
SELECT i.order_item_id, i.order_id, o.store_id, CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE) AS order_date,
       i.product_id, i.quantity,
       i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - i.discount_usd AS amount_usd
FROM order_items AS i
JOIN orders AS o ON o.order_id = i.order_id
JOIN products AS p ON p.product_id = i.product_id;

CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE sales_order AS
SELECT order_id, store_id, order_date, COUNT(*) AS lines, SUM(quantity) AS units, SUM(amount_usd) AS amount_usd
FROM sales_line
GROUP BY order_id, store_id, order_date;

CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE sales_store_month AS
SELECT store_id, month(order_date) AS month, COUNT(*) AS orders, SUM(units) AS units, SUM(amount_usd) AS amount_usd
FROM sales_order
GROUP BY store_id, month(order_date);

SELECT 'built' AS note;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column: the note `built`.

**Try it.** The three tables side by side:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT 'line' AS grain, COUNT(*) AS rows, ROUND(SUM(amount_usd), 2) AS sales FROM sales_line
UNION ALL SELECT 'order', COUNT(*), ROUND(SUM(amount_usd), 2) FROM sales_order
UNION ALL SELECT 'store-month', COUNT(*), ROUND(SUM(amount_usd), 2) FROM sales_store_month;`} />

**Predict:** how many rows will each of the three tables have?

Expected: 3 rows, 3 columns.

*Why:* 3,287, 1,800 and 15 rows, and the same 22,754.25 dollars in each (the canceled order and order 2075 included). Adding up lost no money, because units and sales are measures that add: the sum of the parts is the whole. What it lost is every distinction that the coarser grain does not name.

**Try it.** The owner's second question, which two products are bought together most often, from the line table. Two lines of the same order, a pair, are matched with a self-join:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT pa.product_name AS first_product, pb.product_name AS second_product, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM sales_line AS a
JOIN sales_line AS b ON b.order_id = a.order_id AND b.product_id > a.product_id
JOIN products AS pa ON pa.product_id = a.product_id
JOIN products AS pb ON pb.product_id = b.product_id
GROUP BY pa.product_name, pb.product_name
ORDER BY orders DESC, first_product, second_product
LIMIT 3;`} />

**Predict:** will the most common pair be two drinks, or a drink with something to eat?

Expected: 3 rows, 3 columns.

*Why:* Americano with Latte comes first, in 51 orders, then Latte with Cappuccino and Latte with Cold Brew, 49 each. The best-selling pair is two drinks. Neither coarser table has a product column, so this question has nothing to stand on in them: the detail was added up, and a sum cannot be split back into the values it came from. The first question, orders for each cafe and month, can be answered from all three tables. The line grain answers both, which is why you start from the finest grain the questions need.

### 2. Measures and descriptions

**Idea.** An event leaves two kinds of column in a table. A *measure* is a number the event produced, which you add up or average over many rows. A *description* says who, what, where or when: the cafe, the product, the customer, the day. Kimball Group's pages say that a fact table holds numeric measures and keys to its dimensions, and that queries filter and group by the attributes of a *dimension table*. A column that only names the row, such as `order_item_id`, is a key. Ask of each numeric column: does adding it up over many rows answer a question?

**Try it.** Add up four numbers of the lines, with the price of each line's product:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT ROUND(SUM(p.unit_price_usd), 2) AS sum_of_prices,
       SUM(i.quantity) AS units,
       ROUND(SUM(i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd), 2) AS gross,
       ROUND(SUM(i.discount_usd), 2) AS discounts,
       ROUND(SUM(i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - i.discount_usd), 2) AS sales
FROM order_items AS i
JOIN products AS p ON p.product_id = i.product_id;`} />

**Predict:** which of the numbers is the sales of the cafes, and what does the sum of the prices measure?

Expected: 1 row, 5 columns.

*Why:* 14,453.25 for the sum of the prices, 5,162 units, 22,838.25 gross, 84.00 in discounts and 22,754.25 in sales, for all 1,800 orders: the canceled order and order 2075 are in it. Quantity, discount and amount are the measures. The sum of the prices measures nothing: it adds the price of a product once for every line and ignores how many units were bought. The price describes the product. The amount of a line is the measure: quantity times price, less the discount. This dataset holds one price for each product, so the amount could be rebuilt from the product table; a shop whose menu prices change stores the amount with the event, so that an old sale keeps the price it was paid at.

### 3. Counting events at the grain of a line

**Idea.** At the line grain, a row is a line, not an order. The order id stays in the fact table as a key with no table of its own: Kimball Group call it a *degenerate dimension*; their example is an invoice number on the line items of an invoice.

**Try it.** The lines of mobile orders, and the mobile orders:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS mobile_lines,
       COUNT(DISTINCT i.order_id) AS mobile_orders
FROM order_items AS i
JOIN orders AS o ON o.order_id = i.order_id
WHERE o.payment_method = 'mobile';`} />

**Predict:** `COUNT(*)` here counts rows of the join. Will it be close to the number of mobile orders, or well above it?

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* 1,006 lines in 546 mobile orders, so `COUNT(*)` is 84 percent too high. The orders table agrees with the second number. The payment is an attribute of the order, repeated on each of its lines: harmless to filter by, wrong to count rows of. To count orders at this grain, count the distinct order ids; at a given grain, `COUNT(*)` counts the thing the grain names.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Say the grain in one sentence before you add a column: one row for each order line.
- Keep the finest grain the questions need: units and sales can be added up from it into coarser tables, never split back.
- A measure is a number you add up or average; a description says who, what, where or when; a key names a row.
- Count orders with `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)` at the line grain; `COUNT(*)` counts lines.

**Common trap:** adding up a number that does not belong to the grain. The sum of the unit prices of 3,287 lines is 14,453.25, and it means nothing.

</Aside>

```sql
FROM (SELECT i.order_id
      FROM order_items AS i
      JOIN products AS p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
      GROUP BY i.order_id
      HAVING COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE p.category = 'coffee') > 0
         AND COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE p.category = 'bakery') > 0);
```
