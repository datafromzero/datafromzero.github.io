### 1. Probability as a share, and independence

**Idea.** The *probability* of an event is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) for how likely it is; in a table it is the share of rows where the event is true. Two events are *independent* when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. For independent events the probability of both is the product of the two probabilities, the Multiplication Rule of OpenIntro.

**Try it.** Payment by card and an order at Old Town, as shares of all 1,800 orders:

```python
card = orders["payment_method"] == "card"
old_town = orders["store_id"] == 1
print(round(card.mean(), 3), round(old_town.mean(), 3))
print(round(card.mean() * old_town.mean(), 3), round((card & old_town).mean(), 3))
```

**Predict:** the share of orders paid by card is about 0.56 and the share taken at Old Town is about 0.41. If the payment method and the store told you nothing about each other, what share of all orders would be card orders at Old Town? Will the real share be close?

### 2. Conditional probability, read both ways

**Idea.** The *conditional probability* P(A given B) is the probability of A among the rows where B is true: shrink the table to B, then take the share (Seeing Theory describes this as shrinking the sample space to one event). P(A given B) and P(B given A) have the same numerator, the rows where both hold, and different denominators, so they are different questions.

### 3. Bayes with a table of 1,000

**Idea.** Count cases instead of juggling probabilities: build a table for 1,000 cases, split them by how common the thing is, then by how the check behaves on each group, and read the answer off the table. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage found that Bayesian problems are easier in such *natural frequencies* than in probabilities. *Bayes' theorem* writes the same counting as a formula: P(A given B) = P(B given A) × P(A) ÷ P(B) (OpenIntro).

**Try it.** A made-up scenario: a check flags orders that may hold a keying mistake.

```python
n = 1000
mistakes = 20  # made up: 2 in 100 orders hold a keying mistake
caught = 18  # made up: the check flags 9 in 10 of them
false_alarms = 49  # made up: and 5 in 100 of the 980 correct orders
table = pd.DataFrame({"flagged": [caught, false_alarms], "not flagged": [mistakes - caught, n - mistakes - false_alarms]}, index=["mistake", "correct"])
print(table)
print(caught + false_alarms, round(caught / (caught + false_alarms), 3))
```

**Predict:** suppose 2 orders in 100 hold a keying mistake, the check flags 9 in 10 of the mistakes, and it also flags 5 in 100 of the correct orders. Of 1,000 orders, how many are flagged, and what share of the flagged orders are real mistakes? Guess before you run: about 9 in 10, about half, or about 1 in 4?

**Try it.** The same share by the formula, and the formula on the real orders:

```python
p_mistake, p_flag_given_mistake, p_flag_given_correct = 0.02, 0.90, 0.05
p_flag = p_flag_given_mistake * p_mistake + p_flag_given_correct * (1 - p_mistake)
print(round(p_flag_given_mistake * p_mistake / p_flag, 3))
p_harbor_given_gold, p_gold, p_harbor = both / gold.sum(), gold.mean(), harbor.mean()
print(round(p_harbor_given_gold * p_gold / p_harbor, 3))
```

**Predict:** Bayes' theorem says P(mistake given flag) = P(flag given mistake) × P(mistake) ÷ P(flag). Will it give the same share as the table? And will it give back the share of Harbor orders that belong to gold customers from unit 2, from the other direction?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text

0.158
```

</details>

*Why:* the formula gives 0.269 again, the same counting written as a formula, and on the real orders it turns the share of gold orders at Harbor back into the share of Harbor orders that are gold. P(flag) adds the two ways to be flagged, the 18 hits and the 49 false alarms.

### 4. Base-rate neglect

**Idea.** The *base rate* is how common the thing is before any check. *Base-rate neglect* is answering "how likely is it, given a flag?" from the check's accuracy alone and ignoring the base rate. In the Seeing Theory demo of Bayes' theorem the prior is the disease's incidence in the population.

**Try it.** One check, three base rates, the last one computed from the data:

```python
def real_share(base_rate, hit=0.90, false_alarm=0.05):
    return hit * base_rate / (hit * base_rate + false_alarm * (1 - base_rate))

print(round(real_share(0.02), 3), round(real_share(0.20), 3), round(real_share(1 / len(orders)), 4))
```

**Predict:** the check never changes: it flags 9 in 10 mistakes and 5 in 100 correct orders. Only how common the mistakes are changes. What share of the flagged orders are real mistakes when 2 in 100 orders hold one, when 20 in 100 do, and at the real rate of the data, one order in 1,800 with an extra zero?


<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A probability is a share of rows; for independent events the shares multiply, only approximately in a sample.
- P(A given B) shrinks the table to B; P(B given A) is another question with another denominator.
- Build the table of 1,000 first, then read off the share you want.
- A positive result for a rare thing is mostly false alarms unless the check is very accurate.

**Common trap:** quoting the check's hit rate as the chance that a flag is real.

</Aside>
