2. Five orders cost 4, 5, 6, 7 and 100 dollars. Which describes a typical order better, the mean or the median, and what are they?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   </details>

3. An order has a z-score of 3.5. What does that say, and what do you do before deleting it?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   It lies 3.5 standard deviations above the mean: unusual, not proof of an error. Investigate where it came from, write down what you found, and delete or correct it only on evidence.

   </details>

4. In a made-up case, 1 order in 100 holds a keying mistake. A check flags 9 in 10 of the mistakes and 1 in 10 of the correct orders. Of 1,000 orders, how many are flagged, and how many of those are real mistakes?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   108 flagged: 9 of the 10 mistakes and 99 of the 990 correct orders. Only 9 of the 108 are real, about 8 percent.

   </details>

5. A survey about service is handed only to customers who sit at a table. What is wrong with it, and who does it miss?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   It is a convenience sample, so it carries selection bias: take-away customers have no chance of answering.

   </details>

6. A 95 percent confidence interval for the mean order total, made from 400 orders, is 12.10 to 13.20 dollars. (a) Does it say that 95 percent of orders cost between those values? (b) An interval from 100 orders of the same cafe would be wider or narrower, and by about how much?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   (a) No. It is about the mean of all orders; single orders spread far wider. The 95 percent describes the method: about 95 of 100 such intervals contain the true mean. (b) Wider, about twice as wide: the interval shrinks with the square root of the sample size, and 400 is four times 100.

   </details>

### 1. Kinds of columns

**Idea.** A column is *numeric* when adding and averaging its values means something, and *categorical* when its values are labels. Numeric columns are *discrete* when they count (whole-number jumps) and *continuous* when they measure; categorical ones can be ordered or not. The pandas type, the `dtype`, tells how a column is stored.

**Try it.** Take an id and a count:

```python
print(orders["order_id"].dtype, orders["order_id"].mean())
print(sorted(order_items["quantity"].unique().tolist()))
```

**Predict:** `order_id` is stored as a whole number. Will `mean()` run, and would the result tell you anything? Which values does `quantity` take?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
int64 1900.5
[1, 2, 3, 20]
```

</details>

*Why:* `mean()` runs on any number, but 1900.5 is only the middle of the id range, not a typical order: an id names a row, as an area code does in OpenIntro Statistics, a number that is not numeric. `quantity` takes 1, 2, 3 and 20, whole numbers with jumps, so it is a discrete count. The 20 is the line that the dataset README says should read 2; later lessons come back to it.

### 2. What is one row?

**Idea.** The *unit of observation* is what one row stands for. In `orders` it is an order, in `order_items` one line of an order, in `customers` one customer. OpenIntro calls a row a case or observational unit.

**Try it.** Two averages of `quantity`:

```python
print(order_items["quantity"].mean().round(2))
print(order_items.groupby("order_id")["quantity"].sum().mean().round(2))
```

**Predict:** both lines are called an average quantity. Will they be equal?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
1.57
2.87
```

</details>

*Why:* an average is an average over rows, so the unit decides what it means. The first averages over the 3,287 lines, the second over the 1,800 orders after adding up each order's lines. Say the unit in the sentence: units per line, or units per order.

### 3. Watching or assigning

**Idea.** In an *observational study* the data arise on their own and you only watch; it can show an association, not by itself a causal connection. In an *experiment* you assign the treatment, and assigning it at random is what lets you credit a difference to it. A *confounder* is a variable tied to both the explanatory and the response variable, so that it can explain a difference by itself. All of cafe-sales is observational.

**Try it.** The table above counted the whole period. Count again from April 1 on, when all three stores were open:

```python
orders["month"] = pd.to_datetime(orders["ordered_at"]).dt.month
named = orders.merge(stores[["store_id", "store_name"]], on="store_id", validate="many_to_one")
print(named[named["month"] >= 4].groupby("store_name").size().to_dict())
```

**Predict:** the table counted every order from January to June. Now count only the orders from April 1 on. Will Old Town still be far ahead of Harbor?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
{'Campus': 362, 'Harbor': 355, 'Old Town': 365}
```

An experiment (a made-up one: a coin flip decides each morning whether the till suggests a pastry) cuts that tie, because the coin does not care about the weekday, the weather or the store.

### 4. What could identify a person?

**Idea.** *Personally identifiable information* (PII) is information that can distinguish or trace a person, or that is linked or linkable to one (NIST SP 800-122). Removing the name is not enough: Sweeney found, in 1990 census data, that three ordinary fields, ZIP code, gender and date of birth, likely made 87 percent of the U.S. population unique. A `customer_id` is a *pseudonym*, a number that stands in for a name, so the table is pseudonymous, not anonymous: whoever holds the list that links ids to names can undo it, and the id ties all of a person's orders together.

**Try it.** In `customers`, find those who are alone with their home city and signup date, and count their orders:

```python
same_city_and_day = customers.groupby(["city", "signup_date"])["customer_id"].transform("size")
alone = customers[same_city_and_day == 1]
exposed = orders[orders["customer_id"].isin(alone["customer_id"])]
print(len(alone), len(exposed))
```

**Predict:** of the 150 customers, how many are the only one with their city and signup date? How many of the 1,800 orders belong to them?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
124 1483
```

</details>

*Why:* anyone who knows one of these customers' home city and signup day can pick out their id, and with it every order, each with a store and a minute. The data here is synthetic and describes no real person. A real cafe would tell customers at sign-up what it collects and why, ask for their consent where it applies, collect only what its questions need, as NIST advises (`referred_by` links people to each other), and keep the id-to-name list away from the analysts' copy. The OECD privacy principles that NIST lists say personal data should be obtained fairly and, where appropriate, with the person's knowledge or consent, and used only for the purposes stated. What the law requires depends on where the customers live; this lesson is not legal advice.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Numeric means an average makes sense; an id stored as a number is a label.
- Say what one row stands for before you average anything.
- Only an assigned, randomized comparison can credit a difference to the treatment.
- Look for a confounder before you explain a difference between groups.
- A pseudonymous id is not anonymous; a few ordinary fields can single a person out.

**Common trap:** calling a table anonymous because it has no names.

</Aside>

tier_number = customers["loyalty_tier"].map({"bronze": 1, "silver": 2, "gold": 3})  # the colleague's numbers

</details>

<details>
