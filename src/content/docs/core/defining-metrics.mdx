## The question

The product lead of a budgeting app, a fictional app whose log is the dataset `app-events`, writes in the team chat:

> How many people were active in June? And is it getting easier or harder to turn a new signup into a Plus subscriber?

The data is a log with one row for each thing the app recorded. These are the first eight rows of one person:

```text
10007 2026-01-01 02:23:16 signup_complete ios
10007 2026-01-01 02:25:45 onboarding_complete ios
10007 2026-01-01 03:16:20 add_expense ios
10007 2026-01-02 04:34:57 session_start ios
10007 2026-01-02 04:39:36 add_expense ios
10007 2026-01-03 23:53:17 view_paywall ios
10007 2026-01-03 23:57:03 start_checkout ios
10007 2026-01-03 23:57:46 purchase ios
```

There is no column called `active` and none called `conversion`. Each word of the question hides a choice:

| the lead said | what you must decide |
|---|---|
| active | which event counts |
| people | out of whom |
| June | which days, and counted by what |
| turn a signup into a subscriber | within how long |

By the end you can write a metric as four choices, show how each one moves the number, and say what to put beside a goal.

**Diverge.** In C9 the window of 14 days was given. Unit 2 shows what the number does when the window or the base is another one.

## The big idea

A metric is a fraction with four slots: the unit, the numerator, the denominator and the window; change a slot and you have changed the metric.

## Learn by doing

About 40 minutes, 4 new ideas: the numerator, the window and the base, the unit, and a goal with its components and a guardrail. The Data cell of the notebook loads `users`, `events`, `subs` (the subscription rows) and `assign` (the experiment rows) and adds `bought14` to `users`: 1 if a person has a `purchase` within 14 days of their own signup. The people who signed up by June 16 have a full 14 days of follow-up before the data ends: they are `full`.

### 1. The numerator: who counts

**Idea.** A *metric* is a number written as a fraction with four parts. The *numerator* says who or what counts as having done the thing. The *denominator* says out of whom. The *unit* says what one item of the count is: a person, an event, a day. The *window* says which days, and by what clock. Rodden, Hutchinson and Fu note that what counts as using a product depends on its goals: sometimes a visit, sometimes a finished key task. This unit changes only the numerator: the event that makes a person active.

**Try it.** Count the people active in June (June 1 to 30, by the time the event happened) under four definitions, then look at who "any event" adds:

```python
june = events[(events["event_ts"] >= "2026-06-01") & (events["event_ts"] < "2026-07-01")]     # June 1 to 30, by when it happened
definitions = {
    "any event": june,
    "a return visit (session_start)": june[june["event_name"] == "session_start"],
    "an expense added (add_expense)": june[june["event_name"] == "add_expense"],
    "a visit or an expense": june[june["event_name"].isin(["session_start", "add_expense"])],
}
active = {name: set(rows["user_id"]) for name, rows in definitions.items()}
for name, people in active.items():
    print(f"{len(people):>6,}  {name}")
extra = active["any event"] - active["a visit or an expense"]       # counted by the first definition and not by the last
print(len(extra))
shown = june[june["user_id"].isin(extra)].groupby("event_name")["user_id"].nunique().sort_values(ascending=False, kind="stable")
for name, people in shown.items():
    print(f"{people:>6,}  {name}")
```

**Predict:** the four definitions count distinct people. Which will be the largest? Will "any event" beat "a visit or an expense" by a few hundred people or by thousands?

*Why:* the four numbers run from 4,289 to 7,874, and each is a defensible "active in June". "Any event" is the largest because it counts everything the app records: 2,659 of its people have no visit and no expense in June. The list shows what they have instead. 892 signed up that month, and 155 are there because they canceled: under this definition a person who left counts as active. An ad campaign that brings signups would raise it without anyone using the app. That is how a metric gets gamed, by accident or on purpose: the number goes up and the thing it stands for does not. The goal picks the definition. For an app whose job is to record expenses, an expense added is the closest to the goal.

### 2. The window and the base

**Idea.** The window says which days count. There are two ways to set it. A *cohort reading* follows the same people for the same time after their own start, for example 14 days from each signup. A *calendar reading* counts what happened in a calendar period, whoever started when. The base, the denominator, is the people who could have done the thing. Kohavi, Henne and Sommerfield give the same advice for a change to a checkout page: analyze only the people who reached it, because everyone else adds noise. Rodden, Hutchinson and Fu define retention in the same way: of the people active in one period, the share still active later.

**Try it.** The 5,077 people who signed up in May, read three ways, and then two later groups of signups read today:

```python
may = users[(users["signup_date"] >= "2026-05-01") & (users["signup_date"] <= "2026-05-31")]     # the people who signed up in May
cohort = int(may["bought14"].sum())                                  # cohort reading: a purchase within 14 days of their own signup
ever = int(may["user_id"].isin(purchases["user_id"]).sum())          # no window: a purchase any time up to June 30
in_may = purchases[(purchases["event_ts"] >= "2026-05-01") & (purchases["event_ts"] < "2026-06-01")]     # calendar reading: purchases made in May
print(len(may), cohort, ever, len(in_may), int(in_may["user_id"].isin(may["user_id"]).sum()))
for label, count in (("cohort, 14 days", cohort), ("ever, to June 30", ever), ("calendar, May", len(in_may))):
    print(f"{100 * count / len(may):.1f}%  {label}")
late = users[users["signup_date"] >= "2026-06-17"]                   # signed up in the last two weeks of the data: their 14 days are not all over
june_early = users[(users["signup_date"] >= "2026-06-08") & (users["signup_date"] <= "2026-06-16")]     # June 8 to 16: a full 14 days
print(len(late), f"{100 * late['bought14'].mean():.1f}%", len(june_early), f"{100 * june_early['bought14'].mean():.1f}%")
```

**Predict:** the cohort reading is a purchase within 14 days of the person's own signup. The second reading has no window: a purchase any time up to June 30. The calendar reading divides the purchases made in May, whoever made them, by the May signups. Which reading will be the highest, and which the lowest?

### 4. A goal, its components and a guardrail

**Idea.** One number cannot carry a goal. Kohavi, Henne and Sommerfield recommend choosing a single metric to aim an experiment at, and warn that a short-term one, such as revenue, can be pushed up in ways that hurt the users. So put three things beside it. The *north-star metric* is the one number the product steers by. An *input metric* is a number a team can move that feeds it, such as conversion. A *guardrail* is a metric that does not show whether a change worked but that you do not want to harm; Dmitriev and colleagues call such metrics guardrail metrics. For this app, take the people paying for Plus at the end of a month as the north star. It is made of *components*: new subscriptions come in and cancellations go out.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A metric has four slots: the unit, the numerator, the denominator and the window. Write all four in the sentence that names it.
- A cohort reading follows the same people for the same time; a calendar reading mixes cohorts. A window that has not closed makes the newest cohort look worse.
- Name the base: the same numerator gives different rates out of different people.
- Count people when you mean people. Retried rows push a row count up, never down.
- Put the goal beside its components and a guardrail, and read the guardrail's interval.

**Common trap:** reading a rise in "active people" as more use. Under "any event", 2,659 of the 7,874 people counted in June have no visit and no expense.

</Aside>
