## The question

The success team of a budgeting app writes:

> We can send a personal welcome message to a few new people each day. Can you tell us, the moment someone signs up, who is most likely to buy Plus?

The data are the people of `app-events`, a fictional app. "Buy" means a purchase of Plus within 14 days of signing up. Four of the first people, and the first two who bought:

| user_id | signup_date | channel | platform | region | bought within 14 days |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---:|
| 10001 | 2026-01-01 | organic_search | web | asia_pacific | 0 |
| 10002 | 2026-01-01 | app_store | android | north_america | 0 |
| 10007 | 2026-01-01 | referral | ios | north_america | 1 |
| 10011 | 2026-01-01 | organic_search | web | europe | 1 |

By the end you can write a prediction problem as four lines, say what is known at the moment, split with a seed, fit a <Term id="glossary-baseline">baseline</Term> and a first <Term id="glossary-model">model</Term> on the training part only, and write the verdict on the held-out part.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Regression as a thinking tool*](/core/linear-regression-basics/) (C11) a line predicted an order's total from its units, and a residual was the miss. A model here does the same for a yes or no: it predicts a chance from columns, and its misses are scored. In [*Defining a metric before you compute it*](/core/defining-metrics/) (C12) a metric had a numerator, a denominator, a unit and a window: the target below is one.

**Diverge.** In C11 the line was judged on the rows it was fitted to. Unit 3 shows how a model is judged on rows it has not seen.

</Aside>

## Learn by doing

About 50 minutes, 5 new ideas: the four lines of a problem, what is known at the moment, the split, the baseline and the first model, and the verdict. The setup cell loaded `users` and `events` and gave each person a `platform` and a `signup_ts`.

<OpenInColab />

### 1. Name the target, the unit and the decision

**Idea.** A prediction problem has four lines. The *target* is the one thing you predict, written as a column. The *unit* is what one row stands for. The *prediction moment* is the instant when the prediction is made. The *decision* is what someone will do with it. Writing them first fixes which rows and which columns the problem may use.

| line | this lesson |
|---|---|
| target | `bought14`: 1 if the person buys Plus within 14 days of signing up, else 0 |
| unit | one new person, one row |
| prediction moment | the instant the account is created |
| decision | who gets a personal welcome message, from a team with time for a few |

People who signed up after June 16 have fewer than 14 days before the export of July 1, so a missing purchase would not mean "did not buy". They are left out.

**Try it:** build the table, one row per person.

```python
last_day = pd.Timestamp("2026-06-16")                 # signed up by June 16: a full 14 days before the export of July 1
first_buy = events[events["event_name"] == "purchase"].groupby("user_id")["event_ts"].min()
people = users[(users["signup_date"] <= last_day) & users["signup_ts"].notna()].copy()
people["days_to_buy"] = (people["user_id"].map(first_buy) - people["signup_ts"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400    # NaN: never bought
people["bought14"] = (people["days_to_buy"] <= 14).astype(int)                                                    # NaN compares False: 0
print((users["signup_date"] <= last_day).sum(), len(people), bool(people["user_id"].is_unique))
print(int(people["bought14"].sum()), round(float(people["bought14"].mean()), 4))
```

**Predict:** The table has one row for each person who signed up from January 1 to June 16 and has a signup event. How many people does the table keep, and what share of them have a 1?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
20177 20173 True
2427 0.1203
```

</details>

*Why:* 20,177 people signed up by June 16 and the table keeps 20,173: 4 have no event at all, so no signup time. Each person is one row. 2,427 of them bought within 14 days, 12.03 percent: the 12.0 percent of the dataset README for people with a full follow-up. A person who never bought has no purchase time (`NaN`), and `NaN <= 14` is false, so they get a 0.

### 2. What is known at the moment

**Idea.** A column is a *<Term id="glossary-feature">feature</Term>* only if its value is final at the prediction moment. For each column ask when its value stops changing. A value that settles later belongs to the future: the model cannot have it when the message has to be chosen.

| column | final when | a feature here? |
|---|---|---|
| `channel`, `region` | at signup | yes |
| `platform` | at signup, from the signup event | yes |
| `signup_date` and its hour | at signup | yes |
| `plan_at_signup` | on the signup screen, at the instant of a purchase made there | no, left out for now |

**Try it:** check the table against the events: does the app hold anything about a new person before the signup?

```python
moment = events.merge(people[["user_id", "signup_ts"]], on="user_id", validate="many_to_one")
before = moment[(moment["event_ts"] <= moment["signup_ts"]) & (moment["event_name"] != "signup_complete")]
print(len(before), len(moment), int((moment["event_name"] == "signup_complete").sum()) == len(people))
print(people[["channel", "platform", "region"]].isna().sum().to_dict())
print(round(float(moment.groupby("user_id").size().mean()), 2))
```

**Predict:** The prediction moment is the instant the account exists. Apart from the signup itself, how many events does the app hold about a new person at that instant?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0 168179 True
{'channel': 0, 'platform': 0, 'region': 0}
8.34
```

</details>

*Why:* none of the 168,179 events of these people is earlier than their signup, and each person has exactly one `signup_complete`. The other 7.34 events a person has on average (8.34 with the signup) all come later, so every count of visits, expenses or paywall views is a value from the future. The three columns have no gaps, so the first model has three features: `channel`, `platform` and `region`.

### 3. Split before you look

**Idea.** Keep part of the rows aside to see how a model does on people it has not seen. The *training part* is what the model may learn from; the *test part* is touched once, at the end. the guidance of scikit-learn, a Python library for machine learning, is to split first, before any preprocessing. `train_test_split` shuffles the rows, `test_size` is the share held out, `stratify` keeps the share of 1s equal in both parts, and `random_state` is the *seed* of that shuffle.

**Try it:** split the people 75 to 25.

```python
X = people[["channel", "platform", "region"]]          # columns known at the moment
y = people["bought14"]                                 # the target
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=19, stratify=y)
print(len(X_train), len(X_test))
print(round(float(y_train.mean()), 4), round(float(y_test.mean()), 4))
again = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=19, stratify=y)[0]
other = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=20, stratify=y)[0]
print(bool(again.index.equals(X_train.index)), len(X_train.index.intersection(other.index)))
```

**Predict:** Split the people 75 to 25 with `random_state=19`, keeping the share of buyers equal in both parts. How many people go to each part, and what share of each part are buyers? Then split again with the same seed: do you get the same people? And with seed 20: how many of the first training people are in the new training part?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
15129 5044
0.1203 0.1203
True 11404
```

</details>

*Why:* 15,129 people train the model and 5,044 are held out, and both parts have 12.03 percent buyers. The same seed gives the same people, so anyone can repeat the split, and that is what a seed is for; seed 20 gives a different split, and 11,404 of the first 15,129 training people are in its training part. No seed is special: pick one, write it down and keep it. A different split is a different sample, and unit 5 shows how much the score moves.

### 4. A baseline and a first model, fitted on the training part only

**Idea.** A *baseline* is the simplest honest prediction, the score any model has to beat. For a yes or no it gives every person the share of 1s in the training part as a chance (`DummyClassifier`, strategy `"prior"`). A *model* learns from the columns: here a logistic regression, which turns columns into a chance between 0 and 1 (the Data Scientist track teaches it), after a step that makes one 0/1 column for each value of a text column. `fit` is called on the training part only; `predict_proba` gives the chances. Rule 4 of Google's Rules of Machine Learning asks for a simple first model, which also gives you baseline metrics.

**Try it:** fit both on the training part and look at the chances they give the test part.

```python
baseline = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(X_train, y_train)      # ignores the columns: the training share of buyers for everyone
print(np.round(baseline.predict_proba(X_test.head(3))[:, 1], 4))
model = make_pipeline(
    ColumnTransformer([("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), ["channel", "platform", "region"])]),   # one 0/1 column per value
    LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000),
)
model.fit(X_train, y_train)                                             # only the training part
chance = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]                              # the model's chance of a 1, for each test person
print(round(float(chance.min()), 3), round(float(chance.max()), 3))
probe = pd.DataFrame({"channel": ["referral", "paid_social"], "platform": ["web", "android"], "region": ["north_america", "north_america"]})
print(np.round(model.predict_proba(probe)[:, 1], 3))
rate = (X_train.assign(y=y_train).groupby(["channel", "platform"])["y"].mean() * 100).round(1)
print(rate.loc[[("referral", "web"), ("paid_social", "android")]].to_dict())
```

**Predict:** The baseline gives every person the share of buyers in the training part. What chance does it give a test person? And the first model, which learns from channel, platform and region: what are the lowest and the highest chance it gives anyone in the test part: both near the baseline, or one far above it?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[0.1203 0.1203 0.1203]
0.026 0.292
[0.229 0.027]
{('referral', 'web'): 22.9, ('paid_social', 'android'): 2.9}
```

</details>

*Why:* the baseline gives 0.1203 to everyone. The model's chances run from 0.026 to 0.292. For referral people on the web it says 0.229, and the training share of that group is 22.9 percent; for paid social on Android it says 0.027, against 2.9 percent. So this model has mostly learned a table of group shares from the training part. No one gets a chance above 0.5: what to do with chances like these is the question of [*Choosing a metric: accuracy, precision, recall and error costs*](/core/metrics-and-error-costs/).

### 5. Compare on the held-out part and write the verdict

**Idea.** Score on the test part, once, and always beside the baseline. The *Brier score* is the mean squared difference between the predicted chance and the actual outcome, 0 or 1 (scikit-learn's `brier_score_loss`); smaller is better. It is the squared miss of C11 with chances in place of dollars. A model is judged by how far it gets below the baseline's score on the same test part.

**Try it:** score both on the test part and on the training part, then repeat the split with twenty other seeds.

```python
brier_base = brier_score_loss(y_test, baseline.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1])
brier_model = brier_score_loss(y_test, chance)
print(round(brier_base, 4), round(brier_model, 4), round(1 - brier_model / brier_base, 3))
print(round(brier_score_loss(y_train, model.predict_proba(X_train)[:, 1]), 4))          # the same score on the rows the model learned from
gaps = []
for seed in range(100, 120):                                            # twenty other splits of the same people
    a, b, c, d = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=seed, stratify=y)
    base_s = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(a, c)
    model_s = make_pipeline(ColumnTransformer([("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), ["channel", "platform", "region"])]), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(a, c)
    gaps.append(brier_score_loss(d, base_s.predict_proba(b)[:, 1]) - brier_score_loss(d, model_s.predict_proba(b)[:, 1]))
print(round(min(gaps), 4), round(max(gaps), 4), sum(g > 0 for g in gaps))
```

**Predict:** The Brier score is the average of (chance minus outcome) squared over the test people, where the outcome is 0 or 1: smaller is better. Will the first model beat the baseline on the test part by a lot, a little or not at all? And on the training part, will its score be much better than on the test part?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.1059 0.1008 0.048
0.1014
0.0031 0.0057 20
```

</details>

*Why:* on 5,044 held-out people the baseline scores 0.1059 and the model 0.1008, 4.8 percent lower. On the training part the model scores 0.1014, no better than on the test part: a recipe this simple has not memorized its rows. In the twenty other splits (seeds 100 to 119, so these numbers depend on the seeds) the model beat the baseline every time, by 0.0031 to 0.0057.

In symbols, with $$n$$ people, $$\hat{p}_i$$ the chance given to person $$i$$ and $$y_i$$ the outcome, 0 or 1:

$$
\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2
$$

The verdict, in plain words: *on 5,044 people held out from the fitting, the first model, which knows only channel, platform and region, scores 0.1008 against 0.1059 for giving everyone 12 percent. That is a small gain that held in twenty other splits. It is better than a flat guess and not yet an answer to the decision.*

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A prediction problem is four lines: the target, the unit, the prediction moment and the decision.
- A feature is final at the moment of prediction; ask when each column stops changing.
- Split first, with a seed; fit anything only on the training part.
- Print the baseline's score next to the model's, on the same test part.
- The Brier score is the mean squared miss of the chances; smaller is better.

**Common trap:** reading a score alone. A 0.1008 says nothing until the baseline's score on the same test part stands beside it.

</Aside>
