## The question

A teammate shares a notebook for the welcome-message model of the last lesson, the one that chooses who gets a message at the moment of signup, and writes:

> I added three columns I found in our tables, and the model got much better. Can we ship it?

The data are the people of `app-events`, a fictional app. The three columns: `n_events`, the number of events of the person in the events table; `plus_uses`, how many of those are `plus_feature_used`; and `has_plan`, 1 when the person has a row in the subscriptions table. Six of the first people (person 10112 subscribed, but after the first 14 days), next to the target `bought14` (1 when they bought Plus within 14 days of signing up):

| user_id | channel | n_events | plus_uses | has_plan | bought14 |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10001 | organic_search | 4 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 10002 | app_store | 4 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 10005 | referral | 16 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 10007 | referral | 48 | 35 | 1 | 1 |
| 10011 | organic_search | 37 | 21 | 1 | 1 |
| 10112 | referral | 35 | 14 | 1 | 0 |

By the end you can say what is wrong with columns like these, find them, fit a scaler without letting the test part in, and read a score that looks too good as a warning.

## Learn by doing

About 35 minutes, 4 new ideas: a score that is too good, asking when each value is known, fitting a scaler on the training part only, and a split by time. The setup cell built `people` of C19, with `signup_day` (days since January 1) and `signup_hour` too, and gave you `make_model` (the recipe of C19, with optional scaled columns) and `score` (the Brier score of a fitted model).

### 1. A score that is too good

**Idea.** *Target leakage* is a column that carries information the model would not have at the prediction moment, often because the outcome itself, or what follows it, fills the column in. scikit-learn's guide defines data leakage as the case where "information that would not be available at prediction time is used when building the model". The model learns the leak, and the test part, where the column is filled in the same way, rewards it.

**Try it:** add the three columns of the teammate to the honest recipe and score it on the test part, then score it again with the columns as the app would see them at signup.

```python
n_events = events.groupby("user_id").size()                                          # every event of the person, whenever it happened
plus_uses = events[events["event_name"] == "plus_feature_used"].groupby("user_id").size()
latest = subs.sort_values("updated_at").groupby("subscription_id").tail(1)           # the current row of each subscription
people["n_events"] = people["user_id"].map(n_events).fillna(0)
people["plus_uses"] = people["user_id"].map(plus_uses).fillna(0)
people["has_plan"] = people["user_id"].isin(latest["user_id"]).astype(int)           # a join to the subscriptions table
LEAKY = ["n_events", "plus_uses", "has_plan"]
train, test = train_test_split(people, test_size=0.25, random_state=20, stratify=people["bought14"])
base = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(train, train["bought14"])
print(round(float(brier_score_loss(test["bought14"], base.predict_proba(test)[:, 1])), 4))          # the baseline
honest = make_model().fit(train, train["bought14"])
leaky = make_model(LEAKY).fit(train, train["bought14"])
print(round(score(honest, test), 4), round(score(leaky, test), 4))
at_moment = test.assign(n_events=1, plus_uses=0, has_plan=0)                         # the same people as the app sees them at signup
print(round(score(leaky, at_moment), 4), round(float(leaky.predict_proba(at_moment)[:, 1].max()), 4))      # its score, and its largest chance
```

**Predict:** A teammate adds three columns to the honest recipe of C19: `n_events` (every event of the person in the events table), `plus_uses` (their `plus_feature_used` events) and `has_plan` (1 when the person has a row in the subscriptions table). With seed 20 the baseline scores 0.1059 on the test part. What will the model with the three columns score: about 0.10, about 0.05 or about 0.01? And what would it score on the same people if the app used it at the moment of signup, where `n_events` is 1 and the other two are 0?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.1059
0.1012 0.0115
0.1203 0.0002
```

</details>

*Why:* the honest recipe scores 0.1012 against the baseline's 0.1059, and the recipe with the three columns scores 0.0115, almost nine times lower: the "overly optimistic performance estimates" of the scikit-learn guide. Nothing a person does before signing up explains that. Two of the columns are filled in by the purchase: Plus-only events exist only while a subscription is active, and a subscription row is written when a person buys. The third, `n_events`, counts everything after signup, the Plus-only events and the purchase included. At the moment of signup the same model sees `n_events` = 1 and nothing else, gives every person a chance of at most 0.0002 and scores 0.1203, the share of buyers and worse than the baseline. A model that looks this much better than the baseline, on columns about a person's later life, earns suspicion before it earns a launch.

### 2. Ask when each value becomes known

**Idea.** To find a leak, ask of every column: when does its value stop changing, and is that before the prediction moment? A column that settles later, or that is filled in because of the outcome, must go. A score that jumps when one column is added points at the column to ask about.

**Try it:** add each of the three columns alone to the honest recipe.

```python
one_at_a_time = {col: round(score(make_model([col]).fit(train, train["bought14"]), test), 4) for col in LEAKY}
print(one_at_a_time)
```

**Predict:** Add the three columns to the honest recipe one at a time. Which one alone brings the Brier score closest to 0? Does any of them leave it near the honest 0.1012?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
{'n_events': 0.0814, 'plus_uses': 0.0422, 'has_plan': 0.0117}
```

*Why:* `has_plan` alone brings the score to 0.0117, and none leaves it near 0.1012: even the weakest, `n_events` at 0.0814, is far below it. The audit, for every column the model could use:

| column | settles when | known at signup? |
|---|---|---|
| `channel`, `platform`, `region` | at signup | yes |
| `signup_day`, `signup_hour` | at signup | yes |
| `n_events` | when the data ends: it counts every event, the purchase and what follows it included | no |
| `plus_uses` | after the purchase: Plus-only events exist only while a subscription is active | no |
| `has_plan` | when the purchase is written to the subscriptions table | no |

All three fail the test, and all three go. The honest recipe is the model of unit 1 that scored 0.1012.

### 3. Fit the scaler on the training part only

**Idea.** *Train-test contamination* is the same leak through another door: the test rows influence the model before the test. It happens in every step that learns from data. A *scaler* rewrites a numeric column as the number of standard deviations above or below its mean, and learns that mean and spread from the rows it is fitted on; an imputer, which fills gaps with a value, learns the value the same way. The guide's rule is "never call fit on the test data": fit each such step on the training part, then apply it to both parts. A pipeline does this by construction.

**Try it:** fit a scaler on all people and another on the training part only, and score the model with each.

```python
num = ["signup_day"]
sc_all = StandardScaler().fit(people[num])                    # learned from all people, the test part included
sc_train = StandardScaler().fit(train[num])                   # learned from the training part only
print(round(float(sc_all.mean_[0]), 2), round(float(sc_train.mean_[0]), 2))


def brier_with_scaler(scaler):
    enc = OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore").fit(train[CATS])
    design = lambda d: np.hstack([enc.transform(d[CATS]).toarray(), scaler.transform(d[num])])
    lr = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(design(train), train["bought14"])
    return float(brier_score_loss(test["bought14"], lr.predict_proba(design(test))[:, 1]))


print(round(brier_with_scaler(sc_all), 4), round(brier_with_scaler(sc_train), 4))
piped = make_model(num).fit(train, train["bought14"])         # the pipeline fits its scaler on the rows it is given to fit
print(round(score(piped, test), 4))
```

**Predict:** A scaler learns a mean and a spread from the rows it is fitted on. Take `signup_day`, the days since January 1. Fitted on all people or on the training part only, how different are the two means? And does the model's Brier score change at 4 decimals?

*Why:* the means are 93.33 and 93.26 days, and the three scores agree to 4 decimals, the pipeline's included. The test part is a random quarter of the same people, so what the scaler learns from it is almost what it learns from the other three quarters. Nothing visible happens here, and the rule stays: the code that breaks it looks the same when the test part is not like the training part, which unit 4 shows.

### 4. When the future differs from the past

**Idea.** A model for the app is used on people who have not signed up yet. A *time split* trains on the earlier signups and tests on the later ones, as the app will be used; rule 33 of Google's Rules of Machine Learning says to test on data from after the date of the training data. The rule of unit 3 holds: the scaler is fitted on the earlier part.

**Try it:** train on the people who signed up before May 1, test on those from May 1 to June 16, and look at the scaler both ways.

```python
cut = pd.Timestamp("2026-05-01")
early, late = people[people["signup_date"] < cut], people[people["signup_date"] >= cut]
print(len(early), len(late), round(float(early["bought14"].mean()), 4), round(float(late["bought14"].mean()), 4))
sc_all = StandardScaler().fit(people[num])
sc_early = StandardScaler().fit(early[num])
print(round(float(sc_all.transform(late[num]).mean()), 2), round(float(sc_early.transform(late[num]).mean()), 2))
model_t = make_model().fit(early, early["bought14"])
base_t = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(early, early["bought14"])
print(round(score(model_t, late), 4), round(float(brier_score_loss(late["bought14"], base_t.predict_proba(late)[:, 1])), 4))
share = lambda d: round(float((d["channel"] == "paid_social").mean()), 3)                      # the share of paid_social people
print(share(early), share(late), round(float(people.loc[people["channel"] == "paid_social", "bought14"].mean()), 3))
```

**Predict:** Train on the people who signed up before May 1 and test on those from May 1 to June 16. Is the share of buyers the same in both parts? Is the mean of the late people's scaled `signup_day` about the same through a scaler fitted on all people as through one fitted on the early people only? And does the model still beat the baseline?

<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

12739 7434 0.1301 0.1036
1.03 2.17
0.0883 0.0936
0.155 0.343 0.039

*Why:* 13.0 percent of the early people bought and 10.4 percent of the late ones, mostly because of the mix: paid social is 15.5 percent of the early signups and 34.3 percent of the late ones, and it converts at 3.9 percent (the campaign of April 13 to June 7, as in [*Diagnosing a metric that moved*](/core/diagnosing-a-movement/), C13). The early part also holds the `paywall_v2` experiment, which lifted conversion for its variant from January 12 to March 8, so the early rate is not a clean baseline. Through the scaler that learned from the early people only, the late people sit 2.17 standard deviations above its mean day; through the scaler that also saw them, 1.03. A scaler that has seen the test part makes the future look closer than it is. The model still beats the baseline, 0.0883 against 0.0936, which is 5.6 percent lower; on the random split of unit 1 the gain was 4.4 percent (0.1012 against 0.1059). In this data a random split did not flatter the model; that is something to check, not to assume, and the time split is the first thing to try when a score looks too good.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A column may feed the model only if its value settles before the prediction moment.
- For every column ask when it stops changing, and drop the ones that settle later.
- Fit every step that learns from data (scaler, imputer, selector) on the training part only; a pipeline does it for you.
- When the model will be used on the future, split by time and compare with the random split.
- A score far better than the baseline is a warning to look for a leak, not a result to report.

**Common trap:** trusting a high score because it is high. On a target about people's choices, a model that jumps from 0.10 to 0.01 has been told something.

</Aside>
