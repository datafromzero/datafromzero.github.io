## The big idea

A *distribution* tells how often each value occurs, and its shape, center and spread are read together; a *z-score* then says how far one value sits from the center, in units of spread.

### 1. The shape of a column

**Idea.** A *histogram* counts the values that fall in each slice of the number line. Read its shape: where it peaks, how many humps it has, and which tail is longer. A column is *right skewed* when the long tail points to the high values; `skew()` puts that in one number, positive for a long right tail and negative for a long left one (NIST).

**Try it.** Count the orders in 4-dollar slices, and the skew with and without the largest order:

```python
edges = [0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 200]

print(slices.tolist())

ax.set_xlabel("Order total (USD)")

```

### 2. The z-score

**Idea.** The *z-score* of a value is its distance from the mean divided by the standard deviation, `(x - mean) / sd`. It counts how many standard deviations a value lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean, and a larger absolute z-score is a more unusual value (OpenIntro). It ranks how unusual a value is; it does not say why.

**Try it.** The z-scores of the three largest orders, and how many orders lie beyond 3:

```python
z = (totals - totals.mean()) / totals.std()
for row in sales.assign(z=z).nlargest(3, "total").itertuples():
    print(row.order_id, row.store_id, row.total, round(row.z, 2))
print((z.abs() > 3).sum())
```

**Predict:** how many standard deviations above the mean is each of the three largest orders? How many of the 1,799 orders have a z-score above 3 or below minus 3?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
2075 1 197.0 21.4
2348 2 68.25 6.45
1030 2 50.5 4.39
9
```

</details>

*Why:* the order of 197.00 is 21.4 standard deviations above the mean, far beyond the other two, and 9 orders lie beyond 3. A large z-score earns an order a closer look; it is no verdict.

### 3. The empirical rule, for orientation

**Idea.** A normal curve is bell shaped and symmetric (Seeing Theory). On it, about 68, 95 and 99.7 percent of the values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean: the 68-95-99.7 rule of OpenIntro. Use it to get your bearings on a z-score, not as a test: no column is exactly normal, and a value outside the bands is unusual, not wrong.

**Try it.** The shares of values within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations, for the orders, for the daily sales of the chain, and for the normal curve:

```python
def share_within(x):
    zx = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()
    return [round(float((zx.abs() <= k).mean()), 3) for k in (1, 2, 3)]

print(share_within(totals))
print(share_within(daily))
print([round(float(stats.norm.cdf(k) - stats.norm.cdf(-k)), 3) for k in (1, 2, 3)])
```

**Predict:** for a normal curve about 68, 95 and 99.7 percent of the values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean. Which column will match these shares better, the order totals or the daily sales of the chain?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text

[0.691, 0.961, 0.994]
[0.683, 0.954, 0.997]
```

</details>

*Why:* the daily sales are closer to the normal shares (69.1, 96.1 and 99.4 percent). The order totals put 77.5 percent within 1 standard deviation, not about 68: a long right tail crowds the values near the center. A day adds up about ten orders, and sums come out more bell shaped; *Sampling variability and the Central Limit Theorem* (C6) explains why.

### 4. Investigate before you delete

**Idea.** An *outlier* is a value that appears to deviate markedly from the others (NIST). It may be bad data, random variation or something real; when you cannot tell, the NIST handbook says you typically do not want to simply delete it. Write an *investigation note* first: what the value is, where it comes from, the evidence, the likely cause, and the decision.

**Try it.** Open the lines of the largest order:

```python
suspect = int(sales.loc[sales["total"].idxmax(), "order_id"])
detail = lines[lines["order_id"] == suspect].merge(products[["product_id", "product_name"]], on="product_id", validate="many_to_one")
for row in detail.itertuples():
    print(row.product_name, row.quantity, row.amount)
print(lines[lines["order_id"] != suspect]["quantity"].max())
```

**Predict:** one order has a total above 190. Which line makes it so large, and what is the largest quantity on any other line of the whole table?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
Bagel with Cream Cheese 2 8.5
Turkey Sandwich 20 185.0
Americano 1 3.5
3
```

</details>

*Why:* one line of 20 Turkey Sandwiches makes the order, and no other line in the table has more than 3 units. That is evidence, not proof. A note for the owner:

> **Order 2075** (Old Town, May 1, 13:25), total 197.00, z = 21.4.
> **Where it comes from:** one line, Turkey Sandwich, quantity 20, worth 185.00; its other two lines are ordinary.
> **Evidence:** no other line in the table has a quantity above 3.
> **Likely cause:** a keying slip, 2 typed as 20; the dataset README says so. In a real cafe, ask the store for the till record.
> **Decision:** keep the row, flag it, and report the totals with and without it.

### 5. Compare two groups on all three

**Idea.** To compare two groups, put center, spread and shape side by side. A box plot or a short table of median, IQR and skew does it; one number alone can hide the difference or invent one.

**Try it.** Card and mobile orders:

```python
for method in ["card", "mobile"]:
    t = sales.loc[sales["payment_method"] == method, "total"]
    print(method, len(t), t.median(), t.quantile(0.75) - t.quantile(0.25), round(t.skew(), 1))
```

**Predict:** compare card and mobile orders. Do they differ in center, in spread or in shape?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
card 1001 11.5 9.75 0.9
mobile 546 11.5 9.4375 8.9
```

</details>

*Why:* the medians match and the IQRs are close, but the skews are 0.9 and 8.9. The order of 197.00 was paid by mobile, so the difference in shape is one order, not a way mobile customers order; the note of unit 4 comes first.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Read a column's shape, center and spread together.
- A z-score is `(x - mean) / sd`: it ranks how unusual a value is, it does not say why.
- The 68-95-99.7 rule fits bell-shaped columns; skewed ones drift away from it.
- Investigate an outlier and write the note; delete it only on evidence.
- Compare two groups on center, spread and shape at once.

**Common trap:** using the 68-95-99.7 rule on a skewed column as if it were a test.

</Aside>

```python
answer_2 = [round(float(v), 1) for v in (wd[0], we[0], wd[1], we[1], wd[2], we[2])]
```
