## The big idea

A *distribution* tells how often each value occurs, and its shape, center and spread are read together; a *z-score* then says how far one value sits from the center, in units of spread.

### 1. The shape of a column

**Idea.** A *histogram* counts the values that fall in each slice of the number line. Read its shape: where it peaks, how many humps it has, and which tail is longer. A column is *right skewed* when the long tail points to the high values; `skew()` puts that in one number, positive for a long right tail and negative for a long left one (NIST). The `skew()` of pandas is the adjusted sample skewness; NIST gives the unadjusted formula and notes that many programs compute the adjusted one.

**Try it.** Count the orders in 4-dollar slices, and the skew with and without the largest order:

```python
edges = [0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 200]
slices = pd.cut(totals, edges, right=False).value_counts(sort=False)
print(slices.tolist())
print(round(totals.skew(), 2), round(totals.drop(totals.idxmax()).skew(), 2))
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.hist(totals[totals < 40], bins=edges[:-1])
ax.set_title("Order totals under 40 dollars")
ax.set_xlabel("Order total (USD)")
ax.set_ylabel("Number of orders")
```

**Predict:** count the orders in slices 0 to 4, 4 to 8 and so on, the last slice 40 and over. Which slice will hold the most orders, and is the longer tail on the left or on the right? Will the skew still be that large without the order of 197.00?

### 2. The z-score

**Idea.** The *z-score* of a value is its distance from the mean divided by the standard deviation, $$z = (x - \bar{x}) / s$$, where $$x$$ is the value and $$\bar{x}$$ and $$s$$ are the mean and the standard deviation of its column (dividing by $$n-1$$, as in [*Center and spread*](/core/center-and-spread/) (C2)). It counts how many standard deviations a value lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean, and a larger absolute z-score is a more unusual value (OpenIntro). It ranks how unusual a value is; it does not say why.

**Try it.** The z-scores of the three largest orders, and how many orders lie beyond 3:

```python
z = (totals - totals.mean()) / totals.std()
for row in sales.assign(z=z).nlargest(3, "total").itertuples():
    print(row.order_id, row.store_id, row.total, round(row.z, 2))
print((z.abs() > 3).sum())
```

**Predict:** how many standard deviations above the mean is each of the three largest orders? How many of the 1,799 orders have a z-score above 3 or below minus 3?

For the order of 197.00, with the mean 12.65 and the standard deviation 8.62 of the 1,799 totals:

$$
z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s} = \frac{197.00 - 12.65}{8.62} \approx 21.4
$$

### 3. The empirical rule, for orientation

**Idea.** A normal curve is bell shaped and symmetric (Seeing Theory). On it, about 68, 95 and 99.7 percent of the values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean: the 68-95-99.7 rule of OpenIntro. Use it to get your bearings on a z-score, not as a test: no column is exactly normal, and a value outside the bands is unusual, not wrong.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Read a column's shape, center and spread together.
- A z-score is $$(x - \bar{x}) / s$$: it ranks how unusual a value is, it does not say why.
- The 68-95-99.7 rule fits bell-shaped columns; skewed ones drift away from it.
- Investigate an outlier and write the note; delete it only on evidence.
- Compare two groups on center, spread and shape at once.

**Common trap:** using the 68-95-99.7 rule on a skewed column as if it were a test.

</Aside>
