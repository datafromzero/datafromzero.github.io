## Learn by doing

About 40 minutes, 5 new ideas: the confusion matrix, why accuracy fails on a rare outcome, precision, recall and F1, a threshold from error costs, and MAE and RMSE. The setup cell built `people` of C19 and fitted the honest recipe on the training part of the seed-21 split: `chance` is the model's chance for each of the 5,044 test people, `y_test` their outcomes (607 buyers), and `counts(threshold)` gives `tn, fp, fn, tp` for the rule "flag a person when the chance is at least the threshold".

### 1. The confusion matrix

**Idea.** A model's chance becomes a *call*, a yes or no, once a rule is attached: flag the person when the chance is at least a *threshold*. Each person then falls in one of four cells. A *true positive* (TP) is flagged and bought, a *false positive* (FP) flagged and did not buy, a *false negative* (FN) not flagged and bought, a *true negative* (TN) neither. scikit-learn's `confusion_matrix` puts the true outcome in the rows and the call in the columns.

**Try it:** count the four cells for the rule "flag when the chance is at least 0.5".

```python
cm = confusion_matrix(y_test, chance >= 0.5)         # rows: the true outcome (0, 1); columns: the call (0, 1)
print(cm.tolist())
tn, fp, fn, tp = cm.ravel().tolist()
print(tn, fp, fn, tp)
print(round(float(chance.max()), 3))                  # the largest chance the model gives any test person
```

**Predict:** A person is flagged when the model's chance is at least 0.5. How many of the test people does the model flag? In the confusion matrix, how many are true positives, and where do the buyers sit?

### 2. Why accuracy fails on a rare outcome

**Idea.** *Accuracy* is the share of calls that are right, the true positives and true negatives over all people. It counts a wrong flag and a missed buyer alike, and it counts every person once, so it follows the larger group.

**Try it:** the accuracy of two rules, and of the call "nobody buys".

```python
for t in (0.5, 0.15):
    tn, fp, fn, tp = counts(t)
    print(t, tp + fp, round((tn + tp) / len(y_test), 4))          # threshold, people flagged, accuracy
print(round(float(accuracy_score(y_test, np.zeros(len(y_test), dtype=int))), 4))        # the call "nobody buys"
```

**Predict:** Accuracy is the share of test people called correctly. What does the call "nobody buys" score? What does the model score at the cut 0.5? And a rule that flags everyone with a chance of at least 0.15, which does find buyers: a higher or a lower accuracy?

0.8797

</details>

*Why:* 87.97 percent of the test people did not buy, so the call "nobody buys" is right for 87.97 percent of them, and the model at 0.5, which also flags nobody, scores the same. The rule at 0.15 flags 1,666 people, and its accuracy falls to 66.79 percent: a third of its calls are now wrong, because every flag on a person who does not buy is a wrong call and the non-buyers are the large group. The rule that goes looking for buyers looks worse on accuracy than the rule that finds none. The Crash Course page gives the extreme case: an outcome so rare that a model that always says no is almost always right, and useless.

In symbols, with the four counts of unit 1 at the cut 0.5 (0 true positives, 4,437 true negatives, 0 false positives, 607 false negatives):

$$
\begin{gathered}
\text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} \\
= \frac{0 + 4{,}437}{5{,}044} \approx 0.880
\end{gathered}
$$

### 3. Precision, recall and F1

**Idea.** *Precision* is the share of the flagged people who are buyers: the ability not to flag people who do not buy. *Recall* is the share of the buyers who are flagged: the ability to find the buyers (the scikit-learn page words them as the ability not to label a negative as positive, and to find all the positives). The *F1 score* is their harmonic mean, one number that is low when either is low. Precision improves as false positives fall and recall as false negatives fall, so the two usually pull against each other when the threshold moves.

**Try it:** the three numbers at the threshold 0.15, from the counts and from scikit-learn.

```python
tn, fp, fn, tp = counts(0.15)
precision = tp / (tp + fp)
recall = tp / (tp + fn)
f1 = 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall)
print(tp, fp, fn, tn)
print(round(precision, 3), round(recall, 3), round(f1, 3))
flag = chance >= 0.15
print(round(float(precision_score(y_test, flag)), 3), round(float(recall_score(y_test, flag)), 3), round(float(f1_score(y_test, flag)), 3))
print(round(float(y_test.mean()), 4))                                 # the share of buyers among all test people
```

**Predict:** At the threshold 0.15 the model flags every test person with a chance of at least 0.15. Will the share of flagged people who are buyers (precision) be above or below 50 percent? And the share of all buyers who are flagged (recall)?

*Why:* of the 1,666 people flagged, 299 bought: precision 0.179, about one and a half times the 12.03 percent of buyers among all test people. The flags catch 299 of the 607 buyers: recall 0.493, a little under half. F1 is 0.263, pulled toward the smaller of the two. The counts and the scikit-learn functions give the same numbers.

In symbols, with the counts above:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} = \frac{299}{1{,}666} \\
\text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} = \frac{299}{607} \\
F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}}
\end{gathered}
$$

### 4. A threshold from the cost of each error

**Idea.** A threshold is a choice, and the choice follows what each error costs. Write the two costs and the rule before reading any score, as the ship rule of C14 was written before the data: a false positive costs $$c_{FP}$$, a false negative $$c_{FN}$$, and right calls cost nothing. The total cost of a rule is $$c_{FP} \times FP + c_{FN} \times FN$$, a number of dollars that can be compared across rules, which accuracy cannot do. The people who pay for the decision set the costs, not the data.

**Try it:** the total cost of the rule "flag when the chance is at least one sixth", of the two simple policies, and of every threshold from 0.03 to 0.30.

```python
cost_fp, cost_fn = 1, 5                                                  # assumptions of this example, in dollars


def total_cost(threshold, c_fp=cost_fp, c_fn=cost_fn):
    tn, fp, fn, tp = counts(threshold)
    return fp * c_fp + fn * c_fn


break_even = cost_fp / (cost_fp + cost_fn)
print(round(break_even, 4), total_cost(break_even))
print(int(y_test.sum()) * cost_fn, int((y_test == 0).sum()) * cost_fp)    # message nobody, message everybody
grid = [round(0.03 + 0.01 * k, 2) for k in range(28)]                    # thresholds 0.03 to 0.30
costs = {t: total_cost(t) for t in grid}
best = min(costs, key=costs.get)
print(best, costs[best])
print(round(float(roc_auc_score(y_test, chance)), 3))
```

**Predict:** The rule, written before any score is read (assumptions of this example): a message costs $1 when the person does not buy (a false positive), and a buyer who gets no message costs $5 (a false negative); right calls cost nothing. For the test people, which costs least: messaging nobody, messaging everybody, or messaging the people with a chance of at least 1/6? And on a grid of thresholds from 0.03 to 0.30, is the cheapest one near 1/6 or far from it?

*Why:* messaging the people with a chance of at least 1/6 costs $2,797, against $3,035 for messaging nobody (607 buyers at $5) and $4,437 for messaging everybody (4,437 people at $1). The cheapest threshold on the grid, 0.18, costs $2,792. The best rule saves $238 over 5,044 people against messaging nobody, about 5 cents a person: the model's ranking is weak, and the ROC AUC of 0.657 says so in one number, the area under the ROC curve (scikit-learn's `roc_auc_score`), which ignores the threshold and so cannot say where to cut or what the cut costs. The chance of buying is also not the chance that a message makes someone buy; the $5 assumes it would, and how much a message helps is a question for an A/B test (C14).

The 1/6 comes from comparing, for a person with a true chance $$p$$ of buying, the average cost of flagging with the average cost of not flagging:

$$
\begin{gathered}
(1 - p)\,c_{FP} < p\,c_{FN} \\
\iff p > \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP} + c_{FN}} = \frac{1}{1 + 5} \approx 0.167
\end{gathered}
$$

The rule works to the extent that the chances are close to the true shares of buyers; here they are close to the training shares of their groups (C19), and the cheapest grid threshold, 0.18, is 0.013 from 1/6.

### 5. MAE and RMSE for a numeric target

**Idea.** For a numeric target the miss of a prediction is in the unit of the target, here days. The *mean absolute error* (MAE) is the average of the misses with the sign dropped; the *root mean squared error* (RMSE) squares the misses, averages them and takes the square root, so a long miss counts more, as in the least squares of C11. Neither has a threshold; each judges the number as it is.

**Try it:** among the buyers, give every test buyer one number of days, the training buyers' mean or their median, and score both.

```python
days_train = train.loc[train["bought14"] == 1, "days_to_buy"]            # buyers only: purchases within 14 days of signup
days_test = test.loc[test["bought14"] == 1, "days_to_buy"]
for name, c in (("mean", days_train.mean()), ("median", days_train.median())):
    pred = np.full(len(days_test), c)
    print(name, round(float(c), 2), round(float(mean_absolute_error(days_test, pred)), 3), round(float(root_mean_squared_error(days_test, pred)), 3))
levels = np.arange(0, 3.01, 0.01)                                        # every constant from 0 to 3 days, on the training buyers
mae_by = [mean_absolute_error(days_train, np.full(len(days_train), c)) for c in levels]
rmse_by = [root_mean_squared_error(days_train, np.full(len(days_train), c)) for c in levels]
print(round(float(levels[np.argmin(mae_by)]), 2), round(float(levels[np.argmin(rmse_by)]), 2))
print(int((days_test > 7).sum()), len(days_test))
```

The grid over every constant from 0 to 3 days finds the same on the training buyers: the best constant for MAE is 0.48, the median, and for RMSE 1.73, the mean. Most buyers buy within hours, and 41 of the 607 test buyers waited more than a week: the squares make those waits count. Which metric is right depends on what a miss costs: if every day of error costs the same, MAE; if a long wait costs far more than a short one, RMSE.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- The confusion matrix has four cells: TP, FP, FN, TN. A threshold decides who is flagged, and moving it moves the counts.
- Accuracy follows the larger group: on a rare outcome, the call "nobody" scores high.
- Precision is the share of flagged people who are buyers, recall the share of buyers who are flagged; F1 is their harmonic mean.
- Write the cost of each error first; the total cost of a rule is $$c_{FP} \times FP + c_{FN} \times FN$$, and the break-even chance is $$c_{FP} / (c_{FP} + c_{FN})$$.
- MAE is the typical miss, RMSE punishes long misses; the best constant is the median for MAE and the mean for RMSE.

**Common trap:** judging a model by its accuracy when the outcome is rare. The call "nobody" would have scored the same.

</Aside>
