## The question

The owner asks:

> Two samples of 200 orders gave you 12.45 and 11.34 dollars. Which number should I believe, and how many orders do I need to look at before the average stops jumping around?

The file the samples came from, the first rows again (one row for each order, with its value):

| order_id | store_id | ordered_at | value |
|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 1001 | 2 | 2026-01-01 08:02:00 | 14.00 |

| 1003 | 2 | 2026-01-01 15:47:00 | 12.50 |
| 1004 | 1 | 2026-01-01 16:18:00 | 6.50 |

By the end of this page you can say how far a sample mean is likely to be from the truth, and why more orders help less and less.

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 4 new ideas: a seeded random draw, the sampling distribution of the mean, the standard error, the Central Limit Theorem. The setup cell made `values`, the 1,800 order values as an array, and `mu`, their mean.

<OpenInColab />

### 1. A seeded random draw

**Idea.** `np.random.default_rng(42)` makes a random number generator, and 42 is its seed: the same seed gives the same numbers, as the NumPy documentation says. `rng.choice(values, size=30)` draws 30 values at random and, by default, puts each one back before the next draw, so an order can come up twice and the draws do not depend on each other. We draw this way because the 1,800 orders stand for every order the cafes could take.

**Try it.** Draw two samples of 30 orders, then start again from the same seed:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)         # a random number generator; 42 is its seed
first = rng.choice(values, size=30)     # 30 orders drawn at random; an order can come up twice
second = rng.choice(values, size=30)    # the next 30
print(round(float(first.mean()), 2), round(float(second.mean()), 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)         # the same seed again
print(round(float(rng.choice(values, size=30).mean()), 2))
```

**Predict:** two draws of 30 orders follow each other. Will their means be equal? Then the generator starts again from the same seed: what will the third mean be?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
14.06 10.93
14.06
```

</details>

*Why:* two samples from the same population give different means, 14.06 and 10.93. The third mean is the first one again: the same seed gives the same draws. A seed makes a simulation repeatable. NumPy does not promise the same numbers from a seed across its versions, so the numbers on this page are what the notebook printed with seed 42, and another version may print slightly different ones.

### 2. The sampling distribution of the mean

**Idea.** The sampling distribution of the mean is the pile of means you get from many samples of the same size. We build it by brute force: draw 1,000 samples of 30 orders and keep each sample's mean. `np.histogram` counts how many means fall in each bar of 1 dollar, and the dashed line marks `mu`.

**Try it.** Build the 1,000 means, print them as numbers and draw them:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
means30 = np.array([rng.choice(values, size=30).mean() for _ in range(1000)])   # 1,000 samples, one mean for each
print(round(float(means30.mean()), 2), round(float(means30.std(ddof=1)), 2))
print(round(float(means30.min()), 2), round(float(means30.max()), 2))
edges = np.arange(8, 22.1, 1.0)                                                 # the bars: 1 dollar wide
counts, _ = np.histogram(means30, bins=edges)
print(counts.tolist())
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.hist(means30, bins=edges)
ax.axvline(mu, color="black", linestyle="--")                                   # the mean of all 1,800 orders
ax.set_xlabel("mean of one sample of 30 orders (USD)")
ax.set_ylabel("number of samples")
```

**Predict:** each of the 1,000 values is the mean of 30 orders. Where will they center, how wide will they spread (the SD of single orders is 8.61), and will the histogram be a symmetric bell or lopsided?

### 3. Standard error and standard deviation

**Idea.** Two spreads need two names. The standard deviation (SD) says how far single orders sit from the mean. The standard error (SE) of the mean says how far a sample mean sits from the parameter, and for samples of n orders from a population with SD sigma it is sigma divided by the square root of n, as the OpenIntro textbook gives it. NumPy's `std` divides by n unless you pass `ddof=1`, and pandas' `std` divides by n − 1; by convention n is for a whole population and n − 1 for a sample.

**Try it.** Build the sampling distribution for three sizes and set its SD beside the formula:

```python
sigma = values.std()                    # NumPy divides by n unless told otherwise: right for all 1,800 orders
print(round(float(sigma), 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for n in (30, 120, 480):
    means = np.array([rng.choice(values, size=n).mean() for _ in range(1000)])
    print(n, round(float(means.std(ddof=1)), 2), round(float(sigma / np.sqrt(n)), 2))
```

**Predict:** for samples of 30, 120 and 480 orders, the cell prints the SD of the 1,000 sample means next to the formula. How will the SD of the means change from one size to the next?

*Why:* single orders have an SD of 8.61; the means have an SD of 1.51, 0.81 and 0.40, and the formula gives 1.57, 0.79 and 0.39. Each time n grows four times, the SE falls to about half: it shrinks with the square root of n, so ever more orders buy ever less.

**Idea.** A real study has one sample, not 1,000, so the SE is estimated from it: replace sigma by the sample's own SD, `s`, computed with `ddof=1`.

**Try it.** Estimate the SE from the first sample of 30 of unit 1:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
one = rng.choice(values, size=30)     # the first sample of unit 1
s = one.std(ddof=1)                   # the SD of this one sample, dividing by n - 1
print(round(float(s), 2), round(float(s / np.sqrt(30)), 2))
```

**Predict:** will s divided by the square root of 30 land near the 1.51 that the 1,000 samples gave?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
7.0 1.28
```

</details>

*Why:* this sample has an SD of 7.00 and gives 1.28, a bit under 1.51: the SD of one sample is itself a draw, so one sample gives an estimate of the SE, not the SE. It is still how you put a number on luck when you have only one sample, and *Confidence intervals* (C7) builds on it.

### 4. The Central Limit Theorem

**Idea.** Skewness measures lopsidedness: it is about zero for symmetric data, and positive when there is more weight in the right tail, as the SciPy documentation puts it. Orders are lopsided: most cost under 20 dollars and a few cost far more. We compute the skewness of the orders and of the 1,000 means at three sample sizes.

**Try it.** Print the skewness:

```python
print(round(float(stats.skew(values)), 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for n in (30, 120, 480):
    means = np.array([rng.choice(values, size=n).mean() for _ in range(1000)])
    print(n, round(float(stats.skew(means)), 2))
```

**Predict:** the skewness of the orders themselves is printed first. As the sample size grows from 30 to 120 to 480, will the skewness of the sample means rise, fall or stay near that number?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
6.21
30 0.79
120 0.7
480 0.27
```

*Why:* the orders have a skewness of 6.21, a long right tail. The means have 0.79, 0.70 and 0.27: far lower, and near 0 at 480 orders. That is the Central Limit Theorem in one sentence: **when you average many independent draws, the averages pile up in a bell shape around the population mean, with a spread of sigma divided by the square root of n, whatever the shape of the data, if the sample is large enough.** At 30 and 120 orders the right lean is still there, so how large is large enough depends on how lopsided the data are. The OpenIntro rule of thumb for 30 orders or more asks for no particularly extreme outliers, and the 197.00 order of the file is one.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A sample mean changes with the sample; a seed makes the draw repeat.
- The sampling distribution of the mean centers on the parameter, and its SD is the standard error, sigma divided by the square root of n.
- Halving the standard error takes four times the sample.
- With enough independent draws the means form a bell shape, whatever the data look like.

**Common trap:** using the SD of single orders where the SE of the mean belongs, or expecting twice the sample to halve the SE.

</Aside>
