## The question

The owner asks:

> Two samples of 200 orders gave you 12.45 and 11.34 dollars. Which number should I believe, and how many orders do I need to look at before the average stops jumping around?

The file the samples came from, the first rows again (one row for each order, with its value):

| order_id | store_id | ordered_at | value |
|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 1001 | 2 | 2026-01-01 08:02:00 | 14.00 |
| 1002 | 1 | 2026-01-01 15:38:00 | 12.50 |
| 1003 | 2 | 2026-01-01 15:47:00 | 12.50 |
| 1004 | 1 | 2026-01-01 16:18:00 | 6.50 |

By the end of this page you can say how far a sample mean is likely to be from the truth, and why more orders help less and less.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In *Arrays and vectorized math with NumPy* (P10), a method such as `amount.sum()` turns a whole array into one number. `values.mean()` and `values.std()` do the same, and `rng.choice(values, size=30)` hands you an array to reduce in the same way.

**Diverge.** An array in P10 never changed between runs; the array that `rng.choice` gives is different at every call. Unit 1 shows how to get the same one back.

</Aside>

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 4 new ideas: a seeded random draw, the sampling distribution of the mean, the standard error, the Central Limit Theorem. The setup cell made `values`, the 1,800 order values as an array, and `mu`, their mean.

### 1. A seeded random draw

**Idea.** `np.random.default_rng(42)` makes a random number generator, and 42 is its seed, the number it starts from. `rng.choice(values, size=30)` draws 30 values at random and, by default, puts each one back before the next draw, so an order can come up twice and each draw is independent of the others. We draw this way because the 1,800 orders stand for every order the cafes could take, and because the standard error formula of unit 3 is exact for independent draws.

**Try it.** Draw two samples of 30 orders, then start again from the same seed:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)         # a random number generator; 42 is its seed
first = rng.choice(values, size=30)     # 30 orders drawn at random; an order can come up twice
second = rng.choice(values, size=30)    # the next 30
print(round(float(first.mean()), 2), round(float(second.mean()), 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)         # the same seed again
print(round(float(rng.choice(values, size=30).mean()), 2))
```

**Predict:** two draws of 30 orders follow each other. Will their means be equal? Then the generator starts again from the same seed: what will the third mean be?

### 3. Standard error and standard deviation

**Idea.** Two spreads need two names. The standard deviation (SD) says how far single orders sit from the mean. The standard error (SE) of the mean says how far a sample mean sits from the parameter: it is the SD of the sampling distribution. NumPy's `std` divides by n unless you pass `ddof=1`, and pandas' `std` divides by n − 1; by convention n is for a whole population and n − 1 for a sample.

**Try it.** Build the sampling distribution for three sizes and print its SD:

```python
print(round(float(values.std()), 2), round(float(values.std(ddof=1)), 2))   # the SD of single orders: dividing by n, then by n - 1
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for n in (30, 120, 480):
    means = np.array([rng.choice(values, size=n).mean() for _ in range(1000)])
    print(n, round(float(means.std(ddof=1)), 2))
without = np.array([rng.choice(values, size=480, replace=False).mean() for _ in range(1000)])   # no order twice
print("480 without replacement", round(float(without.std(ddof=1)), 2))
```

**Predict:** the cell prints the SD of single orders two ways, dividing by n and by n − 1, then the SD of the 1,000 sample means for samples of 30, 120 and 480 orders, and last the same for 480 orders drawn without replacement. How will the SD of the means change from one size to the next? Will the last number be larger or smaller than the one before it?

### 4. The Central Limit Theorem

**Idea.** Skewness measures lopsidedness: it is about zero for symmetric data, and positive when there is more weight in the right tail, as the SciPy documentation puts it. Orders are lopsided: most cost under 20 dollars and a few cost far more. We compute the skewness of the orders and of the 1,000 means at three sample sizes.

**Try it.** Print the skewness:

    print(n, round(float(stats.skew(means)), 2))

*Why:* the orders have a skewness of 6.21, a long right tail. The means have 0.79, 0.70 and 0.27: far lower, and near 0 at 480 orders. That is the Central Limit Theorem in one sentence: **when you average many independent draws, the averages pile up in a bell shape around the population mean, with a spread of sigma divided by the square root of n, whatever the shape of the data, if the sample is large enough.** At 30 and 120 orders the right lean is still there, so how large is large enough depends on how lopsided the data are. The OpenIntro rule of thumb for 30 orders or more asks for no particularly extreme outliers, and the 197.00 order of the file is one.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A sample mean changes with the sample; a seed makes the draw repeat.
- The sampling distribution of the mean centers on the parameter, and its SD is the standard error, sigma divided by the square root of n.
- Halving the standard error takes four times the sample.
- With enough independent draws the means form a bell shape, whatever the data look like.

**Common trap:** quoting one sample's mean as if it were the truth, with no number for how far it could be off.

</Aside>
