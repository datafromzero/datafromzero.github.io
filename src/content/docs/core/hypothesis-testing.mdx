1. A test of a new screen gives p = 0.03. Which describes it: (a) the chance that the screen does nothing, (b) the chance of a gap at least this big if the screen does nothing, (c) the chance that the result is right?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   (b). The p-value assumes the screen does nothing, so it cannot be the chance that this is true.

   </details>

2. A test with 40 people in each group finds no significant difference, and a colleague writes "the redesign has no effect". What is wrong with that?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Not significant is not no effect: a small test misses real effects. The interval of the difference shows which sizes the data still allow.

   </details>

3. Two groups of 2,000,000 people convert at 12.0 and 12.1 percent, and p = 0.002. Is it worth acting on?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Not on the p-value alone: with that many people a tiny gap is easy to detect. Report the 0.1 point and weigh it against the cost.

   </details>

4. Cities with more ice-cream sales have more drownings. Name one rival explanation to "ice cream causes drowning".

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   A confounder: hot weather raises both.

   </details>

5. A team looks at an A/B test every day and stops on the first day with p below 0.05. What goes wrong?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Every look is another chance to cross the bar by luck. Fix the sample size before the test starts.

   </details>

6. Weekly signups fell 12 percent. What do you check before looking for a cause?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Whether the fall is real (tracking, an outage, a holiday, a changed definition). Then segment, then look for the driver.

   </details>

## The question

The product manager of a budgeting app asks:

> We put tips on the first screens for six weeks. Did they help people buy the paid plan?

People were assigned at random as they signed up, March 16 to April 26; "bought" means a purchase of Plus within 14 days. The data is the `welcome_tips` experiment of `app-events`, a fictional app.

| group | people | bought Plus | share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| control (the old screens) | 2,449 | 304 | 12.4% |
| variant (the tips) | 2,519 | 306 | 12.1% |

By the end you can say what a <Term id="glossary-p-value">p-value</Term> says about this table, what it leaves out, and how repeated looks make wins that are not there.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Confidence intervals*](/core/confidence-intervals/) (C7), a 95 percent interval holds the values the data do not rule out at the 5 percent level. A test asks that about one value, "no difference".

**Diverge.** A test boils the interval down to one number, the p-value, and unit 2 shows how much that leaves out.

</Aside>

<OpenInColab />

### 1. Say what "nothing is going on" means, then shuffle it

**Idea.** A test starts with two claims, written before you look. The null hypothesis, H0, says nothing is going on: here, the tips do not change the chance to buy. The alternative, H1, says something is: the chance differs, up or down. You also set a bar for calling a p-value small enough, usually 0.05; NIST calls it the significance level, $$\alpha$$. If H0 is true, the label "variant" means nothing, so shuffling the labels makes new splits that H0 could have produced.

**Try it.** Shuffle who is "variant" 10,000 times and record the gap in conversion of each shuffle:

```python
n0, n1 = int((~is_var).sum()), int(is_var.sum())
c0, c1 = int(y[~is_var].sum()), int(y[is_var].sum())
p0, p1 = c0 / n0, c1 / n1                                         # conversion rates
gap = p1 - p0                                                     # variant minus control
print(n0, n1, c0, c1, round(p0, 4), round(p1, 4), round(float(gap), 4))
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
shuffled = np.empty(10_000)
for k in range(10_000):
    mix = rng.permutation(is_var)
    shuffled[k] = y[mix].mean() - y[~mix].mean()
extreme = int((np.abs(shuffled) >= abs(gap)).sum())
print(extreme, round((extreme + 1) / (len(shuffled) + 1), 2))
```

**Predict:** the real gap is the variant's rate minus the control's. Will it sit near the middle of the shuffled gaps or far out in a tail? About how many of the 10,000 shuffles will give a gap at least as far from 0, in either direction?

### 2. What a p-value says, and what it does not

**Idea.** The p-value is the chance, if H0 were true, of a result at least as far from H0 as the one you saw. The spread of the shuffled gaps is the standard error when H0 is true. The test statistic is how many standard errors the real gap lies from the null value, and a bell curve turns that into a p-value.

$$
z = \frac{\text{estimate} - \text{null value}}{\mathrm{SE}}
$$

The estimate is the real gap, the null value is 0, and SE is the spread of the shuffled gaps. The cell also builds the 95 percent interval of the difference, as in lesson C7, with $$\mathrm{SE}_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_0^2 + \mathrm{SE}_1^2}$$, where $$\mathrm{SE}_0$$ and $$\mathrm{SE}_1$$ are the standard errors of the two rates.

**Try it.** Turn the gap into a z-score and a p-value, then build the interval of the difference, in percentage points:

```python

print(round(float(two_prop_p(c0, n0, c1, n1)), 3))
```

Three things a p-value is not, from the principles of the American Statistical Association (ASA):

- **Not the probability that H0 is true.** It was computed by assuming H0; the ASA says p-values do not measure that probability, nor the probability that the data were produced by chance alone.
- **Not the size or importance of an effect**, the ASA says.
- **Not a verdict.** The ASA says decisions should not rest only on whether a p-value passes a threshold. Here the p-value of 0.79, from the shuffles of unit 1, does not say there is no lift: the interval's top end is +1.56 points, so a lift of up to 1.6 points is not ruled out.

### 3. Two ways to be wrong, and power

**Idea.** A test can fail in two ways.

| | the test says significant | the test says not significant |
|---|---|---|
| nothing is going on | false alarm (Type I error) | correct |
| something is going on | correct: the real effect is caught | miss (Type II error) |

<Term id="glossary-statistical-power">**Power**</Term> is the chance of catching a real effect of a stated size: one minus the chance of a miss. The cell simulates experiments with no effect and with a real lift of 2 points, 12.4 to 14.4 percent.

**Try it.** Simulate 20,000 experiments at each group size and print the false-alarm rate and the power at two bars (seed 1; another NumPy version can move the last digit):

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
p_base = 0.124


def experiments(size, p_variant, reps=20_000):
    """p-values of `reps` simulated experiments with `size` people in each group."""
    buyers0 = rng.binomial(size, p_base, size=reps)
    buyers1 = rng.binomial(size, p_variant, size=reps)
    return two_prop_p(buyers0, size, buyers1, size)


table = {}
for size in (1250, 2500, 5000, 10000):
    none = experiments(size, 0.124)
    lift = experiments(size, 0.144)
    table[size] = (float((none < 0.05).mean()), [float((lift < a).mean()) for a in (0.05, 0.01)])
    if size == 2500:
        first_100 = none[:100]
    print(size, round(table[size][0], 2), [round(share, 2) for share in table[size][1]])
```

**Predict:** in the experiments with no effect, what share will have p below 0.05? In the experiments with a real lift of 2 points, how does the share caught change when each group grows from 1,250 to 10,000 people, and when the bar goes from 0.05 to 0.01?

*Why:* of 100 no-effect experiments, 5 are red, as about 5 are expected. The 55 percent rests on three assumptions: 1 test in 10 is real, its lift is exactly 2 points, and the power is about 0.54, as found above. Then 1,000 tests give 54 real wins (100 × 0.54) and 45 false ones (900 × 0.05):

$$
\frac{\text{real wins}}{\text{real wins} + \text{false wins}} = \frac{54}{54 + 45} \approx 0.55
$$

Only about half of the significant results are real, not 95 percent: the base-rate problem of lesson C4. Change an assumption and the share changes.

### 4. Looking again and again

**Idea.** The 5 percent belongs to one test, planned in advance and read once at its end. Every extra look is another chance to cross the bar by luck. Two ways: read the running result each day and stop when it is under 0.05 (optional stopping), or cut the people into segments and report one that is (p-hacking). Researchers who study online experiments (Johari and colleagues, in the sources) write that the usual p-values and intervals are wholly unreliable when users choose the sample size by continuously monitoring. $$
1 - (1 - \alpha)^m = 1 - 0.95^{12} \approx 0.460
$$

where $$m = 12$$ is the number of tests. The three splits cut the same people, so the tests are not independent and 0.46 is only a guide. Dividing the bar by the number of tests (the Bonferroni correction), $$\alpha / m = 0.05 / 12 \approx 0.0042$$, is the simplest repair, and neither segment survives it. The ASA's fourth principle, that proper inference requires full reporting and transparency, adds: report how many cuts you made, not only the one that worked. An assistant asked to "find what the tips changed" runs the same search, so ask how many cuts it tried.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- H0 says nothing is going on and H1 says something is; write both, and the bar, before you look.
- A p-value is the share of luck-only results as far from H0 as yours: not the chance that H0 is true, not the size of the effect, not a verdict.
- The bar $$\alpha$$ sets the false-alarm rate; power, the chance to catch a real effect, grows with more people and falls with a stricter bar.
- The 5 percent belongs to one planned test: every look and every segment adds chances, so count them.

**Common trap:** stopping at the first p below 0.05, or reporting the one segment under it: day 12 (p = 0.022), iOS and web (0.024 and 0.027) all came from tips that do nothing.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="core-hypothesis-testing-1">

**Did the tips change how many people finish onboarding?** Finishing onboarding is an `onboarding_complete` event. Count the shuffles at least as far from 0 as the real gap, either way, and turn the count into a p-value: (count + 1) / (10,000 + 1), rounded to 2 decimals. Expected: `answer_1` is a number between 0 and 1.

```python
finished = tips["user_id"].isin(events.loc[events["event_name"] == "onboarding_complete", "user_id"]).to_numpy().astype(int)    # given: 1 = finished

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
shuffled_f = np.array([finished[m].mean() - finished[~m].mean() for m in (rng.permutation(is_var) for _ in range(10_000))])   # given: the shuffles

```
