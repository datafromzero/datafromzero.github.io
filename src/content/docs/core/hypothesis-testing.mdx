1. A test of a new screen gives p = 0.03. Which describes it: (a) the chance that the screen does nothing, (b) the chance of a gap at least this big if the screen does nothing, (c) the chance that the result is right?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   (b). The p-value is computed by assuming the screen does nothing, so it cannot be the chance that this assumption is true.

   </details>

2. A test with 40 people in each group finds no significant difference, and a colleague writes "the redesign has no effect". What is wrong with that?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   "Not significant" is not "no effect". A small test misses real effects, and the interval of the difference shows how big a one the data still allow.

   </details>

3. Two groups of 2,000,000 people convert at 12.0 and 12.1 percent, and p = 0.002. Is it worth acting on?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Not because of the p-value. Significant does not mean important: with that many people a tiny gap is easy to detect. Report the size, 0.1 point, and weigh it against the cost.

   </details>

4. Cities with more ice-cream sales have more drownings. Name one rival explanation to "ice cream causes drowning".

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   A confounder: hot weather raises both. Anything tied to both numbers can make a pattern that no cause links.

   </details>

5. A team looks at an A/B test every day and stops on the first day with p below 0.05. What goes wrong?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Every look is another chance to cross the bar by luck, so the test calls winners that are not there. Fix the sample size before the test starts.

   </details>

6. Weekly signups fell 12 percent. What do you check before looking for a cause?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Whether the fall is real: a tracking change, an outage, a holiday or a changed definition. Only then split it by segment and look for the driver.

   </details>

## The question

The product manager of a budgeting app asks:

> We put tips on the first screens for six weeks. Did they help people buy the paid plan?

People were assigned at random as they signed up, from March 16 to April 26, and "bought" means a purchase of Plus within 14 days. The data is the `welcome_tips` experiment of `app-events`, a fictional app with synthetic data.

| group | people | bought Plus | share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| control (the old screens) | 2,449 | 304 | 12.4% |
| variant (the tips) | 2,519 | 306 | 12.1% |

By the end you can say what a p-value says about this table, what it leaves out, and how looking again and again at the same data makes wins that are not there.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Confidence intervals*](/core/confidence-intervals/) (C7), a 95 percent interval holds the values that the data do not rule out at the 5 percent level. A test asks that question about one value, "no difference".

**Diverge.** An interval gives a range. A test boils it down to one number, the p-value, and unit 2 shows how much that number leaves out.

</Aside>

## Learn by doing

About 40 minutes, 4 new ideas: the null and the shuffle, the p-value, the two errors and power, and looking again and again. The setup cell gave `tips`, one row for each of the 4,968 people of the experiment, with `is_var` (True for the 2,519 who saw the tips) and `y` (1 if the person bought Plus within 14 days).

<OpenInColab />

### 1. Say what "nothing is going on" means, then shuffle it

**Idea.** A test starts with two claims, written before you look. The null hypothesis, H0, says nothing is going on: here, the tips do not change the chance to buy. The alternative, H1, says something is: the chance differs, up or down. You also set a bar for how small the p-value must be to call a result significant, usually 0.05; NIST calls it the significance level, written $$\alpha$$. If H0 is true, the label "variant" means nothing, so shuffling the labels makes new splits that H0 could have produced.

**Try it.** Shuffle who is "variant" 10,000 times and record the gap in conversion of each shuffle:

```python
n0, n1 = int((~is_var).sum()), int(is_var.sum())                  # people in control and in the variant
c0, c1 = int(y[~is_var].sum()), int(y[is_var].sum())              # buyers in control and in the variant
p0, p1 = c0 / n0, c1 / n1                                         # conversion rates
gap = p1 - p0                                                     # variant minus control
print(n0, n1, c0, c1, round(p0, 4), round(p1, 4), round(float(gap), 4))
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
shuffled = np.empty(10_000)
for k in range(10_000):
    mix = rng.permutation(is_var)                                 # shuffle who is "variant": now the label means nothing
    shuffled[k] = y[mix].mean() - y[~mix].mean()                  # the gap this shuffle produces
extreme = int((np.abs(shuffled) >= abs(gap)).sum())               # shuffles at least as far from 0 as the real gap, either way
print(extreme, round((extreme + 1) / (len(shuffled) + 1), 3))                    # the p-value: the share of shuffles that are this extreme
```

**Predict:** the real gap is the variant's rate minus the control's. Will it sit near the middle of the shuffled gaps or far out in a tail? About how many of the 10,000 shuffles will give a gap at least as far from 0, in either direction?

### 2. What a p-value says, and what it does not

**Idea.** The p-value is the chance, if H0 were true, of a result at least as far from H0 as the one you saw. The spread of the shuffled gaps is the standard error when H0 is true. The test statistic counts how many of them the real gap lies from the null value, and a bell curve turns that count into a p-value.

$$
z = \frac{\text{estimate} - \text{null value}}{\mathrm{SE}}
$$

Here the estimate is the real gap, the null value is 0 (no difference), and SE is the spread of the shuffled gaps. The cell also builds the 95 percent interval of the difference, as in lesson C7, with $$\mathrm{SE}_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_0^2 + \mathrm{SE}_1^2}$$, where $$\mathrm{SE}_0$$ and $$\mathrm{SE}_1$$ are the standard errors of the two rates.

**Try it.** Turn the gap into a z-score and a p-value, then build the interval of the difference, in percentage points:

```python
se_null = shuffled.std(ddof=1)                                   # the spread of the shuffled gaps: the standard error if nothing is going on
z = gap / se_null                                                # how many standard errors from 0 the real gap lies
p_norm = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))                               # both tails of the bell curve
print(round(float(se_null), 4), round(float(z), 3), round(float(p_norm), 3))
print(round(float(two_prop_p(c0, n0, c1, n1)), 3))               # the formula of the next lesson, the same idea
se_diff = np.sqrt(p0 * (1 - p0) / n0 + p1 * (1 - p1) / n1)       # the standard error of the difference, from the two groups
low, high = gap - 1.96 * se_diff, gap + 1.96 * se_diff           # the 95 percent interval of the difference
print(round(float(100 * gap), 2), round(float(100 * low), 2), round(float(100 * high), 2))      # in percentage points
```

Three things a p-value is not, from the principles of the American Statistical Association (ASA):

- **Not the probability that H0 is true.** It was computed by assuming H0. The ASA says p-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or that the data were produced by chance alone.
- **Not the size or importance of an effect.** The ASA says a p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
- **Not a verdict.** The ASA says conclusions and business decisions should not rest only on whether a p-value passes a threshold. Here 0.78 leaves a lift of 1.5 points possible.

### 3. Two ways to be wrong, and power

**Idea.** A test can fail in two ways, and each has a name.

| | the test says significant | the test says not significant |
|---|---|---|
| nothing is going on | false alarm (Type I error) | correct |
| something is going on | correct: the real effect is caught | miss (Type II error) |

**Power** is the chance of catching a real effect of a stated size: one minus the chance of a miss. The cell simulates experiments, once with no effect and once with a real lift of 2 points, from 12.4 to 14.4 percent.

**Try it.** Simulate 2,000 experiments at each group size and print the false-alarm rate and the power at two bars (seed 1; the numbers can move by a few thousandths with another NumPy version):

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
p_base = 0.124                                                   # a baseline conversion rate: control's 12.4 percent


def experiments(size, p_variant, reps=2000):
    """p-values of `reps` simulated experiments with `size` people in each group."""
    buyers0 = rng.binomial(size, p_base, size=reps)
    buyers1 = rng.binomial(size, p_variant, size=reps)
    return two_prop_p(buyers0, size, buyers1, size)


table = {}
for size in (1250, 2500, 5000, 10000):
    none = experiments(size, 0.124)                              # no effect: the null is true
    lift = experiments(size, 0.144)                              # a real lift of 2 points, 12.4 to 14.4 percent
    table[size] = (float((none < 0.05).mean()), [float((lift < a).mean()) for a in (0.05, 0.01)])
    if size == 2500:
        first_100 = none[:100]                                   # kept for the next cell
    print(size, round(table[size][0], 3), [round(share, 3) for share in table[size][1]])
```

**Predict:** in the experiments with no effect, what share will have p below 0.05, whatever the size? In the experiments with a real lift of 2 points, how does the share caught change when each group grows from 1,250 to 10,000 people, and when the bar goes from 0.05 to 0.01?

### 4. Looking again and again

**Idea.** The 5 percent belongs to one test, planned in advance and read once at its planned end. Every extra look is another chance to cross the bar by luck. Two ways to look again: read the running result each day and stop when it is under 0.05 (optional stopping), or cut the people into segments and report one that is (p-hacking). Johari and colleagues write that the usual p-values and intervals are wholly unreliable when users choose the sample size by continuously monitoring a test. The dataset's documentation says the tips do nothing for anyone, so every significant result below is a false alarm; real work gives no such certainty.

$$

$$

where $$m = 12$$ is the number of tests. The three splits cut the same people, so the tests are not independent and 0.46 is only a guide to the size. Dividing the bar by the number of tests, $$\alpha / m = 0.05 / 12 \approx 0.0042$$, is the simplest repair, and neither segment survives it. An assistant asked to "find what the tips changed" runs the same search, so ask how many cuts it tried.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- H0 says nothing is going on and H1 says something is; write both, and the bar, before you look.
- A p-value is the share of luck-only results as far from H0 as yours. It is not the chance that H0 is true, not the size of the effect and not a verdict.
- The bar $$\alpha$$ sets the false-alarm rate; power is the chance to catch a real effect, and it grows with more people and falls with a stricter bar.
- The 5 percent belongs to one planned test: every look and every segment adds chances, so count them.

**Common trap:** stopping at the first p below 0.05, or reporting the one segment under it: here day 12 (p = 0.022) and iOS and web (p = 0.024 and 0.027) all came from tips that do nothing.

</Aside>

def power_at(size, p_variant, p_control=0.124, reps=2000):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
    buyers0 = rng.binomial(size, p_control, size=reps)
    buyers1 = rng.binomial(size, p_variant, size=reps)
    return float((two_prop_p(buyers0, size, buyers1, size) < 0.05).mean())
