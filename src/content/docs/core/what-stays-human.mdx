## The question

An assistant wrote this week's report for the three cafes, and every number in it passed the five checks of *Trust, but verify: checking AI output* (C38). The owner asks:

> So what is left for me to do?

Next week the assistant offers to:

| the offer |
|---|
| decide what "regular customer" means |
| write every query |
| decide whether Harbor's weaker June matters |
| email the report to the supplier, customer list attached |

By the end you can sort twelve data tasks into three piles, and say who answers for each part of the job.

## Learn by doing

About 35 minutes, 5 new ideas: who sets the question and its words, how to sort the work, who answers, when to stop, and what never goes into a tool. The SQL runs in the page, each block complete on its own. Every assistant answer here was written for this lesson; none is a real transcript. Treat the customers table as if it described real people.

### 1. The question and its words are yours

**Idea.** A question such as "who are our regular customers?" has no answer until someone says what *regular* means. An assistant picks a meaning without telling you, and sounds as sure whichever it picked. Choose the meaning yourself, write it in one sentence, and put the sentence in the data dictionary of your context pack from *Prompting with the data in the room* (C37).

**Try it.** Three plausible meanings, counted over customers with completed orders: the gold loyalty tier; ten or more completed orders; at least one completed order in each of the six months. The last column counts customers who meet all three.

<RunSQL query={`WITH per_customer AS (
  SELECT customer_id,
         COUNT(*) AS orders,
         COUNT(DISTINCT date_trunc('month', ordered_at)) AS months
  FROM orders
  WHERE status = 'completed' AND customer_id IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM customers WHERE loyalty_tier = 'gold') AS gold_tier,
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM per_customer WHERE orders >= 10) AS ten_orders,
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM per_customer WHERE months = 6) AS every_month,
  (SELECT COUNT(*)
   FROM per_customer p
   JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = p.customer_id
   WHERE c.loyalty_tier = 'gold' AND p.orders >= 10 AND p.months = 6) AS all_three;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 4 columns.

**Predict:** Which meaning gives the most customers, and how many customers meet all three?

*Why:* 15, 86 and 50, and only 3 for all three. No meaning is wrong: each answers another question, who earns the reward, who comes often, who comes steadily. An offer sent to "regular customers" reaches different people under each, so the sentence that fixes the meaning is part of the answer.

### 2. Sort the work into three piles

**Idea.** Ask three things of a task. Can you check the result against something outside the model, quickly? What does a wrong result cost? Must it stay with you? From the answers come three piles. **Delegate**: the assistant drafts, and you run the checks of C38. **Do it yourself**: you decide, or checking would cost more than doing; the Missing Semester course at MIT (2026) puts it this way: "Sometimes verifying code can be harder than writing the code yourself; for critical code, consider writing it by hand." **Never delegate**: handing it over is itself the mistake.

**Try it.** Sort these twelve tasks of the cafe job into the three piles:

| # | task |
|---|---|
| 1 | Draft the SQL for completed orders by store and month. |
| 2 | Decide that "regular customer" means ten or more orders. |
| 3 | Paste the loyalty list, with names and emails, to get a mailing drafted. |
| 4 | Explain a DuckDB error message and propose a fix. |
| 5 | Decide whether the 20-unit line of order 2075 is a typo or a real bulk order. |
| 6 | Write the first version of the weekly memo from numbers you already checked. |
| 7 | Sign the report that goes to the supplier. |
| 8 | Choose the one number that will judge the new menu. |
| 9 | Check the assistant's query against your schema. |
| 10 | Draft a first bar chart of orders by month. |
| 11 | Tell the Harbor manager that her first three months cannot be compared with the older cafes yet. |
| 12 | Tidy the column names of a CSV file. |

**Predict:** Which tasks go in each pile, and how many piles of each size?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the sort</summary>

| pile | tasks | why |
|---|---|---|
| delegate | 1, 4, 6, 10, 12 | Each result is cheap to check: count and reconcile (1); run the fix (4); trace each number (6); compare chart and table (10); read the new names (12). |
| do it yourself | 2, 5, 8, 9, 11 | A definition (2); a fact held by the staff, not the file (5); what the cafe wants (8); a model's check of itself is no check (9); a conversation that builds trust (11). |
| never delegate | 3, 7 | Personal data stays out of a tool (unit 5); a signature is the act of answering (unit 3). |

</details>

*Why:* five, five and two. The lines move with cost: a cheaper check lets a task go to the first pile, a costlier mistake pulls it to the second. The third pile stays put.

### 3. The person who signs answers

**Idea.** An assistant cannot be held to account, and it signs nothing. The person whose name is on a result answers for it, even when the assistant wrote every line. Put that name in the log of C38 with two more columns: who signed, and what was not checked.

| answer | verdict | signed | not checked |
|---|---|---|---|
| loyalty tier, query revised | accept | your name, 2026-11-23 | whether "loyalty tier" is the owner's meaning of loyal |

**Try it.** Vaccaro and colleagues (*Nat. Hum. Behav.*, October 2024) pooled 106 experiments of people working with AI, with 370 effect sizes in all.

**Predict:** On average, did a person and an AI together beat the better of the two working alone?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

No. On average the pair did worse than the better of the two alone (Hedges' g = -0.23, a standardized difference). The pair gained where the person alone had beaten the AI, and lost where the AI alone had beaten the person.

</details>

*Why:* the pair does well where you are the stronger party: you know the work well enough to catch what the assistant gets wrong, which is what the earlier parts of this roadmap were for. Where you work, someone may have to help: Article 4 of the EU AI Act tells providers and deployers of AI systems to take measures that support the AI literacy of their staff, without requiring any one person to reach a set level.

### 4. Stop when the data cannot answer

**Idea.** Decide before you start what stopping looks like: the same check fails twice, or the question needs something the five tables do not hold. The assistant will not stop on its own; it writes a reason either way. Your claim stops where the data stops.

**Try it.** An assistant wrote to the owner: "Harbor is losing customers: its orders fell in June because the novelty wore off." Harbor is store 3. This query counts its completed orders and its customers with an id, by month:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT date_trunc('month', ordered_at) AS month,
       COUNT(*) AS orders,
       COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS customers
FROM orders
WHERE store_id = 3 AND status = 'completed'
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month;`} />

Expected: 3 rows, 3 columns.

**Predict:** Which of the three claims does the table support: orders fell, customers fell, the novelty wore off?

*Why:* orders went 123, 121, 111, so they fell in June. Customers went 56, 62, 56: June equals April, so "losing customers" is not in the table. No column records why anyone orders, so "novelty" cannot be checked, and three months are too few to call a trend. The honest stop: orders were lower in June, customers were not, the reason is unknown, ask the staff.

### 5. Personal data stays out of the tool

**Idea.** Personal data is any information that relates to an identified or identifiable living person; the European Commission lists a name and surname, a home address and an IP address among its examples. A prompt travels to a service you do not run. Never paste personal data into a tool, and do not paste your employer's confidential data either.

**Try it.** A real cafe's customer export has seven columns: `customer_id`, `full_name`, `email`, `phone`, `signup_date`, `city`, `loyalty_tier`.

**Predict:** Which of its real rows may you paste into a free assistant?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the answer</summary>

None. Paste the column names and types, and sample rows you invented, as the context pack of C37 allows. The assistant needs the shape of the data, never the people in it.

</details>

*Why:* the context pack asks for sample rows, so the pack is where this rule bites. Invented rows show the assistant the same columns at no risk. Exercise 3 tests a belief that tempts people to paste anyway.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- You set the question and the words, one sentence each, in your pack.
- Delegate what you can check outside the model; do yourself what you must decide; never delegate what is itself the mistake.
- The person who signs answers: the log holds a name and what was not checked.
- Stop when the same check fails twice or the data cannot hold the answer.
- Never paste personal data: paste the schema and invented rows.

**Common trap:** letting the assistant pick a word. Its meaning of "regular" or "lost" is a choice that moves the count, and nobody chose it.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="core-what-stays-human-1">

**Which cafe has the most regular customers?** An assistant answered with the query below, which runs: a regular has at least 5 completed orders at that cafe. Run it. Then finish the job: count customers under a second meaning, a customer's home cafe is the one where they ordered most (on a tie, the lower `store_id`), and say which answer you would send and why that choice is yours. Expected: 3 rows, 2 columns (`store_name`, `customers`) for each query.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT s.store_name, COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM (
  SELECT store_id, customer_id
  FROM orders
  WHERE status = 'completed' AND customer_id IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY store_id, customer_id
  HAVING COUNT(*) >= 5
) AS regulars
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = regulars.store_id
GROUP BY s.store_name
ORDER BY s.store_name;`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Count each customer's completed orders at each cafe, number each customer's cafes from the most orders, and keep number 1.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

```sql
WITH visits AS (
  SELECT customer_id, store_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
  FROM orders
  WHERE status = 'completed' AND customer_id IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY customer_id, store_id
)
SELECT *, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY orders DESC, store_id) AS rn
FROM visits
-- keep rn = 1, join stores, count by store_name
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
WITH visits AS (
  SELECT customer_id, store_id, COUNT(*) AS orders
  FROM orders
  WHERE status = 'completed' AND customer_id IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY customer_id, store_id
), ranked AS (
  SELECT *, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY orders DESC, store_id) AS rn
  FROM visits
)
SELECT s.store_name, COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM ranked r
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = r.store_id
WHERE r.rn = 1
GROUP BY s.store_name
ORDER BY s.store_name;
```

```text
store_name  customers
Campus      45
Harbor      54
Old Town    45
```

The first query gives Campus 37, Harbor 36 and Old Town 37, so Harbor is last. The second gives Harbor 54, first by nine customers. The same question has two leaders, because "regular" has two meanings. Three customers tie for most orders between cafes, and the tie rule sends them to the lower `store_id`: a second choice that someone made. Verdict: **revise**. The answer must say which meaning it used, and you pick the one that fits what the owner will do with it.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## On your own

<Exercise id="core-what-stays-human-2">

**Why did ten customers not come in June?** The owner asks for the customers whose last completed order was before June 1. Before you start, write what you expect in words: how many rows, and which columns the five tables could give you. List the customers with their id, tier, city, signup date, date of last order and number of orders, then write three sentences: what the table shows, what it cannot show, and your decision, stop or ask someone. Expected: 10 rows, 6 columns.

<RunSQL query={`-- Write your query here, then press Run.`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Group the completed orders by customer. A customer's last order is the maximum of `ordered_at`; a condition on an aggregate goes in `HAVING`.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"Ten customers, their last orders between late April and the last day of May, mostly with more than three orders each."* Write the question of the third sentence before you look at the table: which column of the five could tell you why?

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT c.customer_id, c.loyalty_tier, c.city, c.signup_date,
       CAST(MAX(o.ordered_at) AS DATE) AS last_order, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM customers c
JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY c.customer_id, c.loyalty_tier, c.city, c.signup_date
HAVING MAX(o.ordered_at) < '2026-06-01'
ORDER BY last_order, c.customer_id;
```

```text
customer_id  loyalty_tier  city     signup_date  last_order  orders
39           bronze        Alameda  2025-09-07   2026-04-25  5
48           bronze        Alameda  2025-09-22   2026-05-01  4
15           silver        Oakland  2025-06-29   2026-05-11  16
26           silver        Oakland  2025-07-26   2026-05-20  14
85           bronze        Alameda  2025-12-27   2026-05-27  7
98           gold          Oakland  2026-02-05   2026-05-28  16
90           silver        Alameda  2026-01-09   2026-05-29  7
45           silver        Alameda  2025-09-19   2026-05-30  4
29           bronze        Alameda  2025-08-06   2026-05-31  3
```

A sample answer: *Ten customers have no completed order in June; their last orders fall between April 25 and May 31. The table cannot show why: the five tables hold no reason, and data that ends on June 30 cannot tell a customer who left from one who returns in July. Decision: stop here and ask the cafe staff; an assistant's reason would be a guess.* Any decision that names who to ask or what to collect is right.

</Solution>

<Exercise id="core-what-stays-human-3">

**Spot the flaw.** An analyst removed `full_name`, `email` and `phone` from the customers table and pasted the rest, `customer_id`, `signup_date`, `city` and `loyalty_tier`, into a free assistant. Her note said: "No names, so it is not personal data." Run the query. How many of the 150 customers are the only one with their city, signup date and tier, and what is the flaw? Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

<RunSQL query={`WITH groups AS (
  SELECT city, signup_date, loyalty_tier, COUNT(*) AS customers
  FROM customers
  GROUP BY city, signup_date, loyalty_tier
)
SELECT SUM(customers) FILTER (WHERE customers = 1) AS alone,
       SUM(customers) AS all_customers
FROM groups;`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think of someone who knows when a friend joined the loyalty program and where she lives. What would they find in the rows?

</details>

138 of the 150 customers are the only one with their city, signup date and tier, so anyone who knows those facts about a person can pick out the row. The European Commission's page says that different pieces of information, which together can lead to the identification of a particular person, may also be personal data. Removing the names did not remove the people, and `customer_id` still points to a table that holds them. The fix is the rule of unit 5: paste the column names and invented rows. Coarser groups help but settle nothing: by city and tier alone the smallest group has 2 customers, and the rows are still real people. Verdict: **reject**.
