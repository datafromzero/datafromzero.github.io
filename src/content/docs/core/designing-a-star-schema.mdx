## The question

The owner brings four questions to Monday's meeting:

> 1. Which payment method do customers prefer at each cafe?
> 2. Does the average order of a loyalty customer depend on the tier: bronze, silver or gold?
> 3. Do weekends sell more coffee than weekdays?
> 4. How many units does an order hold at each cafe, on weekdays and on weekends?

Today every report joins the five tables again, and two people have already reached different totals. The owner wants one model, built once, that all four questions run on. The data is `cafe-sales`, the fictional chain.

By the end you can turn questions like these into the grain, the fact table, the dimensions and their keys, build the star in DuckDB, and check it.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Star schema: facts and dimensions*](/sql/star-schema/) (S29) you built a fact table with one row for each order line and dimensions around it, and checked that a join kept every line.

**Diverge.** There the tables were handed to you, with the keys of the source. Here the questions choose them, the dimensions get keys of their own, and unit 2 shows what that changes.

</Aside>

## Warm-up

From memory first, then click to check.

1. In [*Grain, facts and dimensions*](/core/grain-facts-and-dimensions/) (C17), what does the grain of a fact table say, and which one do you choose when several questions need different detail?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   What one row stands for. Choose the finest grain the questions need, because additive measures such as units and sales can be added up from it into the coarser ones.

   </details>

2. In [*Star schema: facts and dimensions*](/sql/star-schema/) (S29), write the query that checks that joining a fact table `fact_sales` to a dimension `dim_store` neither drops nor repeats a line, and say what you compare it with.

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   `SELECT COUNT(*) FROM fact_sales AS f JOIN dim_store AS s ON s.store_id = f.store_id;` The count must equal the rows of `fact_sales`, 3,287.

   </details>

### 1. From questions to the grain

**Idea.** Kimball Group lists four decisions of a dimensional design, in this order: the business process, the grain, the dimensions and the facts. Here the process is selling. For each question, find the number it asks for and the words after "by". The numbers are the facts, the "by" words are the dimensions, and the finest detail any question needs sets the grain.

**Predict:** for each of the four questions, which number does it ask for and which words follow "by"? Then: should one row of the fact table stand for an order or for an order line? The next query tests one of the two answers.

**Try it.** How many orders hold products of more than one category?

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) AS orders,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE categories > 1) AS in_two_or_more_categories
FROM (SELECT i.order_id, COUNT(DISTINCT p.category) AS categories
      FROM order_items AS i
      JOIN products AS p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
      GROUP BY i.order_id);`} />

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* 862 of the 1,800 orders hold two or more categories. Questions 1, 2 and 4 count orders and could be answered from one row for each order. Question 3 asks for coffee sales, and an order with a coffee and a croissant cannot be given one category. The finest detail any question needs is the line, so the fact table gets one row for each order line. Here is the reading of all four questions:

| question | the number it asks for | the "by" words |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | orders | cafe, payment method |
| 2 | average amount of an order | loyalty tier |
| 3 | sales of coffee | weekend or weekday, product category |
| 4 | units for each order | cafe, weekend or weekday |

The facts are quantity and the amount of a line, and a count of orders by distinct order id (C17). The dimensions are the cafe, the customer, the day, the product and the payment: five. A dimension exists to slice the facts by, so a small table such as the payment (three values) can be a dimension. In [*Modeling the domain before the tables*](/core/modeling-the-domain/) (C16) it was only a column; making it a dimension is a design choice, and the star would also work with the payment as a plain column, which is how unit 3 leaves it for now.

### 2. The keys of a dimension

**Idea.** Give each dimension a *surrogate key*: an integer the warehouse assigns, with no meaning, kept beside the *natural key* of the source (`store_id`). Kimball Group give the reasons: natural keys can repeat when a dimension keeps history, they can clash between source systems, and surrogate keys let the warehouse own its keys. The fact table stores the surrogate key. The same page says that the date dimension can take a more meaningful key; this lesson uses the date written as a number, 20260101.

**Try it.** Build the dimensions of cafe, customer and day. The first two get their keys in order of name or id:

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE dim_store AS
SELECT CAST(row_number() OVER (ORDER BY store_name) AS INTEGER) AS store_key, store_id, store_name, city
FROM stores;

CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE dim_customer AS
SELECT 0 AS customer_key, CAST(NULL AS INTEGER) AS customer_id, 'unknown' AS loyalty_tier, 'unknown' AS city
UNION ALL
SELECT CAST(row_number() OVER (ORDER BY customer_id) AS INTEGER), customer_id, loyalty_tier, city
FROM customers;

CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE dim_date AS
SELECT CAST(strftime(d, '%Y%m%d') AS INTEGER) AS date_key, CAST(d AS DATE) AS full_date,
       month(d) AS month, dayname(d) AS weekday, isodow(d) >= 6 AS is_weekend
FROM generate_series(TIMESTAMP '2026-01-01', TIMESTAMP '2026-06-30', INTERVAL 1 DAY) AS t(d);

SELECT 'built' AS note;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 1 column: the note `built`.

**Try it.** The rule of a dimension is one row for each key. Check it, with the rows next to the distinct keys:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT 'dim_store' AS dimension, COUNT(*) AS rows, COUNT(DISTINCT store_key) AS distinct_keys FROM dim_store
UNION ALL SELECT 'dim_customer', COUNT(*), COUNT(DISTINCT customer_key) FROM dim_customer
UNION ALL SELECT 'dim_date', COUNT(*), COUNT(DISTINCT date_key) FROM dim_date
ORDER BY dimension;`} />

**Predict:** the source has 150 customers and the days run from January 1 to June 30. How many rows does each dimension have?

Expected: 3 rows, 3 columns.

*Why:* 3 stores, 151 customers and 181 days, each with as many distinct keys as rows. The 151 are the 150 customers and one more: an *unknown member*, a row with key 0 for an event whose customer is missing, so that it has a row to point at (key 0 is this lesson's convention, not the Kimball page's). Now look at the store keys:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT * FROM dim_store ORDER BY store_key;`} />

Expected: 3 rows, 4 columns. *Why:* Campus has `store_key` 1 and `store_id` 2. The surrogate key follows the alphabet and the source key follows the order in which the cafes opened, so the two numbers differ on purpose.

### 3. The fact table, and the checks that come with it

**Idea.** The fact table has one row for each order line, the key of each dimension, the order id (a degenerate dimension, as in C17) and the measures, and nothing descriptive. You build it by joining each source row to each dimension on the natural key and keeping the surrogate key. A `LEFT JOIN` keeps the line of an order with no customer, and `COALESCE` sends it to key 0. Kimball Group describe a fact table the same way: numeric measures and foreign keys to its dimensions.

**Try it.** Build the fact table. `product_id` and `payment_method` stay as plain columns for now; the project step turns them into dimensions.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE fact_sale AS
SELECT CAST(strftime(CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE), '%Y%m%d') AS INTEGER) AS date_key,
       s.store_key,
       COALESCE(c.customer_key, 0) AS customer_key,
       i.product_id, o.payment_method,
       o.order_id, o.status AS order_status,
       i.quantity, i.discount_usd,
       i.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - i.discount_usd AS amount_usd
FROM order_items AS i
JOIN orders AS o ON o.order_id = i.order_id
JOIN products AS p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
JOIN dim_store AS s ON s.store_id = o.store_id
LEFT JOIN dim_customer AS c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id;

SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows, ROUND(SUM(amount_usd), 2) AS sales FROM fact_sale;`} />

**Predict:** the source has 3,287 lines worth 22,754.25 dollars. What does the fact table hold?

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* `3287 | 22754.25`, the same as the source, canceled order and order 2075 included. Before anyone uses a star it passes three checks, and these are the first two: as many rows as the lines, and as much money as the source. The third is that no key is without a row in its dimension. A `LEFT JOIN` from the fact table to a dimension returns an empty key where nothing matched:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE d.date_key IS NULL) AS no_date,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE s.store_key IS NULL) AS no_store,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE c.customer_key IS NULL) AS no_customer
FROM fact_sale AS f
LEFT JOIN dim_date AS d ON d.date_key = f.date_key
LEFT JOIN dim_store AS s ON s.store_key = f.store_key
LEFT JOIN dim_customer AS c ON c.customer_key = f.customer_key;`} />

Expected: 1 row, 3 columns. *Why:* three zeros. The one line of the walk-in order points at customer key 0 instead of at nothing. The three checks passed. The picture of the star so far:

### 4. Ask the questions of the star

**Idea.** A question to a star runs in four moves: start from the fact table, join the dimensions the question names, filter and group by their columns, and add up a measure. At the line grain an order is a group of rows, so count orders with `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)`. Question 1 asks for payment by cafe; the payment is still a plain column of the fact table.

**Try it.** Question 1, for the completed orders:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT s.store_name, f.payment_method, COUNT(DISTINCT f.order_id) AS orders
FROM fact_sale AS f
JOIN dim_store AS s ON s.store_key = f.store_key
WHERE f.order_status = 'completed'
GROUP BY s.store_name, f.payment_method
ORDER BY s.store_name, orders DESC, f.payment_method;`} />

**Predict:** which payment method comes first at each cafe?

Expected: 9 rows, 3 columns.

*Why:* card first at all three, then mobile, then cash: at Campus 400, 209 and 106 orders. The nine numbers add up to 1,799, the completed orders of the source: a question on the star is checked against a number the source already gives. Question 2 is Exercise 1, question 4 is Exercise 2, and question 3 needs the product dimension, which the project step adds.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Start from the questions: the numbers are the facts, the "by" words are the dimensions, the finest thing counted is the grain.
- A dimension has a surrogate key beside the natural key, and a row 0 for what is unknown.
- Build the fact table by joining each dimension on its natural key and keeping the surrogate key.
- Check the star before use: rows, total, and keys without a row in their dimension.
- At the line grain, count orders with `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)`.

**Common trap:** starting from the tables you have instead of the questions you must answer. One row for each order looks tidy, and 862 of the 1,800 orders hold two or more categories.

</Aside>

       ROUND(SUM(f.quantity) * 1.0 / COUNT(DISTINCT f.order_id), 2) AS units_per_order

JOIN dim_store AS s ON s.store_key = f.store_key
