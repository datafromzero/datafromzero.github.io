## The question

A supplier sells an assistant that "answers any question about your data". The owner of the cafes tried it last week on the cafe tables, and it gave the right number of orders for each store in seconds. Now the supplier offers it for a warehouse of hundreds of tables, made up for this lesson.

> It was right last week. Can I trust it with the warehouse?

| | the owner's test | the supplier's offer |
|---|---|---|
| what the assistant sees | a few tables, pasted in whole | hundreds of tables |
| the job | one question | many questions that depend on each other |

By the end you can sort a data task into what an assistant does reliably, unreliably and badly, and say why it can be right on one test and wrong on the next.

## The big idea

A current AI assistant is a strong drafter and a weak authority: it does best on a small job with all the data in view, and gets fewer right as the schema grows, the steps pile up or the answer must come from memory.

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 3 new ideas: the cliff edge, three classes of task, and three questions for any claim. The SQL runs in the page. Exercise 2 uses a free assistant: Claude or ChatGPT, each the backup of the other when one reaches its limit.

### 1. The cliff edge: what the assistant cannot see

**Idea.** An assistant writes a query from what is in front of it: your question and the table and column names you gave it. The less of the database it has to guess, the easier the job. The authors of Spider 2.0 ran one code agent on three sets of test questions; the dated box at the end has the three results.

**Try it.** List the tables of cafe-sales, with their columns:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT table_name,
       COUNT(*) AS columns,
       STRING_AGG(column_name, ', ' ORDER BY ordinal_position) AS column_names
FROM information_schema.columns
WHERE table_schema = 'main'
GROUP BY table_name
ORDER BY table_name;`} />

**Predict:** How many columns do the tables hold in all, and could you paste them whole into a prompt?

Expected: 5 rows, 3 columns.

*Why:* 4 + 5 + 4 + 6 + 5 = 24 columns, a list short enough to paste whole, so a question about this schema is at the easy end. The abstract of the Spider 2.0 paper says its databases often have over 1,000 columns. No prompt holds those: the assistant has to choose which columns matter, and a wrong choice runs without an error.

### 2. Three classes of task

**Idea.** Sort a task by how much of it the assistant can see and how far you can check the result. *Reliably* always means as a draft that you check. *Unreliably* means useful, but every result needs a check. *Badly* means that wrong answers are common, or look right. These classes are this course's reading of published tests, as of 2026-11-22, not a published scale.

**Try it.** Sort these five tasks into the three classes, before you open the answers:

- **a.** Count the completed orders of each cafe in April.
- **b.** Reconcile the till reports, the bank deposits and the supplier invoices of a quarter.
- **c.** Choose the right test for "did Harbor's cash share differ from Campus?".
- **d.** Write two sentences about a small table you pasted into the chat.
- **e.** Give two studies that show loyalty cards raise spending.

**Predict:** Which two are `badly`, which two are `reliably` (as a draft), and which one is left?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the classes</summary>

- **a. Reliably, as a draft.** One question, a schema you pasted, an answer you can recount.
- **b. Badly.** Several steps that depend on each other, over data plus documentation: the kind of job DABstep scores, where even the best agent of 2025 got only a small share of the hardest tasks right.
- **c. Unreliably.** In StatQA the models' most common error was choosing a method that did not apply to the data (the paper calls it an applicability error), so the output looks fine and the choice is wrong.
- **d. Reliably, as a draft.** You hold the table and can check every figure in the two sentences.
- **e. Badly.** A 2023 study of citations in short papers written by an assistant found many fabricated.

</details>

*Why:* the classes follow what the assistant can see (a and d) and what it must recall or choose (c and e), and how many steps lean on each other (b). The cliff edge is wherever one of those three grows.

### 3. Three questions for any claim

**Idea.** A capability number answers three questions: what was measured, on what data, and when. A number that leaves one open is an advertisement.

**Try it.** An advertisement, made up for this lesson: "Our assistant solves 9 of 10 SQL questions."

**Predict:** Which of the three questions does it leave open?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the answer</summary>

All three. It does not say what counts as solved (the query runs, or the result is right), which questions (tidy or enterprise), or when the test was run and with which version.

</details>

*Why:* the three results in the dated box belong to one agent and three sets of test questions. The METR team reports that the length of software task that models complete has grown fast over several years, which is why the numbers carry a date and a review date.

<Aside type="note" title="A map for Part 6: the four Ds">

Anthropic's AI Fluency framework, by Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller, names four habits of working with AI: **Delegation** (deciding whether, when and how to use an assistant), **Description** (telling it clearly what you want), **Discernment** (judging what it returns) and **Diligence** (taking responsibility for AI-assisted work). It is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so we link to it and describe it, and do not copy it. In our mapping, this lesson and C42 are about Delegation, C37 about Description, C38 to C41 about Discernment, and the verification log of C43 about Diligence.

</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- An assistant is a strong drafter and a weak authority.
- It does worse as the schema grows, the steps pile up or the answer must come from memory.
- Sort a task by what the assistant can see and how far you can check the result: reliably (as a draft), unreliably, badly.
- A capability number needs three answers: what, on which data, when.

**Common trap:** "It was right on my test, so it will be right on the warehouse." A tidy test is the easy end.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="core-what-ai-can-and-cannot-do-1">

**Read a claim.** A page says: "Assistant X reaches 85 percent execution accuracy on a SQL benchmark." Answer the three questions, one line each. The first is written for you: *What was measured?* The share of queries whose result matches the right answer. Finish with *On what data?* and *When?*, and say where on the page you would look.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

A benchmark has a name and a version. The data is the databases it uses: how many columns, how many dialects. The date is the one of the test run, not of the page.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

*On what data?* The benchmark's databases, named and versioned: tidy and small, or large and messy. *When?* The date and the model version of the run, not the date of the article.

</details>

<Solution>

*On what data?* The named benchmark and its version, and what its databases look like: a result on tidy small schemas says little about yours. *When?* The date of the test run and the version of the assistant, because both change within months. Where to look: the benchmark's own page or paper and its leaderboard, not the headline. A claim that cannot answer all three stays an advertisement.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## On your own

<Exercise id="core-what-ai-can-and-cannot-do-2">

**A tidy test and a test it cannot pass.** Open Claude or ChatGPT on its free tier and attach [`orders.csv`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/datafromzero/datafromzero.github.io/v0.1.0/datasets/cafe-sales/v1/orders.csv) (open it and save the page as a file). Ask two questions: *How many customers have ordered at more than one cafe?* and *What was the average customer rating at each cafe?* Before you start, write in one line what you expect each answer to look like. The reply differs from run to run, so compare each number with the query, not with a model answer. Expected from the query: 1 row, 1 column.

<RunSQL query={`-- Write your query here, then press Run.`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Count the customers whose orders touch two or more different `store_id` values. For the second question, list the columns of `orders` and look for a rating.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

```sql
SELECT COUNT(*)
FROM (
  SELECT customer_id
  FROM orders
  WHERE customer_id IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY customer_id
  HAVING ...
);
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM (
  SELECT customer_id
  FROM orders
  WHERE customer_id IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY customer_id
  HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT store_id) > 1
);
```

```text
customers
117
```

All 1,800 orders count, and the walk-in order has no customer. A tidy question like this one is class `reliably`, as a draft: if the assistant's number is not 117, find out why. The second question cannot be answered, because no table has a rating column (`orders` has `order_id`, `customer_id`, `store_id`, `ordered_at`, `status`, `payment_method`). A good reply says so; any rating it states was not in the file. Write which of the two things you saw.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
