### 1. Population, sample, parameter and statistic

**Idea.** The population is every case you care about: here the 1,800 orders of the file. A sample is some of them. A number computed from the whole population is a parameter; the same calculation on a sample is a statistic. In a simple random sample every case has the same chance of being picked, as the OpenIntro textbook describes it. `Series.sample(200, random_state=0)` draws one, no order twice; `random_state` is a seed, so the same number gives the same draw on every run.

**Try it.** Compute the parameter and one statistic:

```python
value = orders["value"]                       # one number for each order
mu = value.mean()                             # the parameter: computed from all 1,800 orders
sample = value.sample(200, random_state=0)    # a simple random sample: 200 orders, none twice
xbar = sample.mean()                          # the statistic: computed from the sample
print(len(value), round(float(mu), 2), len(sample), round(float(xbar), 2))
```

**Predict:** the sample is 200 of the 1,800 orders. Will its mean equal the mean of all 1,800 exactly, land close to it, or be far from it?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
1800 12.64 200 12.45
```

</details>

*Why:* the parameter is 12.64 and the statistic, with seed 0, is 12.45: close, but 0.19 low. The 200 orders are only part of the population, so the statistic is an estimate of the parameter.

In symbols, the parameter and the statistic are one calculation on different rows:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i \qquad \text{(parameter)} \\
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \qquad \text{(statistic)}
\end{gathered}
$$

where $$\mu$$ ("mu") is the mean of the whole population of $$N$$ values and $$\bar{x}$$ is the mean of a sample of $$n$$ values (`mu` and `xbar` in the code), and $$x_i$$ is the value of the $$i$$-th order. Here $$N = 1{,}800$$ and $$n = 200$$. The 1,800 order values add up to 22,754.25 and the 200 drawn ones to 2,490.50:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mu = \frac{22{,}754.25}{1{,}800} \approx 12.64 \\
\bar{x} = \frac{2{,}490.50}{200} \approx 12.45
\end{gathered}
$$

**Try it.** Draw the sample again with the seeds 0 to 4:

```python
print([round(float(value.sample(200, random_state=seed).mean()), 2) for seed in range(5)])
```

**Predict:** will the five sample means agree with each other?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[12.45, 11.34, 13.09, 12.34, 13.12]
```

### 2. A random sample and a convenience sample

**Idea.** A convenience sample is made of the cases that are easiest to reach, such as the orders placed before 9 a.m. To judge a way of sampling, repeat it 1,000 times and compare the average of its sample means with the parameter. That gap is the bias of the way of sampling. The OpenIntro textbook adds that it is hard to tell which part of a population a convenience sample stands for.

**Try it.** Draw 1,000 samples of 200 orders in two ways, each with its own seed from 0 to 999:

```python
mornings = orders[orders["hour"] < 9]["value"]    # the orders easiest to reach: placed before 9 a.m.
print(len(mornings), round(float(mornings.mean()), 2))
random_means = [value.sample(200, random_state=seed).mean() for seed in range(1000)]
morning_means = [mornings.sample(200, random_state=seed).mean() for seed in range(1000)]
print(round(float(np.mean(random_means)), 2), round(float(np.mean(morning_means)), 2))
```

**Predict:** what will the average of the 1,000 sample means be for each way, to the nearest 0.1?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
329 11.67
12.62 11.68
```

</details>

*Why:* the morning samples draw only from the 329 orders before 9 a.m., which average 11.67, so their means gather around 11.67. The random samples gather around 12.62.

**Try it.** Subtract the parameter, and look at the smallest and largest mean of each list:

```python
print(round(float(np.mean(random_means) - mu), 2), round(float(np.mean(morning_means) - mu), 2))
print(round(float(np.min(random_means)), 2), round(float(np.max(random_means)), 2))
print(round(float(np.min(morning_means)), 2), round(float(np.max(morning_means)), 2))
```

**Predict:** compare each average with the parameter. Do the two lists of means overlap, and does any morning mean reach 12.64?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
-0.03 -0.97
10.99 14.7
10.64 12.59
```

*Why:* the random samples miss the parameter by 0.03, which is luck left in 1,000 draws. The morning samples miss it by 0.97 on the low side, and none of the 1,000 reaches 12.64: that gap is a bias, and more draws would not shrink it. The two lists overlap, so one morning sample alone could not show its bias. We know the truth here only because we hold the whole population.

In symbols, if $$\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2, \dots, \bar{x}_B$$ are the means of the $$B$$ samples drawn in one way, the bias of that way of sampling is their average minus the parameter:

$$
\text{bias} = \frac{\bar{x}_1 + \bar{x}_2 + \dots + \bar{x}_B}{B} - \mu
$$

For the $$B = 1{,}000$$ morning samples the means average 11.675 and $$\mu$$ is 12.641, to three decimals, so the bias is $$11.675 - 12.641 = -0.966$$, which rounds to the −0.97 printed above. The same calculation on the random samples gives −0.03.

### 3. The people who are missing

**Idea.** Two more biases come from people who are missing. In survivorship bias the data holds only the cases that lasted long enough to be recorded: the statistician Abraham Wald met it when he estimated how vulnerable aircraft were from data on the aircraft that survived (a 1984 journal article by Mangel and Samaniego, listed in the sources, tells the story). In nonresponse bias you ask many people, but those who answer differ from those who do not, and the OpenIntro textbook notes that it can skew the results.

- **A.** A card at the cash register asks, "How was your visit, 1 to 5?" and 60 cards come back, averaging 4.7. Does 4.7 describe all customers?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

**A.** No. Only customers who reached the cash register and chose to fill in a card could be in; those who left unhappy or never came back are missing.

**B.** No. Only people who arrive first could be in it, and they may differ from the people who come at lunch.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A parameter describes the population and is fixed; a statistic describes a sample and changes with it.
- The error of a random sample is luck; the error of a convenience sample leans one way, and more draws do not remove it.
- Data of survivors and surveys that only some answer miss people for a reason.
- Before you use a sample, ask who could be in it, who could not and who did not answer.

**Common trap:** trusting a sample because it is large: more orders from the same easy-to-reach place shrink the luck, not the bias.

</Aside>
