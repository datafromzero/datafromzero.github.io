### 1. Population, sample, parameter and statistic

**Idea.** The population is every case you care about: here the 1,800 orders of the file. A sample is some of them. A number computed from the whole population is a parameter; the same calculation on a sample is a statistic. In a simple random sample every case has the same chance of being picked, as the OpenIntro textbook describes it. `Series.sample(200, random_state=0)` draws one without picking an order twice, and `random_state` is a seed: the same number gives the same draw on every run.

**Try it.** Compute the parameter and one statistic:

```python
value = orders["value"]                       # one number for each order
mu = value.mean()                             # the parameter: computed from all 1,800 orders
sample = value.sample(200, random_state=0)    # a simple random sample: 200 orders, none twice
xbar = sample.mean()                          # the statistic: computed from the sample
print(len(value), round(float(mu), 2), len(sample), round(float(xbar), 2))
```

**Predict:** the sample is 200 of the 1,800 orders. Will its mean equal the mean of all 1,800 exactly, land close to it, or be far from it?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
1800 12.64 200 12.45
```

</details>

*Why:* the parameter is 12.64 and the statistic, with seed 0, is 12.45: close, but 0.19 low. The 200 orders are only part of the population, so the statistic is an estimate of the parameter.

**Try it.** Draw the sample again with the seeds 0 to 4:

```python
print([round(float(value.sample(200, random_state=seed).mean()), 2) for seed in range(5)])
```

**Predict:** will the five sample means agree with each other?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[12.45, 11.34, 13.09, 12.34, 13.12]
```

</details>

*Why:* the parameter is one fixed number, 12.64, and a statistic changes with the orders you happen to draw. The next lesson, *Sampling variability and the Central Limit Theorem* (C6), measures how much.

### 2. A random sample and a convenience sample

**Idea.** A convenience sample is made of the cases that are easiest to reach, such as the orders placed before 9 a.m. To judge a way of sampling, repeat it 1,000 times and compare the average of its sample means with the parameter. That gap is the bias of the way of sampling. The OpenIntro textbook adds that it is hard to tell which part of a population a convenience sample stands for.

**Try it.** Draw 1,000 samples of 200 orders in two ways, each with its own seed from 0 to 999:

```python
mornings = orders[orders["hour"] < 9]["value"]    # the orders easiest to reach: placed before 9 a.m.
print(len(mornings), round(float(mornings.mean()), 2))
random_means = [value.sample(200, random_state=seed).mean() for seed in range(1000)]
morning_means = [mornings.sample(200, random_state=seed).mean() for seed in range(1000)]
print(round(float(np.mean(random_means)), 2), round(float(np.mean(morning_means)), 2))
```

**Predict:** where will the average of the 1,000 sample means land for each way: near the parameter, 12.64, or somewhere else?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
329 11.67
12.62 11.68
```

</details>

*Why:* the morning samples draw only from the 329 orders before 9 a.m., which average 11.67, so their means gather around 11.67. The random samples gather around 12.62.

**Try it.** Subtract the parameter, and look at the smallest and largest mean of each list:

```python
print(round(float(np.mean(random_means) - mu), 2), round(float(np.mean(morning_means) - mu), 2))
print(round(float(np.min(random_means)), 2), round(float(np.max(random_means)), 2))
print(round(float(np.min(morning_means)), 2), round(float(np.max(morning_means)), 2))
```

**Predict:** compare each average with the parameter. Do the two lists of means overlap, and does any morning mean reach 12.64?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
-0.03 -0.97
10.99 14.7
10.64 12.59
```

### 3. The people who are missing

**Idea.** Two more biases come from people who are missing. In survivorship bias the data holds only the cases that lasted long enough to be recorded: Abraham Wald met it when he estimated how vulnerable aircraft were from data on the aircraft that survived (Mangel and Samaniego, 1984). In nonresponse bias you ask many people, but those who answer differ from those who do not, and the OpenIntro textbook notes that it can skew the results.

**Try it.** Count the visits of each customer from the orders table, then from the customer list:

```python
visits = orders.groupby("customer_id").size()                 # only customers who ordered have a row here
print(len(visits), int(visits.sum()), round(float(visits.mean()), 2))
all_visits = customers["customer_id"].map(visits).fillna(0)   # every customer, 0 visits for one who never ordered
print(len(all_visits), round(float(all_visits.mean()), 2))
```

**Predict:** how many customers have a row in `visits`, and will their average number of visits be higher or lower than the average over all 150 customers?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
145 1799 12.41
150 11.99
```

</details>

*Why:* five customers never ordered, so the orders table never shows them. The 145 who appear hold 1,799 of the 1,800 orders, because the walk-in order has no customer id, and their average of 12.41 is about 0.4 too high against 11.99. The five missing customers would have pulled it down.

**Try it.** A made-up scenario, not in the data: the cafe asks all 150 customers how often they visit, and only customers with 18 or more visits answer:

```python
# A made-up scenario, not in the data: the cafe asks all 150 customers "How often do you visit?",
# and suppose that only customers with 18 or more visits answer.
answered = all_visits[all_visits >= 18]
print(len(answered), round(float(answered.mean()), 2), round(float(all_visits.mean()), 2))
```

**Predict:** how many customers answer, and how far is the average they report from the true average of all 150?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
43 22.05 11.99
```

</details>

*Why:* 43 customers answer and report 22.05 visits, against a true 11.99. Nobody lied: the people who answer visit more, and the people who did not answer are exactly the ones the average needs. The cutoff of 18 is invented to show the effect; a real survey cannot see its non-responders.

### 4. Check a sample with three questions

No new idea here: the questions that units 1 to 3 answered, asked before you compute anything.

1. Who could be in the sample, and who could not?
2. Who was never recorded, or dropped out?
3. Who was asked and did not answer?

Then compare the sample with a number you know about the population. Try them on each case before you open the answer.

- **A.** A card at the till asks, "How was your visit, 1 to 5?" and 60 cards come back, averaging 4.7. Does 4.7 describe all customers?
- **B.** A manager asks feedback only from the first ten customers each day. Does it describe all customers?
- **C.** 200 orders are drawn with `Series.sample` from all 1,800. What can their mean value support?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

**A.** No. Only customers who came, reached the till and chose to fill in a card could be in; customers who left unhappy, or never came back, are missing, and the non-responders are most of the visitors.

**B.** No. Only people who arrive first could be in it, a convenience sample of early arrivals, who may differ from the people who come at lunch or in the afternoon.

**C.** A statement about the average order value of these 1,800 orders, with the luck of the draw: every order had the same chance. It says nothing about what customers think, and nothing about orders outside January to June 2026.

</details>

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A parameter describes the population and is fixed; a statistic describes a sample and changes with it.
- The error of a random sample is luck; the error of a convenience sample leans one way, and more draws do not remove it.
- Data of survivors and surveys that only some answer miss people for a reason.
- Before you use a sample, ask who could be in it, who could not and who did not answer.

**Common trap:** reading a bias without its sign, or comparing two biases without looking at their size: a bias is the sample's value minus the truth.

</Aside>
