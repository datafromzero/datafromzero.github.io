## The question

The team of a budgeting app, a fictional app whose log is the dataset `app-events`, gets three alerts this spring:

| what moved | the numbers |
|---|---|
| paywall views, counted as `view_paywall` events | 2,379 in the four weeks before May 18, 1,619 in the four weeks from May 18 |
| return visits from `north_america` on Monday, May 25 | 57, where the four Mondays around it average 109.75 |
| 14-day conversion of new signups | 12.95% for people who signed up from March 9 to April 12, 9.88% from April 13 to June 7 |

Which of the three is a real problem, and what caused it? By the end you can walk from a moving number to its cause in a fixed order and tell a change of mix from a change of rate.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Defining a metric before you compute it*](/core/defining-metrics/) (C12) you fixed what a number means. Here the definition stays and the number moves.

**Diverge.** In *Comparing two groups* (C9) the groups were made by a coin. Here the two groups are two periods, and unit 4 shows what that can hide.

</Aside>

## Warm-up

From memory first, then click to check.

1. In [*Defining a metric before you compute it*](/core/defining-metrics/) (C12), May's conversion came out differently as a cohort reading and as a calendar reading. What separates the two?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   A cohort reading follows each person for the same window from their own signup. A calendar reading counts what happened in the period, so its numerator and its denominator can describe different people.

   </details>

2. In *Correlation is not causation* (C10), a gap shrank inside the groups of a third column. What made the gap?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   The groups: a gap that shrinks when you compare like with like was made by who was in each group.

   </details>

## The big idea

A blended rate can fall while every group holds still, if the mix of the groups moves: so before you ask what got worse, ask who is in the number.

### 1. Is the pipeline telling the truth

**Idea.** Before you explain a movement, check that the number still measures the same thing.

**Try it.** Count the paywall views in the four weeks before May 18 and in the four weeks from May 18, by event name:

```python
paywall = events[events["event_name"].str.contains("paywall")]        # every event whose name contains "paywall"
windows = {"Apr 20 to May 17": ("2026-04-20", "2026-05-18"), "May 18 to Jun 14": ("2026-05-18", "2026-06-15")}
for label, (start, stop) in windows.items():
    rows = paywall[(paywall["event_ts"] >= start) & (paywall["event_ts"] < stop)]
    print(label, rows["event_name"].value_counts().to_dict(), len(rows))
renamed = paywall[paywall["event_name"] != "view_paywall"]            # the names other than view_paywall
print(renamed["platform"].value_counts().to_dict(), renamed["event_ts"].min())
```

**Predict:** by `view_paywall` alone the views fell by about a third between the two windows. The cell counts the events whose name contains "paywall", by name, in the same two windows. Did people look at the paywall about a third less often?

*Why:* by the old name the views fell 32 percent, from 2,379 to 1,619. From May 18 the iOS app sends `paywall_viewed` for the same event: 914 rows in the second window, 1,303 in all, every one on iOS. Over both names there are 2,533, 6.5 percent more than before. Nothing about behavior changed: the metric lost a name, and the definition must list every name of the event. The names are one of three ways a pipeline can fake a movement; the next check shows the other two.

**Try it.** On March 17 the collector was down for part of the afternoon. Count that day's events per hour by when they happened and by when they were received, then measure the lag:

```python
day = events[(events["event_ts"] >= "2026-03-17") & (events["event_ts"] < "2026-03-18")]
arrived = events[(events["received_at"] >= "2026-03-17") & (events["received_at"] < "2026-03-18")]
per_hour = pd.DataFrame({"happened": day.groupby(day["event_ts"].dt.hour).size(), "received": arrived.groupby(arrived["received_at"].dt.hour).size()}).fillna(0).astype(int)
for hour in range(13, 22):
    print(f"{hour:02d}:00  happened {per_hour.loc[hour, 'happened']:>3}  received {per_hour.loc[hour, 'received']:>3}")
lag_hours = (events["received_at"] - events["event_ts"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
outage = (events["event_ts"] >= "2026-03-17 14:00") & (events["event_ts"] < "2026-03-17 20:00")
print(int(outage.sum()), int((lag_hours >= 1).sum()), int(((lag_hours >= 1) & ~outage).sum()), int((lag_hours < 0).sum()))
```

**Predict:** counted by the time of receipt, what will the hourly count do between 14:00 and 19:59, and what will it do at 20:00? Will counting by the time the events happened look the same?

*Why:* by the time of receipt the count is 0, 0, 1, 0, 0 and 0 from 14:00 to 19:59 and 286 at 20:00. By the time the events happened nothing is missing: the 240 events of those six hours arrived after the collector came back. A dashboard by arrival time shows a hole and a spike, and neither is behavior. The last line counts the rest: 1,135 events arrived an hour or more after they happened, 223 of the 240 outage events and 912 others, and 30 carry a time later than their arrival, a phone clock that ran ahead. ### 4. Mix or rate

**Idea.** A blended rate is the sum, over the segments, of each one's share of the people times its own rate. It can change because the shares moved (the *mix*), because the rates moved (the *rate*), or both. To split them, give the later people the earlier rates and see where the blend lands: the distance covered is the mix part, and what remains is the rate part. This is one choice of order; the Why gives the other.

**Try it.** The campaign weeks with the old channel rates, and the standard error of the rate part:

```python
w0, w1 = base["channel"].value_counts(normalize=True), camp["channel"].value_counts(normalize=True)      # shares of the people
r0, r1 = base.groupby("channel")["bought"].mean(), camp.groupby("channel")["bought"].mean()             # conversion by channel
n0, n1 = base["channel"].value_counts(), camp["channel"].value_counts()
before, during = base["bought"].mean(), camp["bought"].mean()
counterfactual = (w1 * r0).sum()                                    # the later shares with the earlier rates
mix_part, rate_part = counterfactual - before, during - counterfactual
print(f"{100 * before:.2f} {100 * during:.2f} {100 * (during - before):.2f}")
print(f"{100 * counterfactual:.2f} {100 * mix_part:.2f} {100 * rate_part:.2f}")
se = np.sqrt((w1 ** 2 * (r1 * (1 - r1) / n1 + r0 * (1 - r0) / n0)).sum())      # the standard error of the rate part
print(f"{100 * se:.2f}", round(float(rate_part / se), 2))
# the other order: the earlier shares for the rate part, so the mix part is what is left, at the later rates
rate_early = (w0 * (r1 - r0)).sum()
mix_late = (during - before) - rate_early
interaction = rate_part - rate_early                                # the part that depends on both changes at once
se_early = np.sqrt((w0 ** 2 * (r1 * (1 - r1) / n1 + r0 * (1 - r0) / n0)).sum())
print(f"{100 * mix_late:.2f} {100 * rate_early:.2f} {100 * interaction:+.2f}")
print(f"{100 * se_early:.2f}", round(float(rate_early / se_early), 2))
```

In symbols, with $$k$$ a segment, $$w_k$$ its share of the people, $$r_k$$ its conversion, $$n_k$$ its number of people, and the subscripts 0 for the earlier period and 1 for the later one, the blend is $$\sum_k w_k r_k$$ and

$$
\text{mix} = \sum_k (w_{k,1} - w_{k,0})\, r_{k,0}, \qquad \text{rate} = \sum_k w_{k,1}\,(r_{k,1} - r_{k,0})
$$

The two add up to the change of the blend. The other order uses $$w_{k,0}$$ in the rate part and $$r_{k,1}$$ in the mix part, and the two orders differ by the interaction $$\sum_k (w_{k,1} - w_{k,0})(r_{k,1} - r_{k,0})$$. The standard error of the rate part, with the later shares fixed, is

$$
\text{SE} = \sqrt{\sum_k w_{k,1}^{2}\left(\frac{r_{k,1}(1 - r_{k,1})}{n_{k,1}} + \frac{r_{k,0}(1 - r_{k,0})}{n_{k,0}}\right)}
$$

It even passes the 12.95 before the campaign, which Exercise 1 splits. The next step is a test, not a chart: report conversion by channel, so that a changed mix cannot look like a failing product.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Check in this order: the pipeline (names, gaps, late and repeated rows), the calendar (the same weekday, a holiday for those who keep it), the segments, then mix against rate.
- Split by a column fixed before the outcome and look at both the shares and the rates. Two splits can tell two stories.
- The mix part has an order: say it, and read the rate part against its standard error.
- State a prediction that can fail, and test it on the next data.

**Common trap:** "it fell in every region, so it is a global fault". Every region fell by 2.0 to 3.8 points while the channel mix alone explains 2.86 of the 3.07.

</Aside>
