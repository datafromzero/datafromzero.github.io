## The question

The product lead of a budgeting app says:

> People who are active in their first week buy Plus more often. Let's get everyone active in week one.

Can you trust that? Take the 20,177 people who signed up by June 16, so that each of them has had 14 days to buy. A person's *first-week events* are the visits (`session_start`) and the expenses (`add_expense`) in the 7 days after signup. Call a person *engaged* with 5 or more.

| in the first week | people | bought within 14 days |
|---|---:|---:|
| engaged | 3,697 | 16.5% |
| not engaged | 16,480 | 11.0% |

By the end you can say how strong that link is, which three explanations compete with "engagement makes people buy", and what it would take to use those words. Rows that a client retry wrote twice (the dataset README lists them) are counted; dropping them moves this table by under 0.2 points.

**Diverge.** A gap you can see is not yet a cause you can name. Unit 3 shows what happens to the gap above once a third column is held fixed.

### 1. One number for a scatter

**Idea.** The *correlation coefficient*, written $$r$$, is one number between −1 and 1 for how closely two columns follow a straight line. Its sign is the direction, its size is how tightly the points hug the line, and 0 means no straight-line link. It has no units, and swapping the two columns does not change it. pandas computes it with `Series.corr` (the Pearson method by default); SciPy's page for it says it "measures the linear relationship between two datasets".

**Try it.** The correlation of first-week events with buying, and the pieces it is made of:

```python
x, y = people["events7"], people["bought"]
print(round(x.corr(y), 3))
dx, dy = x - x.mean(), y - y.mean()
print(round((dx * dy).sum(), 2), round((dx ** 2).sum(), 2), round((dy ** 2).sum(), 2))
print(round(x.mean(), 2), round(y.mean(), 2))
```

**Predict:** r runs from −1 to 1. Will the correlation between a person's events in the first week and buying be close to 0, around 0.5 or close to 1?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.078
1646.64 210510.46 2135.07
2.58 0.12
```

</details>

*Why:* r is 0.078, close to 0: a faint straight-line link, although engaged people buy 5.5 points more often. The gap compares two groups; r asks how closely every person's count and buying follow one line, and only 0.12 of the people bought. A weak r is not proof of no effect, and a strong one is not proof of one.

In symbols, with $$n$$ people, $$x_i$$ the first-week events of person $$i$$, $$y_i$$ equal to 1 if the person bought and 0 if not, and $$\bar{x}$$ and $$\bar{y}$$ the two means (2.58 events and 0.12, the share who bought):

$$
r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
$$

The top adds each person's distance from the x mean times their distance from the y mean: a plus on the same side of both means, a minus on opposite sides (the corners of the picture above). The bottom scales the sum to fit between −1 and 1. For the 20,177 people the three sums are 1,646.64, 210,510.46 and 2,135.07:

$$
r = \frac{1{,}646.64}{\sqrt{210{,}510.46 \times 2{,}135.07}} \approx 0.078
$$

### 2. Three rival explanations

**Idea.** When two columns go together, three rivals compete with "the first causes the second". *Reverse causation*: the second causes the first. A *confounder*: a third thing drives both. *Coincidence*: chance. Each has a check.

| Rival | In the app | A check |
|---|---|---|
| Reverse causation | The second column drives the first | Which came first? |
| A confounder | Something about the person drives both | Compare inside the groups of that thing (unit 3) |
| Coincidence | The pattern is luck | Does it show again in other people? |

**Try it.** The clock. People who bought within 14 days: how much of their first-week activity came after the purchase?

```python
first_buy = ev[(ev["event_name"] == "purchase") & (ev["days"] <= 14)].groupby("user_id", as_index=False)["event_ts"].min().rename(columns={"event_ts": "buy_ts"})
buyers_week1 = week1.merge(first_buy, on="user_id")  # the first-week events of the people who bought
print(len(buyers_week1), round((buyers_week1["event_ts"] > buyers_week1["buy_ts"]).mean(), 3))
```

**Predict:** People who bought within 14 days made 7,898 visits and expenses in their first week. What share of them happened after the purchase: a few, about half or most?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
7898 0.555
```

</details>

*Why:* 55.5% of the 7,898 first-week events of the buyers came after the purchase, so the first-week count mixes activity before and after buying. It cannot say which came first; count only events before the purchase to apply the clock.

### 5. What earns causal language

**Idea.** Words such as "causes", "raises" and "because of" are earned by a design, not by a number. The strongest is a coin toss that assigns the groups. Without it, say "goes with" and name the rivals that are still open. An A/B test is that design; *Designing an A/B test* (C14) builds one.

**Predict:** In the paywall_v2 test, a coin put each new person in the control or the variant group. Will the channel mix of the two groups differ by more than a point or two?

*Why:* the coin made the groups alike: the largest difference in channel share is 1.8 points (app_store: 25.5 and 23.7), and a coin makes the groups alike in expectation, including in things nobody recorded; here the recorded channel mix agrees. So the 2.6-point gap between 12.7% and 15.3% is unlikely to come from the channel or any other trait of the people: it is the new paywall or chance, which the A/B lessons tell apart. Engaged against not engaged is different: nobody assigned it, the people chose.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A correlation $$r$$ measures the straight-line part only, and one point can decide it: plot first, then compute.
- Three rivals to "A causes B": reverse causation, a confounder, coincidence. Check each; a timing check counts only what came before the outcome.
- Compare inside the groups of the third thing: a gap that vanishes there was made by the groups.
- A comparison can flip inside the subgroups (Simpson): compare like with like, and ask how big each gap is next to chance.
- Causal words need a design, ideally a coin. Otherwise write "goes with".

**Common trap:** reading "engaged people buy more" as "engagement makes people buy".

</Aside>
