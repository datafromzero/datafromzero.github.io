## The question

The owner asks:

> For each cafe, which months carried the biggest share of its sales, and how did each month compare with the one before?

The first months of the two older cafes, one row for each store and month:

| store_id | month | amount |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 1,591.00 |
| 1 | 2 | 1,162.00 |
| 2 | 1 | 1,518.75 |
| 2 | 2 | 1,508.25 |

Every row has to stay, and each needs a number that comes from its own cafe. By the end of this page you will add such columns, in the way of the window functions of the SQL stage.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In *Window functions: keep the rows, add the context* (S14), `SUM(orders) OVER (PARTITION BY day)` wrote the day's total beside every row and kept all rows. `groupby("store_id")["amount"].transform("sum")` writes the store's total beside every row of the store. `LAG(x) OVER (PARTITION BY ...)` of *Comparing to the previous row* (S16) is `groupby(...).shift(1)`, and `RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY ... ORDER BY ... DESC)` of *Ranking and top-N per group* (S15) is `groupby(...).rank(method="min", ascending=False)`.

**Diverge.** In S16 the first rows of a moving average used only the rows that existed. Unit 4 shows what `rolling(3)` does on its first rows.

</Aside>

## The big idea

A group calculation can shrink the table to one row for each group, or keep every row and write the group's result beside it; `transform`, `rank`, `shift` and `rolling` are the second kind.

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 4 new ideas: `transform`, `rank`, `shift` within groups, and `rolling`. The last unit only checks them. The setup cell made `store_month`, the sales of each store in each month, and `per_customer`, the orders of each customer in each store.

### 1. transform: the group's answer on every row

**Idea.** `groupby("key")["column"].transform("sum")` runs the same calculation for each group as `sum()` does, but hands the answers back to the rows of the table instead of making a new table.

**Try it.** The total of each store, two ways:

```python
totals = store_month.groupby("store_id")["amount"].sum()
broadcast = store_month.groupby("store_id")["amount"].transform("sum")
print(len(totals), len(broadcast))
print(broadcast.head(3).tolist())
```

**Predict:** how many values will each of the two have, and what will the first three values of `broadcast` be?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
3 15
[9144.25, 9144.25, 9144.25]
```

</details>

*Why:* 3 values against 15, one for each store against one for each row. The first three rows belong to store 1, so each holds store 1's total, 9,144.25. Now the share of each month in its own store, and the biggest shares:

```python
store_month["store_total"] = broadcast
store_month["share"] = store_month["amount"] / store_month["store_total"]
print(store_month.sort_values("share", ascending=False).head(4).round(3))
```

**Predict:** which store and month will have the biggest share of its own store's sales, and how many of the first four rows will belong to that store?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
    store_id  month   amount  store_total  share
12         3      4  1596.50      4606.25  0.347
13         3      5  1546.50      4606.25  0.336
14         3      6  1463.25      4606.25  0.318
4          1      5  1868.50      9144.25  0.204
```

</details>

*Why:* the first three rows are Harbor's, with 0.347, 0.336 and 0.318. Harbor has only three months, so each month is about a third of its sales, and every month of the other cafes is smaller. A share is a share of the group, so it depends on how many rows the group has. Shares of a group add up to 1, which the check unit shows.

### 2. rank: places, with ties

**Idea.** `column.rank()` gives each value its place, counting from the smallest. `ascending=False` counts from the largest. `method` says what rows with the same value get: `"min"` gives all of them the lowest place of the tie, as `RANK()` does, and `"dense"` gives the next place number with no gap, as `DENSE_RANK()` does. The places come back as decimals.

**Try it.** Harbor's eight most active customers, ranked both ways. `per_customer` counts every order that has a customer:

```python
harbor = per_customer[per_customer["store_id"] == 3]
ranked = harbor.assign(
    rank_min=harbor["orders"].rank(method="min", ascending=False),
    rank_dense=harbor["orders"].rank(method="dense", ascending=False),
)
print(ranked.sort_values(["rank_min", "customer_id"]).head(8))
```

**Predict:** four customers have 10 orders and five have 9. What will `rank_min` and `rank_dense` show for the customers with 9?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
     store_id  customer_id  orders  rank_min  rank_dense
285         3          104      11       1.0         1.0
220         3            7      10       2.0         2.0
224         3           13      10       2.0         2.0
264         3           77      10       2.0         2.0
284         3          103      10       2.0         2.0
223         3           12       9       6.0         3.0
236         3           33       9       6.0         3.0
246         3           47       9       6.0         3.0
```

</details>

*Why:* `rank_min` shows 6.0 and `rank_dense` shows 3.0. The four customers with 10 orders hold places 2 to 5, so `"min"` skips to 6 for the next value, while `"dense"` goes on from 2 to 3. Now inside each store at once. A `groupby` before `rank` restarts the places for every store:

```python
per_customer["place"] = per_customer.groupby("store_id")["orders"].rank(method="min", ascending=False)
top = per_customer[per_customer["place"] <= 3]
print(top.groupby("store_id").size())
```

**Predict:** for each store, how many customers will have place 3 or better?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
store_id
1    3
2    5
3    5
dtype: int64
```

</details>

*Why:* 3, 5 and 5. In store 1 the places are 1, 2 and 2: two customers tie at place 2, and nobody holds place 3, so three pass. In store 2 three customers tie at place 3, and in store 3 four tie at place 2, so five pass in each. Asking for "the top 3" with `"min"` can return more than three rows, as in S15.

### 3. shift inside each group

**Idea.** `groupby("key")["column"].shift(1)` puts the value of the previous row of the same group beside each row.

**Try it.** The month before, within each store:

```python
store_month["prev"] = store_month.groupby("store_id")["amount"].shift(1)
print(store_month["prev"].isna().sum())
print(store_month[store_month["store_id"] == 3][["month", "amount", "prev"]])
```

**Predict:** how many rows of `prev` will be empty, and which rows are they?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
3
    month   amount    prev
12      4  1596.50     NaN
13      5  1546.50  1596.5
14      6  1463.25  1546.5
```

</details>

*Why:* 3: the first row of each store. Harbor's April has no month before it, because Harbor has no March, as in S16. Now the same without the `groupby`:

```python
plain = store_month["amount"].shift(1)
first_harbor = store_month.index[store_month["store_id"] == 3][0]
print(plain.isna().sum())
print(plain[first_harbor])
```

**Predict:** how many rows will be empty now, and what will sit beside Harbor's April?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
1
1590.75
```

</details>

*Why:* 1 empty row, and 1,590.75 beside Harbor's April: that is the June of Campus, the row above it in the table. Without the `groupby`, pandas has no idea where one store ends, so a store's first row borrows from the last row of the one before.

### 4. rolling: a window that slides

**Idea.** `series.rolling(3).mean()` takes the mean of each row and the two rows before it, the window of three rows, and moves one row at a time. A window counts rows, not days. The second line below adds a setting, `min_periods=1`.

**Try it.** The three-month average of Old Town, store 1:

```python
old_town = store_month[store_month["store_id"] == 1]["amount"]
print(old_town.rolling(3).mean().round(2).tolist())
print(old_town.rolling(3, min_periods=1).mean().round(2).tolist())
```

**Predict:** what will the first two values of each list be?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[nan, nan, 1435.83, 1419.58, 1655.08, 1612.25]
[1591.0, 1376.5, 1435.83, 1419.58, 1655.08, 1612.25]
```

</details>

*Why:* `nan, nan` for the first and 1,591.0, 1,376.5 for the second. With `rolling(3)` the first two rows have fewer than three values in their window, so they stay empty. The pandas page for `rolling` names `min_periods` as the smallest number of values a window must hold, and with `min_periods=1` the first rows average the rows that exist, as S16's moving average did: 1,591.00 alone, then 1,591.00 and 1,162.00 together. Now one average for each store, as in unit 3:

```python
by_store = store_month.groupby("store_id")["amount"].rolling(3).mean()
plain_roll = store_month["amount"].rolling(3).mean()
print(by_store.isna().sum(), plain_roll.isna().sum())
```

**Predict:** how many empty values will each version have, with and without the `groupby`?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
6 2
```

</details>

*Why:* 6 against 2. The grouped version starts again in each store, so each store has two empty values at its start. The other one slides across the borders, so Campus's January average would include Old Town's last months. The grouped result has the store id and the old row label together as its row label; `by_store.reset_index(level=0, drop=True)` puts the row labels back, and the column can be assigned.

### 5. Check: two ways to the same numbers

Write down what you expect, then run the cell. The shares are those of unit 1, and `old_town` is the series of unit 4.

```python
print(store_month.groupby("store_id")["share"].sum().round(6).tolist())
by_hand = old_town.iloc[1:4].mean()
print(round(old_town.rolling(3).mean().iloc[3], 6) == round(by_hand, 6))
```

**Predict:** what will the shares of each store add up to, and does the average that `rolling` gives for Old Town's April equal the mean of February, March and April worked out by hand?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[1.0, 1.0, 1.0]
True
```

</details>

*Why:* each store's shares add up to 1.0, and the answer to the second is `True`: a rolling average is the mean of the rows in its window and nothing else.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- `transform("sum")` repeats each group's result on every row of the group; `sum()` gives one row for each group.
- `rank(method="min", ascending=False)` is `RANK`, `method="dense"` is `DENSE_RANK`; a `groupby` before `rank` restarts the places for each group.
- `groupby(...).shift(1)` is `LAG` inside each group; the first row of a group holds `nan`.
- `rolling(3).mean()` waits for three rows; `min_periods=1` averages the rows that exist.
- Reconcile: shares add up to 1, and a window result can be checked by hand.

**Common trap:** `shift` and `rolling` without a `groupby` slide across the borders between groups, so a group's first rows borrow from the group above.

</Aside>
