## The question

Dana sends the Monday dashboard of [*Build and publish your first dashboard*](/data-analyst/first-bi-dashboard/) (DA10), the one you build yourself two lessons from now, to the three store managers with one question: "Can everyone read this?" Its numbers are right. The question is about the screen: its colors, its numbers, and what a screen reader says about it.

The colors of that screen:

| used for | color | sits on |
|---|---|---|
| Campus bars | `#0072B2` | white chart panel |
| Harbor bars | `#AD9100` | white chart panel |

## The big idea

A readable dashboard passes four tests: every color has a measured contrast, no meaning rests on color alone, every number carries only the digits a reader needs for one stated window, and every chart's takeaway is also written as text.

### 1. Contrast is a number

**Idea.** The *contrast ratio* of two colors is (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), where L1 and L2 are the *relative luminance* of the lighter and the darker color: its brightness from 0 (black) to 1 (white). Ratios run from 1:1 to 21:1. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.1 (WCAG) ask for at least 4.5:1 for text (criterion 1.4.3), or 3:1 for large text, 18 point or 14 point bold. They ask for at least 3:1 for the parts of a graphic you need in order to understand it, such as a chart's bars (1.4.11), unless text labels carry the same information; that label text then falls under 1.4.3. Each is measured against the color next to it. The ratio is not rounded: 4.499:1 does not meet 4.5:1.

Luminance comes from the three channels. Each channel c (0 to 255) becomes c / 255, then c / 12.92 if that is at most 0.04045, else ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) to the power 2.4. Then L = 0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B.

**Try it:** the block builds helpers (`lin` is the channel rule, `part` one channel's share of L, `ratio` the ratio) and a view of the colors in The question, two light grays for small labels and the white card, then prints each against the white card and the page.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE MACRO lin(c) AS CASE WHEN c <= 0.04045 THEN c / 12.92 ELSE POWER((c + 0.055) / 1.055, 2.4) END;
CREATE OR REPLACE MACRO ratio(a, b) AS (GREATEST(a, b) + 0.05) / (LEAST(a, b) + 0.05);
CREATE OR REPLACE MACRO part(hex, start, weight) AS weight * lin(CAST('0x' || SUBSTR(hex, start, 2) AS INTEGER) / 255.0);
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW palette AS
SELECT name, hex, part(hex, 2, 0.2126) + part(hex, 4, 0.7152) + part(hex, 6, 0.0722) AS lum
FROM (VALUES ('Campus bar', '#0072B2'), ('Harbor bar', '#AD9100'), ('Old Town bar', '#00A07A'),
             ('title text', '#333333'), ('gray text A', '#767676'), ('gray text B', '#999999'),
             ('white card', '#FFFFFF'), ('page background', '#F4F4F2')) AS c(name, hex);

SELECT f.name, f.hex, ROUND(f.lum, 4) AS luminance,
       ROUND(ratio(f.lum, w.lum), 2) AS on_white, ROUND(ratio(f.lum, p.lum), 2) AS on_page
FROM palette f, palette w, palette p
WHERE w.name = 'white card' AND p.name = 'page background'
  AND f.name NOT IN ('white card', 'page background')
ORDER BY f.name;`} />

**Predict:** which bar color has the least contrast with the white card, and does it still reach 3:1? Does the gray `#767676` pass 4.5:1 on both backgrounds?

Expected: 6 rows, 5 columns.

*Why:* on the white card the bars read 5.19 (Campus), 3.08 (Harbor) and 3.33 (Old Town): all three pass, Harbor by 0.08. On the page color they read 4.71, 2.79 and 3.02: Harbor fails and Old Town passes by 0.02. The bars sit on the white panel, so this is the case to avoid, not the dashboard's. The title color `#333333` gives 12.63 and 11.47. The gray `#767676` gives 4.54 on white and 4.12 on the page: `#999999` fails on both.

### 2. Color never works alone, and means one thing

**Idea.** WCAG criterion 1.4.1: color is not the only visual means of conveying information. Microsoft's accessibility page says to supplement color with text or icons, and lists pairs that are hard to tell apart, among them green and red, green and brown, and light green and yellow. Two colors with a lightness ratio near 1:1 differ only in hue. Microsoft's design page also asks for the same color for the same dimension value in every chart, with the same scales and ordering.

**Try it:** the three store colors in pairs: their lightness ratio, and the gray each would be with the same luminance (the channel rule run backwards, `encode`, then 0 to 255). Run unit 1 first, because it builds `palette` and `ratio`.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE MACRO encode(l) AS 1.055 * POWER(l, 1 / 2.4) - 0.055;

CREATE OR REPLACE MACRO gray_level(l) AS CAST(ROUND(255 * encode(l)) AS INTEGER);

SELECT a.name AS one, b.name AS other, ROUND(ratio(a.lum, b.lum), 2) AS lightness_ratio,
       gray_level(a.lum) AS gray_one, gray_level(b.lum) AS gray_other
FROM palette a, palette b
WHERE a.name < b.name AND a.name LIKE '%bar' AND b.name LIKE '%bar'
ORDER BY lightness_ratio;`} />

**Predict:** which two of the three store colors are closest in lightness?

Expected: 3 rows, 5 columns.

*Why:* Harbor and Old Town, 1.08 apart (grays 147 and 141); Campus and Old Town are 1.56 apart (109 and 141) and Campus and Harbor 1.69 (109 and 147). Take the hue away and the gold and the green are almost one gray. Microsoft's list also includes green and blue, which is Campus blue beside Old Town green, and blue and gray and green and gray; it names light green and yellow, not this gold and green. Before you decide, run the screen through a color-blindness simulator, a tool that shows how a picture looks to a color-blind person, such as Coblis or Vischeck, which that page names.

In the DA10 dashboard each store is also named in text (on the store chart's axis, beside the hour chart's rows), so color is not the only cue there. One thing is off: the blue `#0072B2` is Campus in the store and hour charts and also the color of all four bars of the category chart. ### 3. Numbers a reader can compare

**Idea.** Microsoft's design pages say: do not exceed three or four numerals; show a measure with one or two numerals left of the decimal point and scale it to thousands or millions ("3.4 million", not "3,400,000"); drop data labels nobody needs; and keep time frames distinct and consistent, never last month beside a chart filtered to one month. Base the decimals on the smallest gap the reader needs to see; the DA10 dashboard's axis ticks (\$0.00 to \$10,000.00) carry zeros that say nothing.

**Try it:** the block rebuilds the table of DA7 (`sales_clean`, order 2075 as 2 units), then prints revenue by store and for all stores in two label forms, with the dates each row covers.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW sales_clean AS
SELECT oi.order_item_id AS line_id, o.order_id, o.ordered_at, CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE) AS order_date,
       s.store_name AS store, p.product_name AS product, p.category,
       CASE WHEN oi.order_item_id = 6952 THEN 2 ELSE oi.quantity END AS quantity,
       p.unit_price_usd AS unit_price, oi.discount_usd AS discount,
       (CASE WHEN oi.order_item_id = 6952 THEN 2 ELSE oi.quantity END) * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd AS amount,
       o.payment_method, COALESCE(c.loyalty_tier, 'walk-in') AS loyalty_tier
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = o.store_id
LEFT JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed';

SELECT COALESCE(store, 'all stores') AS store, ROUND(SUM(amount), 2) AS revenue,
       printf('$%.1fK', SUM(amount) / 1000) AS one_decimal,
       printf('$%.2fK', SUM(amount) / 1000) AS two_decimals,
       STRFTIME(MIN(order_date), '%B %-d') || ' to ' || STRFTIME(MAX(order_date), '%B %-d') AS window
FROM sales_clean
GROUP BY ROLLUP (store)
ORDER BY store NULLS LAST;`} />

**Predict:** with one decimal, do any two stores print the same label?

Expected: 4 rows, 5 columns.

*Why:* Campus and Old Town both print \$9.0K, though they differ by \$21.00 (\$8,998.75 and \$8,977.75); with two decimals they read \$9.00K and \$8.98K. One decimal is enough for a card: all stores read \$22.6K, 3 numerals instead of the 7 of \$22,582.75. The value underneath does not change, only its label; display units apply on top of the value's format string (Microsoft's display-units page). The window column shows that Harbor starts on April 1, the day it opened, so a title that compares stores must say so, as the headline of the DA10 dashboard does ("in half the time").

### 4. Say it in words

**Idea.** *Alt text* is a description that screen readers read aloud and the screen does not show. Microsoft's accessibility page says it should give the insight you want the reader to take away; a screen reader already reads a visual's title and type, so skip "bar chart". Power BI's alt text box holds 250 characters. Two more rules from that page: keep important information out of tooltips, because people with motor problems and people who do not use a mouse have difficulties accessing them, and screen readers cannot read a report tooltip; and when key information is reachable only through a click, pre-filter the visuals so the conclusion is already on screen.

**Try it:** the alt text of the store chart, built from the data. Run unit 3 first.

<RunSQL query={`WITH r AS (SELECT store, SUM(amount) AS revenue FROM sales_clean GROUP BY store)
SELECT alt, LENGTH(alt) AS characters
FROM (SELECT 'Revenue by store, January 1 to June 30, 2026: '
             || STRING_AGG(store || ' ' || printf('$%.1fK', revenue / 1000), ', ' ORDER BY store)
             || '. Harbor opened on April 1 and sold '
             || CAST(ROUND(100 * MAX(revenue) FILTER (WHERE store = 'Harbor') / AVG(revenue) FILTER (WHERE store <> 'Harbor')) AS INTEGER)
             || ' percent of the average of the two older cafes.' AS alt
      FROM r);`} />

**Predict:** is the sentence under 250 characters?

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* 173 characters. It names the measure, the window, the three values and the takeaway. Fixed text goes stale when the data changes; Microsoft's page describes alt text built from a DAX measure (a formula in Power BI's formula language) with conditional formatting, which is why a query builds this one.

<Aside title="Tableau and Power BI: where these settings are">

These menus and right-clicks are in the programs on a computer; you need a computer to follow them.

- **Contrast.** Tableau: default text colors follow 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text, as in unit 1). Power BI: 4.5:1 for title, axis and data label text is on Microsoft's checklist, which names three checkers; its built-in themes help, and you pick one from the Themes gallery on the Design ribbon in Power BI Desktop or with View > Theme in the Power BI service.
- **Second cue.** Tableau: labels, size, shape and position as well as color; labels are off by default. Power BI: Format, Shapes, Show Markers, a different marker shape for each line.
- **One color per value.** Tableau: upper-right corner of the color legend, Edit Colors, pick the item, pick a color. Power BI: a series takes theme colors in the order its members appear in each visual, so a member can change color between visuals; assign it a theme color in the formatting pane.
- **Number format.** Tableau: right-click a number in the view, Format, Numbers; the Custom formats take decimal places and units. Power BI: search "Display units" in the Format pane and choose Thousands, Millions or None (Auto picks K or M by size).
- **Alt text.** Tableau: Worksheet, Accessibility, or Data Guide, Edit alt text, in authoring mode. Power BI: Format, expand General, the Alt Text box at the bottom.
- **Tooltips.** Tableau: enable tooltips and check "Use mark tooltip for alt text on marks" so assistive technology announces them. Power BI: extra information only.

</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Test a color against the color it sits on: 4.5:1 for text (3:1 if large), 3:1 for graphics a label does not explain.
- Give every meaning a second cue: a name, a label or a shape.
- One store, one color, in every chart.
- Fewest digits, one stated window.
- Alt text states the takeaway.

**Common trap:** testing a color on white when it sits on the page color.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="data-analyst-dashboards-everyone-can-read-1">

**A shorter axis.** The DA10 dashboard's store chart draws its axis from \$0.00 to \$10,000.00 in steps of \$2,000.00. The editor lists the six ticks as they read now. Before you run it, write the six labels you would show. Then add a column `short_label` with them (`$0`, then `$2K` to `$10K`) and a column `numerals_saved`: how many numerals each label loses. Expected: 6 rows, 2 columns for the starter; 6 rows, 4 columns when finished.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT tick, '$' || format('{:,.2f}', CAST(tick AS DOUBLE)) AS now_label
FROM generate_series(0, 10000, 2000) AS t(tick);`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

A `CASE` for the zero tick, then `'$' || CAST(tick / 1000 AS INTEGER) || 'K'`. To count numerals, delete every other character: `LENGTH(REGEXP_REPLACE(text, '[^0-9]', '', 'g'))`. `REGEXP_REPLACE(text, pattern, replacement, 'g')` replaces every match of the pattern (`'g'` means all of them, not only the first); `[^0-9]` matches one character that is not a digit, so replacing it with an empty string `''` leaves only the digits.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

```sql
CASE WHEN tick = 0 THEN '$0' ELSE ... END AS short_label
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
WITH ticks AS (
  SELECT tick, '$' || format('{:,.2f}', CAST(tick AS DOUBLE)) AS now_label,
         CASE WHEN tick = 0 THEN '$0' ELSE '$' || CAST(tick / 1000 AS INTEGER) || 'K' END AS short_label
  FROM generate_series(0, 10000, 2000) AS t(tick)
)
SELECT tick, now_label, short_label,
       LENGTH(REGEXP_REPLACE(now_label, '[^0-9]', '', 'g')) - LENGTH(REGEXP_REPLACE(short_label, '[^0-9]', '', 'g')) AS numerals_saved
FROM ticks
ORDER BY tick;
```

The labels are \$0, \$2K, \$4K, \$6K, \$8K and \$10K. The first tick loses 2 numerals and each of the other five loses 5 (\$10,000.00 has 7, \$10K has 2). Every tick is a multiple of 1,000, so a digit after the K would say nothing.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

One `WITH` block, a CTE as in [*Readable queries with CTEs*](/sql/ctes/) (S13), for the orders of each store and hour (`COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)`), then `arg_min(hour, orders)` for Harbor's quietest hour: it returns the `hour` of the row where `orders` is smallest. Join the pieces with `||`.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"One sentence that gives the window, Harbor's quietest hour with its count and the other two stores' counts at that hour, and its length, under 250."*

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
WITH h AS (
  SELECT store, EXTRACT(hour FROM ordered_at) AS hour, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
  FROM sales_clean
  GROUP BY store, EXTRACT(hour FROM ordered_at)
),
harbor AS (SELECT arg_min(hour, orders) AS quiet_hour, MIN(orders) AS quiet_orders FROM h WHERE store = 'Harbor')
SELECT alt, LENGTH(alt) AS characters
FROM (SELECT 'Orders by hour of day for each store, January 1 to June 30, 2026 (Harbor from April 1). Harbor is quietest at '
             || harbor.quiet_hour || ' a.m. with ' || harbor.quiet_orders || ' orders, against '
             || (SELECT orders FROM h WHERE store = 'Campus' AND hour = harbor.quiet_hour) || ' at Campus and '
             || (SELECT orders FROM h WHERE store = 'Old Town' AND hour = harbor.quiet_hour) || ' at Old Town in that hour.' AS alt
      FROM harbor);
```

Harbor's fewest orders are at 7 a.m. (20), against 79 at Campus and 73 at Old Town in that hour. The sentence is 186 characters, under 250, and it states the takeaway and not the chart type.
