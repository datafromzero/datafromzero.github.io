## The question

This is chapter 3 of the dashboard project. Dana opens the first draft and frowns:

> The Orders card says 3,286. We have never sold that many. And Orders looks wrong when I click Bakery.

Two cards, two different mistakes. The data is the table of chapter 1, built from `orders` (one row per visit), `order_items` (one row per product line), `products` and `stores`.

## Warm-up

From memory first, then click to check.

1. In [*Grain, facts and dimensions*](/core/grain-facts-and-dimensions/) (C17), what does the grain of a table say?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   What one row stands for: one line of an order, for example, or one order.

   </details>

2. In [*Designing a star schema*](/core/designing-a-star-schema/) (C18), what do the rows of a fact table and a dimension table hold?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   A fact row holds measurements and the keys of its dimensions; a dimension row describes one thing (a product, a store).

   </details>

3. In [*Using an AI assistant for Python, with checks*](/python/ai-assisted-code/) (P27), Exercise 3, why did the average of six monthly averages not give the average of all the tea lines?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   Each month's average counted once, however many lines the month had, so every month got the same weight.

   </details>

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 4 ideas: the grain of the fact, totals that do not add, measures against columns, and where a filter stops. Run unit 1 first: the later units use its view.

### 1. Declare the grain, then count for it

**Idea.** The grain of a fact table "establishes exactly what a single fact table row represents" (Kimball). Dana's dashboard sells products, so the grain is one order line, and the line carries its order's keys. A card that counts rows then counts lines. The count of orders is a *distinct* count of the order key.

**Try it:** the table at line grain, and the two ways to count.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW flat AS
SELECT o.order_id, o.store_id, o.customer_id, o.status, oi.order_item_id AS line_id, p.category,
       (CASE WHEN oi.order_item_id = 6952 THEN 2 ELSE oi.quantity END) * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd AS amount
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id;

SELECT COUNT(*) AS card_counting_rows,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS card_counting_orders,
       SUM(amount) AS revenue
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed';`} />

**Predict:** a distinct count of the order key gives the real number of orders. Is it closer to 1,000, 1,800 or 2,500?

Expected: 1 row, 3 columns.

*Why:* 1,799 orders (one of the 1,800 is canceled); the card that counts rows shows 3,286, 83 percent more. The revenue is right at this grain, \$22,582.75, because an amount belongs to a line. Order 2075 touches the revenue (as 2 units) and not the order count. Dana's first draft counted rows, so it sums the right thing under the wrong name.

### 2. A total is a measure too

**Idea.** A *measure* is calculated for each cell of a chart, from the rows that cell's filters leave. The total is one more cell, worked out from all the rows.

**Try it:** distinct orders by category, with the total.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT category, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY category
UNION ALL
SELECT 'all categories', COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed'
ORDER BY orders DESC;`} />

**Predict:** do the four categories add up to the total, to less, or to more? By how many orders?

Expected: 5 rows, 2 columns.

*Why:* coffee 1,258, bakery 648, tea 470 and food 406 add up to 2,782, which is 983 more than the 1,799 of the total row. Nothing is wrong: an order with a coffee and a bagel is in the coffee cell, in the bakery cell and once in the total cell, so 983 of the 2,782 counts repeat an order already counted in another category. A distinct count does not add up across a dimension that splits an order. A dashboard that adds the bars, or hides the total row, shows a number that is not the number of orders.

### 3. Measure, calculated column and the total cell

**Idea.** A *calculated column* is a formula that gives a result for every row of a table: the amount of a line, which needs the price from `products`. Microsoft's page says it "calculates a result for every row in the table", and lists import-mode columns as materialized, derived when the data refreshes. A measure (unit 2) is not stored per row: it is worked out when the report asks, for each cell, and Microsoft's page says its calculated results change as you interact with the report. Revenue is a measure (a sum of the column).

**Try it:** run unit 1 first. Revenue per order by store, a total cell, and the average of the three store cells.

<RunSQL query={`WITH r AS (
  SELECT s.store_name AS store, SUM(f.amount) AS revenue, COUNT(DISTINCT f.order_id) AS orders
  FROM flat f
  JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = f.store_id
  WHERE f.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY s.store_name
)
SELECT cell, revenue_per_order
FROM (
  SELECT 1 AS k, store AS cell, ROUND(revenue / orders, 2) AS revenue_per_order FROM r
  UNION ALL SELECT 2, 'total cell', ROUND(SUM(revenue) / SUM(orders), 2) FROM r
  UNION ALL SELECT 3, 'average of the three', ROUND(AVG(revenue / orders), 2) FROM r
) AS t
ORDER BY k, cell;`} />

**Predict:** the total cell of a ratio measure shows the average of the cells above it, or revenue over orders for all the rows?

Expected: 5 rows, 2 columns.

*Why:* the total cell reads 12.55, the ratio of all the rows; the average of the three cells is 12.63. Harbor's 355 orders weigh less than Old Town's 729, and an average of cells gives each store the same weight. A ratio measure is recomputed from the rows of every cell, total included, and is never an average of the cells. Define it once, and the tool computes it in every cell.

<details>
<summary>Check: column or measure?</summary>

In `flat`, `amount` is a calculated column (a value stored for every line); revenue per order is a measure (a ratio recomputed for each cell).

</details>

### 4. Where a filter stops

**Idea.** A relationship joins a table with unique keys (the *one* side, a dimension) to a table that repeats them (the *many* side, the fact). With a single direction, a filter on the one side reaches the many side. A card counts the rows of the table its measure comes from.

**Try it:** run unit 1 first. Dana clicks Bakery. A card that counts the rows of `orders` and a card that counts distinct orders of the fact:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders WHERE status = 'completed') AS orders_table_rows,
       (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) FROM flat WHERE status = 'completed' AND category = 'bakery') AS orders_from_fact;`} />

**Predict:** after the click, how many orders does the second card show: about 648, about 703 or 1,799?

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* the distinct count from the fact reads 648, the orders with a bakery line (703 is the number of bakery lines). The `orders` table is not filtered: its card still reads 1,799, because a filter does not climb from the many side to the one. Dana's second symptom is the wiring, not the data. The fix is to count from the fact, which the filter reaches, or to give the fact table the order's attributes (a star).

<Aside title="In Tableau Public: relationships">

On the Data Source page, relationships connect logical tables: drag a second table next to the first until the line appears and match the fields. The Help page says Tableau builds the joins from the fields each sheet uses, keeps each table's level of detail, and that you do not choose a join type. Check each card against the numbers on this page.

</Aside>

<Aside title="Power BI: relationships and measures">

On the Modeling tab, Manage relationships, then New: pick the two tables and their columns, and a cardinality of Many to one, with the many side on the fact. Cross filter direction Single means filtering choices in connected tables work on the table where values are aggregated; Both treats two tables as one, and Microsoft advises against it when two fact tables share dimensions. Make a measure with Modeling, then New measure.

</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Declare the grain first; a card that counts rows counts the grain.
- Count a coarser thing (orders) with a distinct count of its key.
- A total is a measure: distinct counts and ratios do not add up from their parts.
- Filters run from the one side to the many side; count from where the filter lands.

**Common trap:** summing the bars of a distinct count and calling the total a bug.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="data-analyst-bi-modeling-for-dashboards-1">

**A column worked out once, then two measures.** Dana wants morning against the rest of the day. The editor adds a calculated column, `part_of_day`: *morning* before 11 a.m. by the order's time, else *later*. It then gives the revenue of each part. Add the distinct orders of each part and a `total` row with its own distinct count, then say whether the two parts add up to the total. Run unit 1 first. Expected: 3 rows, 3 columns.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW flat_day AS
SELECT f.*, CASE WHEN EXTRACT(hour FROM o.ordered_at) < 11 THEN 'morning' ELSE 'later' END AS part_of_day
FROM flat f
JOIN orders o ON o.order_id = f.order_id;

SELECT part_of_day, SUM(amount) AS revenue
FROM flat_day
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY part_of_day
ORDER BY part_of_day;`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The `total` row is a second `SELECT` without `GROUP BY`, stacked with `UNION ALL`. Put the `ORDER BY` after the last `SELECT`.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

```sql
SELECT part_of_day, SUM(amount) AS revenue, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
FROM flat_day
...
UNION ALL
SELECT 'total', ...
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT part_of_day, SUM(amount) AS revenue, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
FROM flat_day
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY part_of_day
UNION ALL
SELECT 'total', SUM(amount), COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)
FROM flat_day
WHERE status = 'completed'
ORDER BY revenue;
```

Morning \$8,387.00 from 678 orders, later \$14,195.75 from 1,121, total \$22,582.75 from 1,799. This time the orders add up: 678 + 1,121 = 1,799. The part of the day is worked out from the order's time, so every order has one value and sits in one part. In unit 2 the category belonged to the line, so an order could sit in two. A distinct count adds up across a dimension only when each order has a single value of it.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT category, SUM(amount) AS revenue,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(amount) / SUM(SUM(amount)) OVER (), 1) AS share
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed' AND store_id = 3
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY revenue DESC;
```

Coffee 47.0, food 22.6, bakery 15.8 and tea 14.6 percent of Harbor's \$4,606.25, adding to 100. The window sums the grouped rows, so the filter shrinks the denominator with the numerator. A denominator taken from the whole table would give coffee 9.6 percent and four shares that add to 20.4.

## Check yourself

Without looking back: what does the grain declare, and why does a filter on products not change a count of the `orders` table?

<details>
<summary>answer</summary>

The grain says what one row of the fact table stands for. A filter runs from the one side of a relationship to the many side: products filter the order lines, and the order lines do not filter the orders table.

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague defines the card: "Revenue = the sum of quantity times unit price over completed lines, with order 2075 as 2 units." She says it ties out because it is "within 0.4 percent of the cafe's figure of \$22,582.75".

The query gives \$22,666.75, \$84.00 above the cafe's figure. The difference is the column she left out:

SELECT SUM(oi.discount_usd) AS discounts

JOIN orders o ON o.order_id = oi.order_id

Discounts total \$84.00. The dataset README defines the amount of a line as quantity times price minus discount, so her measure is a different metric with a close value.
