## The question

> The Orders card says 3,286. We have never sold that many. And when I click Bakery, Orders does not move.

Two cards, two different mistakes. The data is the table of chapter 1, built from `orders` (1,800 rows, one per visit), `order_items` (3,287 rows, one per product line), `products` and `stores`.

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   What one row stands for: one line of an order, for example, or one order.

   </details>

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   A fact row holds measurements and the keys of its dimensions; a dimension row describes one thing (a product, a store).

   </details>

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 4 ideas: the grain of the fact, totals that do not add, measures against columns, and where a filter stops. Run unit 1 first: the later units use its view.

### 1. Declare the grain, then count for it

**Idea.** The grain of a fact table "establishes exactly what a single fact table row represents" (Kimball). Dana's dashboard sells products, so the grain is one order line, and the line carries its order's keys. A card that counts rows then counts lines. The count of orders is a *distinct* count of the order key.

**Try it:** the table at line grain, and the two ways to count.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW flat AS
SELECT o.order_id, o.store_id, o.customer_id, o.status, oi.order_item_id AS line_id, p.category,
       (CASE WHEN oi.order_item_id = 6952 THEN 2 ELSE oi.quantity END) * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd AS amount
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id;

SELECT COUNT(*) AS card_counting_rows,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS card_counting_orders,
       SUM(amount) AS revenue
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed';`} />

**Predict:** the card that counts rows shows how many more than the card that counts distinct orders: about 10, 80 or 200 percent more?

Expected: 1 row, 3 columns.

*Why:* 3,286 against 1,799, 83 percent more. The revenue is right at this grain, \$22,582.75, because an amount belongs to a line. Order 2075 touches the revenue (as 2 units) and not the order count. Dana's first draft counted rows, so it sums the right thing under the wrong name.

### 2. A total is a measure too

**Idea.** A *measure* is calculated for each cell of a chart, from the rows that cell's filters leave. An order with a coffee and a bagel is in the coffee cell, in the bakery or food cell, and once in the total cell. So distinct counts do not add up across a dimension that splits an order.

**Try it:** distinct orders by category, with the total.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT category, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY category
UNION ALL
SELECT 'all categories', COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed'
ORDER BY orders DESC;`} />

**Predict:** do the four categories add up to the total, to less, or to more? By how many orders?

Expected: 5 rows, 2 columns.

*Why:* coffee 1,258, bakery 648, tea 470 and food 406 add up to 2,782, which is 983 more than the 1,799 of the total row. Nothing is wrong: 983 times an order sits in a second or third category. A dashboard that adds the bars, or hides the total row, shows a number that is not the number of orders.

### 3. Measure or calculated column

**Idea.** A *calculated column* is worked out once for every row when the model loads and stored: the amount of a line, which needs the price from `products`. A *measure* is worked out when the report asks, in the filter context of each cell; Microsoft's page says the results of measures change as you interact with the report. Revenue is a measure (a sum of the column); revenue per order is a ratio of two measures, recomputed in every cell, total included, and never an average of the cells.

**Try it:** run unit 1 first. Revenue per order by store, a total cell, and the average of the three store cells.

<RunSQL query={`WITH r AS (
  SELECT s.store_name AS store, SUM(f.amount) AS revenue, COUNT(DISTINCT f.order_id) AS orders
  FROM flat f
  JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = f.store_id
  WHERE f.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY s.store_name
)
SELECT store AS cell, ROUND(revenue / orders, 2) AS revenue_per_order FROM r
UNION ALL SELECT 'total cell', ROUND(SUM(revenue) / SUM(orders), 2) FROM r
UNION ALL SELECT 'average of the three', ROUND(AVG(revenue / orders), 2) FROM r;`} />

**Predict:** the total cell of a ratio measure shows the average of the cells above it, or revenue over orders for all the rows?

Expected: 5 rows, 2 columns.

*Why:* the total cell reads 12.55, the ratio of all the rows; the average of the three cells is 12.63. Harbor's 355 orders weigh less than Old Town's 729, and an average of cells gives each store the same weight. Define the measure once, and the tool computes it in every cell.

### 4. Where a filter stops

**Idea.** A relationship joins a table with unique keys (the *one* side, a dimension) to a table that repeats them (the *many* side, the fact). In the default single direction a filter on the one side reaches the many side; it does not climb from the many side to the one. Where the chart has a measure from a table the filter cannot reach, the card ignores the filter. The fix is to count from the fact, which the filter reaches, or to give the fact table the order's attributes (a star).

**Try it:** run unit 1 first. The orders table has 1,799 completed orders. Dana clicks Bakery. A card that counts the rows of `orders` and a card that counts distinct orders of the fact:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders WHERE status = 'completed') AS orders_table_rows,
       (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) FROM flat WHERE status = 'completed' AND category = 'bakery') AS orders_from_fact;`} />

**Predict:** after the click, the first card shows 1,799, about 648 or about 703?

Expected: 1 row, 2 columns.

*Why:* the orders table is not filtered: its card still reads 1,799, while the distinct count from the fact reads 648, the orders with a bakery line (703 is the number of bakery lines). Dana's second symptom is the wiring, not the data.

<Aside title="In Tableau Public: relationships">

On the Data Source page, relationships connect logical tables: drag a second table next to the first until the line appears and match the fields. The Help page says Tableau builds the joins from the fields each sheet uses, keeps each table's level of detail, and that you do not choose a join type. Check each card against the numbers on this page.

</Aside>

<Aside title="Power BI: relationships and measures">

On the Modeling tab, Manage relationships, then New: pick the two tables and their columns, and a cardinality of Many to one, with the many side on the fact. Cross filter direction Single means filtering choices in connected tables work on the table where values are aggregated; Both treats two tables as one, and Microsoft advises against it when two fact tables share dimensions. Make a measure with Modeling, then New measure.

</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Declare the grain first; a card that counts rows counts the grain.
- Count a coarser thing (orders) with a distinct count of its key.
- A total is a measure: distinct counts and ratios do not add up from their parts.
- Filters run from the one side to the many side; count from where the filter lands.

**Common trap:** summing the bars of a distinct count and calling the total a bug.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="data-analyst-bi-modeling-for-dashboards-1">

**Revenue per order, for each category.** Dana asks what an order that includes each category is worth. The editor gives the revenue of each category. Add the number of orders of each category and the ratio, each from its own rows. Run unit 1 first. Expected: 4 rows, 4 columns.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT category, SUM(amount) AS revenue
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY category;`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Orders are a distinct count of `order_id` in the same `GROUP BY`, and the ratio is the sum over that count.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

```sql
SELECT category, SUM(amount) AS revenue,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders,
       ...
FROM flat
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT category, SUM(amount) AS revenue,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(amount) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id), 2) AS revenue_per_order
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY category;
```

Bakery \$5.90 (648 orders), coffee \$8.59 (1,258), food \$11.64 (406) and tea \$6.88 (470). Each cell divides its own revenue by its own orders. An order with a coffee and a bagel is in two rows, so these four values are not parts of the card's \$12.55 and do not average to it.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<summary>Hint</summary>

Harbor is `store_id = 3`. The denominator is the revenue of all Harbor rows: `SUM(SUM(f.amount)) OVER ()` after the `GROUP BY`.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"Four categories, coffee a little under half, and shares that add to 100."*

```sql
SELECT category, SUM(amount) AS revenue,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(amount) / SUM(SUM(amount)) OVER (), 1) AS share

```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT category, SUM(amount) AS revenue,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(amount) / SUM(SUM(amount)) OVER (), 1) AS share
FROM flat
WHERE status = 'completed' AND store_id = 3
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY revenue DESC;
```

Coffee 47.0, food 22.6, bakery 15.8 and tea 14.6 percent of Harbor's \$4,606.25, adding to 100. The window sums the grouped rows, so the filter shrinks the denominator with the numerator. A denominator taken from the whole table would give coffee 9.6 percent and four shares that add to 20.4.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Check yourself

Without looking back: what does the grain declare, and why does a filter on products not change a count of the `orders` table?

<details>
<summary>answer</summary>

The grain says what one row of the fact table stands for. A filter runs from the one side of a relationship to the many side: products filter the order lines, and the order lines do not filter the orders table.

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague defines the card: "Revenue = the sum of quantity times unit price over completed lines, with order 2075 as 2 units." She says it ties out because it is "within 0.4 percent of the cafe's figure of \$22,582.75".

The query gives \$22,666.75, \$84.00 above the cafe's figure. The difference is the column she left out:

```sql
SELECT SUM(oi.discount_usd) AS discounts
FROM order_items oi
JOIN orders o ON o.order_id = oi.order_id

```

Discounts total \$84.00. The dataset README defines the amount of a line as quantity times price minus discount, so her measure is a different metric with a close value. "Within 0.4 percent" is not tying out: a card ties out when the difference is zero or fully explained. Subtract the discount in the column that holds the line amount.
