## The big idea

A change is only as good as its two periods: they must be complete, and they must be like each other.

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 5 ideas: the percent change, the incomplete period, like with like, the rolling average, the headline and memo. The editors run DuckDB; BigQuery differences are noted.

### 1. A percent change needs a base

**Idea.** A *period-over-period change* is the new value minus the previous one, divided by the previous one. In SQL, `LAG` brings the previous row next to the current one, so order the rows by the period.

**Try it:** revenue of each month with the month before and the change.

<RunSQL query={`WITH monthly AS (
  SELECT CAST(DATE_TRUNC('month', o.ordered_at) AS DATE) AS month,
         ROUND(SUM(oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd), 2) AS revenue
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
  JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY month
)
SELECT month, revenue,
       LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS previous,
       ROUND(100.0 * (revenue - LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month)) / LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month), 1) AS change_pct
FROM monthly
ORDER BY month;`} />

**Predict:** which month has the biggest change, up or down?

Expected: 6 rows, 4 columns.

*Why:* April, +46.6 percent: Harbor opened on April 1, so April has a store that March did not. The others are February −14.1 (28 days against 31), March +12.2, May +16.2 and June −12.2. Order 2075 is in May: corrected, May is +12.4 and June −9.3. A year-over-year change would divide by the same month a year earlier, but the data starts on January 1, 2026, so every month would be `NULL`. Report that, and do not invent a base.

> **Cost line.** Reads about 154,039 bytes of 3 tables (estimate from types), billed at least 30 MB, about 0.00017 to 0.00018 dollars at \$6.25 per TiB (BigQuery pricing page, read 2026-11-23). In BigQuery write `DATE_TRUNC(DATE(o.ordered_at), MONTH)`. **Cheaper:** keep the six rows and compute the changes from them.

### 2. Flag a period that is not complete

**Idea.** The last row of a report is often a period still in progress. Count the days each period holds, and flag any that holds fewer than a full one. A week has 7 days.

**Try it:** the last five weeks, with the days they hold and the change on the week before.

<RunSQL query={`WITH weekly AS (
  SELECT CAST(DATE_TRUNC('week', o.ordered_at) AS DATE) AS week_start,
         COUNT(DISTINCT CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE)) AS days,
         ROUND(SUM(oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd), 2) AS revenue
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
  JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY week_start
)
SELECT week_start, days, revenue,
       ROUND(100.0 * (revenue - LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY week_start)) / LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY week_start), 1) AS change_pct,
       CASE WHEN days = 7 THEN 'complete' ELSE 'partial' END AS period
FROM weekly
ORDER BY week_start DESC
LIMIT 5;`} />

**Predict:** what does the change of the newest week say, and should you report it?

Expected: 5 rows, 5 columns.

*Why:* the week of June 29 holds 2 days, Monday and Tuesday, because the data ends on June 30, and it shows −79.9 percent. The first week of the year is also partial: it starts on Monday, December 29, and holds 4 days. Report a partial week as partial, or compare it with the same days of the week before. Counting the days that have orders assumes every day has one; where a day can be empty, join a calendar of days instead. DuckDB's `date_trunc('week', …)` starts the week on Monday, the first column shows it. BigQuery's `WEEK` begins on Sunday; use `ISOWEEK` for Monday.

### 3. Compare like with like

**Idea.** A total hides what changed under it. Divide by the *store-days*, one store open for one day, so a new store does not look like growth. Then compare with what the weekday mix predicts: a Saturday earns more than a Thursday, and months hold different mixes. Here order 2075 is corrected to a quantity of 2.

**Try it:** for each month, the store-days, the revenue per store-day, its change, and an index where 100 means the month earned what its weekdays predict.

<RunSQL query={`WITH store_days AS (
  SELECT CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE) AS day, o.store_id,
         SUM((CASE WHEN o.order_id = 2075 AND oi.product_id = 15 THEN 2 ELSE oi.quantity END)
             * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd) AS revenue
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
  JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY day, o.store_id
),
typical AS (
  SELECT DAYOFWEEK(day) AS dow, AVG(revenue) AS typical_revenue
  FROM store_days
  GROUP BY dow
),
monthly AS (
  SELECT CAST(DATE_TRUNC('month', s.day) AS DATE) AS month,
         COUNT(*) AS store_days,
         ROUND(SUM(s.revenue) / COUNT(*), 2) AS per_store_day,
         ROUND(100.0 * SUM(s.revenue) / SUM(t.typical_revenue), 1) AS vs_weekday_mix
  FROM store_days s
  JOIN typical t ON t.dow = DAYOFWEEK(s.day)
  GROUP BY month
)
SELECT month, store_days, per_store_day,
       ROUND(100.0 * (per_store_day / LAG(per_store_day) OVER (ORDER BY month) - 1), 1) AS change_pct,
       vs_weekday_mix
FROM monthly
ORDER BY month;`} />

**Predict:** which month earned the most above what its weekdays predict?

Expected: 6 rows, 5 columns.

*Why:* May, at 104.8, with 93 store-days and 10 weekend days (June has 8). Per store-day, April is 48.79 against March's 48.31: the 46.6 percent of unit 1 was a third store. June's change is −6.2 percent and its index is 100.5, so June looks like a normal month and May like a good one. The index runs from 95.7 to 104.8 over the six months.

### 4. Smooth the noise, but not a mistake

**Idea.** A *moving average* replaces each day with the average of the last seven, so the weekly rhythm and single odd days fade. Name the window once and reuse it.

**Try it:** the 7-day average as recorded and with order 2075 corrected; count the days on which the two differ.

<RunSQL query={`WITH daily AS (
  SELECT CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE) AS day,
         SUM(oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd) AS recorded,
         SUM((CASE WHEN o.order_id = 2075 AND oi.product_id = 15 THEN 2 ELSE oi.quantity END)
             * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd) AS corrected
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
  JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY day
),
smooth AS (
  SELECT day,
         AVG(recorded) OVER w AS avg_recorded,
         AVG(corrected) OVER w AS avg_corrected
  FROM daily
  WINDOW w AS (ORDER BY day ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)
)
SELECT COUNT(CASE WHEN ABS(avg_recorded - avg_corrected) > 0.001 THEN 1 END) AS days_that_differ,
       MIN(CASE WHEN ABS(avg_recorded - avg_corrected) > 0.001 THEN day END) AS first_day,
       MAX(CASE WHEN ABS(avg_recorded - avg_corrected) > 0.001 THEN day END) AS last_day
FROM smooth;`} />

**Predict:** on how many days does the average differ?

Expected: 1 row, 3 columns.

*Why:* 7 days, May 1 to May 7: the day of order 2075 is in the window of that day and the six after it. On May 1 the day's revenue is \$305.50 recorded and \$139.00 corrected, and the 7-day average is \$162.39 against \$138.61. A moving average hides noise, not a typing error: fix the data first.

### 5. One sentence, then a memo

**Idea.** The headline is one sentence with the change, its comparison and its caveat. For Dana: *Revenue per store-day fell 6.2 percent in June, from \$53.08 to \$49.78 (order 2075 corrected), but June matched what its weekdays predict, and May was the high month.* The memo follows the format of C15.

> **Decision:** do not decide the July promotion on June's dip; decide it after the first four complete weeks of July.
> **Context:** Dana asked whether sales are down before choosing a promotion; the data covers January 1 to June 30, three cafes since April.
> **Tension:** June is the largest fall of the five month-to-month changes (−6.2 percent against −4.9 in February), but May was the largest rise (+8.8), and the months differ in length, weekdays and number of stores.
> **Evidence:** revenue per store-day was \$49.78 in June and \$53.08 in May, order 2075 set aside. Against the weekday mix, June is 100.5 and May 104.8, and the six months range from 95.7 to 104.8. The total fell 12.2 percent as recorded, 9.3 corrected.
> **Recommendation:** wait for July's first four complete weeks, compare them with the same weekdays, and promote if a complete month falls below February's \$47.68, the lowest so far.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A change divides by the previous period; `LAG` brings it next to the current row, ordered by the period.
- Count the days of each period and flag the partial ones: the first and last often are.
- Divide by store-days so a new store is not growth, and compare with the weekday mix.
- A moving average hides noise and spreads a mistake; fix the mistake first.
- Say when a comparison cannot be made (no year-over-year with six months).

**Common trap:** reading the newest row of a report as a trend.

</Aside>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

<Solution>

```sql
WITH store_days AS (
  SELECT CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE) AS day, o.store_id,
         SUM(oi.quantity * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd) AS revenue
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
  JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY day, o.store_id
)
SELECT DAYNAME(day) AS weekday, COUNT(*) AS store_days, ROUND(AVG(revenue), 2) AS per_store_day
FROM store_days
GROUP BY weekday, DAYOFWEEK(day)
ORDER BY per_store_day DESC;
```

<RunSQL query={`WITH daily AS (
