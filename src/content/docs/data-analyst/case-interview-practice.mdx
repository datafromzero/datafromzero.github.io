## The question

An interviewer, or a manager, hands you a one-line case and waits. You have five minutes and no laptop. These four lines are one case of each family:

| family | the case |
|---|---|
| define | Dana: "Tell me whether Harbor is doing well." |
| diagnose | "Old Town's revenue fell from January to February. Why?" |
| design | "Would a free pastry with every coffee raise orders?" |
| judge | "Take 20 percent off coffee at 7 a.m. at Harbor: worth it?" |

Employers publish how they screen. Meta describes its first data scientist interview as a 45-minute conversation with analytical and technical parts, and GitLab lists interviews with a data team member, a business team member and an executive. The four families are this lesson's grouping. Answer aloud, then score yourself, 0 to 2 on each line:

| criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| frames | jumps in | one question, or no decision named | restates, asks two or more, names the decision |
| structures | no order | an order, not announced | announces the steps first |
| defines | undefined words | some parts | numerator, denominator, unit, window, and a guardrail where success is asked |
| checks | opinions | a check named | checks in a stated order, with numbers and units |
| concludes | no recommendation | a recommendation | recommendation, assumptions aloud, what would change it |

Ten points; 8 is a pass here, and the bar is ours.

## The big idea

Answer a case in four moves: frame it, announce your steps, check with data in a fixed order, and finish with a recommendation and what would change it.

## Learn by doing

About 30 minutes, 4 cases, one for each family. Say each answer aloud before you read ours. The SQL runs in the page. Run each block after the ones above it.

### 1. Define: "Is Harbor doing well?"

**Idea.** A define case is vague on purpose. Do not answer it: frame it. Ask what "well" is compared with, for which decision and by when. Then state the metric with its four parts (C12) and a *guardrail*, a second number that must not get worse.

**Try it.** Completed orders for each cafe since April 1, the 91 days all three were open, with order 2075 counted as 2 units:

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW sales AS
SELECT o.order_id, s.store_name AS store, o.ordered_at, CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE) AS order_date,
       p.category,
       (CASE WHEN oi.order_item_id = 6952 THEN 2 ELSE oi.quantity END) * p.unit_price_usd - oi.discount_usd AS amount
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = o.store_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed';

SELECT store,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_date) AS days,
       ROUND(COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) * 1.0 / COUNT(DISTINCT order_date), 2) AS orders_per_day,
       ROUND(SUM(amount) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id), 2) AS revenue_per_order
FROM sales
WHERE ordered_at >= DATE '2026-04-01'
GROUP BY store
ORDER BY store;`} />

**Predict:** on orders per day, is Harbor ahead of the older cafes, level or behind?

Expected: 3 rows, 4 columns.

*Why:* Harbor takes 3.90 orders a day, Campus 3.98 and Old Town 4.01: level, 2 to 3 percent fewer. Its orders are slightly larger: \$12.98 against \$12.52 and \$12.80. Out loud: *"What is well compared with, for which decision, by when? Assuming Dana decides whether to keep going: my metric is completed orders per open day since April 1, against the older cafes over the same days; my guardrail is revenue per order, so that a discount cannot buy orders. Harbor is level, 3.90 against 3.98 and 4.01. I would keep going and check again at six months."*

### 2. Diagnose: "Old Town's February"

**Idea.** Walk C13's order aloud and stop when something explains the move: the pipeline (are the rows all there), the calendar (the length of the months), the segments (where it moved), then orders against order size. When nothing explains it, say so.

**Try it.** Old Town for January and February:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT strftime(order_date, '%Y-%m') AS month,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_date) AS days,
       ROUND(SUM(amount), 2) AS revenue,
       ROUND(SUM(amount) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_date), 2) AS revenue_per_day,
       ROUND(COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) * 1.0 / COUNT(DISTINCT order_date), 2) AS orders_per_day,
       ROUND(SUM(amount) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id), 2) AS revenue_per_order
FROM sales
WHERE store = 'Old Town' AND order_date < DATE '2026-03-01'
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month;`} />

**Predict:** once the length of the months is allowed for, how big is the fall in revenue per day?

Expected: 2 rows, 6 columns.

*Why:* every day has orders (31 and 28 days). Revenue falls 27.0 percent, from \$1,591.00 to \$1,162.00, and 19.1 percent per day, so the calendar explains 8 of the 27 points. Orders per day fall 10.9 percent and revenue per order 9.2: both moved. Now the segments:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT category,
       ROUND(SUM(amount) FILTER (WHERE order_date < DATE '2026-02-01'), 2) AS january,
       ROUND(SUM(amount) FILTER (WHERE order_date >= DATE '2026-02-01'), 2) AS february,
       ROUND(SUM(amount) FILTER (WHERE order_date >= DATE '2026-02-01') - SUM(amount) FILTER (WHERE order_date < DATE '2026-02-01'), 2) AS change
FROM sales
WHERE store = 'Old Town' AND order_date < DATE '2026-03-01'
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY change;`} />

**Predict:** which category fell most, and did any rise?

Expected: 4 rows, 4 columns.

*Why:* coffee fell \$283.25, tea \$117.00 and food \$78.75, while bakery rose \$50.00: coffee is 66 percent of the \$429.00 fall, and no single category explains it.

Out loud: *"Nothing is missing. February is shorter, which explains 8 points of 27. Fewer orders a day and smaller orders both fell, mostly in coffee. It is the largest of the 12 monthly changes, but not by much. I found no cause in the data; I would ask Old Town what changed, and watch March."*

### 3. Design: "Would a free pastry raise orders?"

**Idea.** A design case needs five things: the unit you randomize, the arms, the primary metric with a guardrail, the duration, and the decision rule written first. A cafe cannot randomize people at the till, so randomize *cafe-days*. Duration follows C14's sizing: for a mean, days per arm are about 15.68 times the variance of the daily count, divided by the square of the rise worth acting on; 15.68 is 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)², the bar and power of C14.

**Try it.** The spread of orders per cafe-day, and the days per arm for two rises:

<RunSQL query={`WITH day_counts AS (
  SELECT store, order_date, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
  FROM sales
  GROUP BY store, order_date
),
s AS (SELECT AVG(orders) AS mean, VAR_SAMP(orders) AS variance FROM day_counts)
SELECT ROUND(mean, 2) AS orders_per_day,
       ROUND(SQRT(variance), 2) AS spread,
       CEIL(15.68 * variance / POWER(0.10 * mean, 2)) AS days_for_10pct,
       CEIL(15.68 * variance / POWER(0.25 * mean, 2)) AS days_for_25pct
FROM s;`} />

**Predict:** how many cafe-days per arm to catch a 10 percent rise in orders per day, and a 25 percent rise?

Expected: 1 row, 4 columns.

*Why:* orders per day average 3.97 with a spread of 1.40, so 196 cafe-days per arm catch a 10 percent rise (0.40 orders a day) and 32 catch 25 percent (0.99). With three cafes each day gives three cafe-days: 392 cafe-days is 131 days, 19 whole weeks, and 64 is 22 days, 4 whole weeks.

Out loud: *"I would randomize cafe-days, balanced by weekday. Primary metric: orders per day; guardrail: revenue per order, since the pastry costs money. If a 25 percent rise is the least that pays, four weeks. Rule, written first: keep the pastry if orders per day rise by at least 0.99 and the guardrail does not fall. Risk: loyalty customers who see both arms."*

### 4. Judge: "Is 20 percent off coffee at 7 a.m. worth it?"

**Idea.** A judge case has no right answer, only a defended one. Size what is at stake, say how each side could be wrong, decide, and name the check that would change your decision. A discount of 20 percent keeps revenue only if orders rise by a quarter, since 1 ÷ 0.8 is 1.25.

**Try it.** Harbor's orders that include a coffee at 7 a.m., from April 1 to June 30:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS coffee_orders_at_7,
       ROUND(SUM(amount), 2) AS coffee_revenue,
       ROUND(0.2 * SUM(amount), 2) AS discount_cost,
       ROUND(0.2 / 0.8 * COUNT(DISTINCT order_id), 1) AS extra_orders_to_break_even
FROM sales
WHERE store = 'Harbor' AND hour(ordered_at) = 7 AND category = 'coffee';`} />

**Predict:** how many extra coffee orders at 7 a.m. in 91 days pay for the discount, on revenue alone?

Expected: 1 row, 4 columns.

*Why:* 15 orders and \$121.50 of coffee; the discount gives away \$24.30 if nobody changes, and it breaks even at 3.8 more orders in 91 days. The bet is small both ways. Out loud: *"At most \$24.30 a quarter is at stake, and four extra orders would pay for it on revenue alone. A test could not see four orders against a daily spread of 1.4, so I would run it for a month as a pilot. The check that changes my answer is the margin on a coffee: at a margin of 60 cents on the dollar the break-even rise is 50 percent, not 25."*

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Frame first, announce your steps, check in a fixed order, end with what would change it.
- Define: a metric with four parts, plus a guardrail.
- Diagnose: pipeline, calendar, segments, mix or rate; say when nothing explains it.
- Design: unit, arms, metric, duration from the rise worth finding, a rule written first.
- Judge: size the stake, decide, name the check that would change it.

**Common trap:** starting to compute before you have said what the number is for.

</Aside>

<Exercise id="data-analyst-case-interview-practice-3">

**Spot the flaw.** For a design case, a candidate sizes a test of a new price on daily revenue. She takes each cafe's revenue for each month, 15 numbers, and runs the sizing for a 10 percent rise: "15 days per arm, so a test of about two weeks."

<RunSQL query={`WITH month_revenue AS (
  SELECT store, strftime(order_date, '%Y-%m') AS month, SUM(amount) AS revenue
  FROM sales
  GROUP BY store, month
),
s AS (SELECT AVG(revenue) AS mean, VAR_SAMP(revenue) AS variance FROM month_revenue)
SELECT CEIL(15.68 * variance / POWER(0.10 * mean, 2)) AS per_arm
FROM s;`} />

Run unit 1 first. Expected: 1 row, 1 column. Run it, then size the test properly and say what is wrong.

<Solution>

The query gives 15, but its spread is the spread of cafe-months, so 15 counts cafe-months: more than a year of three cafes. The test randomizes cafe-days, so the spread must be that of daily revenue:

```sql
WITH day_revenue AS (
  SELECT store, order_date, SUM(amount) AS revenue
  FROM sales
  GROUP BY store, order_date
),
s AS (SELECT AVG(revenue) AS mean, VAR_SAMP(revenue) AS variance FROM day_revenue)
SELECT CEIL(15.68 * variance / POWER(0.10 * mean, 2)) AS per_arm
FROM s;
```

It gives 360 cafe-days per arm (daily revenue averages \$49.85 with a spread of \$23.87), 24 times more. A months-sized spread is small because a month averages 30 noisy days; the days of the test inherit the noise of days.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
