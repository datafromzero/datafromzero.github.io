## The question

This is chapter 7 of the dashboard project. On Monday you have five minutes with Dana, who owns the cafes, and Sam, who runs Harbor and would like a morning barista. On your dashboard's hour-by-hour chart, Harbor's 7 a.m. bar looks low. Sam will ask: are you sure? That is *pushback*: a listener doubting or arguing with what you present, which you answer with evidence.

## Learn by doing

About 50 minutes, 3 ideas: the five-minute shape, three questions to expect, an answer to "are you sure?". The SQL runs in the page. Run each block after the ones above it.

### 1. The five-minute shape

**Idea.** A *readout* is a short talk that ends in a decision. Five parts of about a minute each: the decision, the one chart, the evidence, what could be wrong, and what would change it. GitLab's posting for a data analyst asks for "clear status updates to stakeholders" and reports that are "actionable": the decision comes first because it is the part the reader acts on.

**Try it.** The numbers of the evidence: each cafe's completed orders since April 1, the three cafes' same 91 days, and the share taken at 7 a.m.

<RunSQL query={`CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW orders_done AS
SELECT o.order_id, s.store_name AS store, o.ordered_at
FROM orders o
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = o.store_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed';

SELECT store,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7) AS orders_at_7,
       COUNT(*) AS orders,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7) / COUNT(*), 1) AS share_pct
FROM orders_done
WHERE ordered_at >= DATE '2026-04-01'
GROUP BY store
ORDER BY store;`} />

**Predict:** how does Harbor's share of orders at 7 a.m. compare with the older cafes'?

Expected: 3 rows, 4 columns.

*Why:* Harbor took 20 of its 355 orders at 7 a.m., 5.6 percent; Campus took 43 of 362 and Old Town 45 of 365, 11.9 and 12.3 percent. The first three parts of the script write themselves:

```text
1. Decision (30 s): no extra 7 a.m. barista at Harbor yet; I will look again in September.
2. The chart (60 s): in the same 91 days Harbor took 20 orders at 7 a.m.; Campus took 43 and Old Town 45.
3. Evidence (90 s): at Harbor 5.6 percent of orders come at 7 a.m.; at the older cafes about 12.
4. What could be wrong (60 s): unit 2.
5. What would change it (60 s): unit 3.
```

### 2. Three questions to expect

**Idea.** A reader asks one of three things: is the number right, is the comparison fair, how sure are you. Prepare a check for each before you walk in. The first is answered by the checks of chapter 6, run each Monday. For the third, a count has a rough range. The NIST handbook gives the standard deviation of a Poisson count, which models events in a time interval, as the square root of its mean. Treating the count as bell-shaped around its mean (a normal approximation, less exact for small counts) puts about 95 percent of counts within 1.96 standard deviations: the count plus or minus 1.96 times its square root.

Sam asks the second question: "Is that a fair comparison?"

**Try it.** Orders at 7 a.m. since January 1, and since April 1:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7) AS since_january,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7 AND ordered_at >= DATE '2026-04-01') AS since_april
FROM orders_done
GROUP BY store
ORDER BY store;`} />

**Predict:** do the older cafes' counts since April stay near their counts since January?

Expected: 3 rows, 3 columns.

*Why:* Campus falls from 79 to 43 and Old Town from 73 to 45, because six months is twice as many days; Harbor's 20 all fall since April. The dashboard's 79 and 73 against 20 was not like with like. The readout uses 91 days for all three.

**Try it.** The third question, "20 is a tiny number": the rough range of each count since April 1.

<RunSQL query={`WITH n AS (
  SELECT store, COUNT(*) AS orders_at_7
  FROM orders_done
  WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7 AND ordered_at >= DATE '2026-04-01'
  GROUP BY store
)
SELECT store, orders_at_7,
       ROUND(orders_at_7 - 1.96 * SQRT(orders_at_7)) AS low,
       ROUND(orders_at_7 + 1.96 * SQRT(orders_at_7)) AS high
FROM n
ORDER BY store;`} />

**Predict:** does Harbor's range reach the older cafes' ranges?

Expected: 3 rows, 4 columns.

*Why:* Harbor's runs from 11 to 29, Campus's from 30 to 56 and Old Town's from 32 to 58. They do not overlap, but only just. The honest answer to "is it tiny?" is that the count is small, and the gap is still larger than the range alone would usually allow. The range also assumes a steady rate through the week, which Exercise 2 puts in doubt, so read it as rough.

### 3. Answer "are you sure?" with a check

**Idea.** "Are you sure?" asks for the check behind a claim. Three answers fail: a louder yes gives nothing to test, a retreat throws away what you ran, and a new claim answers another question. A good answer has three parts: *what I checked, what it showed, what it cannot show*. If the check shows you wrong, say so and change the claim.

Sam says: "Give it time. Harbor will catch up."

**Try it.** Orders at 7 a.m. for each cafe and month since April:

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store, strftime(ordered_at, '%Y-%m') AS month, COUNT(*) AS orders_at_7
FROM orders_done
WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7 AND ordered_at >= DATE '2026-04-01'
GROUP BY store, month
ORDER BY store, month;`} />

**Predict:** what is the lowest month of an older cafe, and how high does Harbor get?

Expected: 9 rows, 3 columns.

*Why:* the lowest older month is 10 (Campus, April); Harbor's best is 9, in May, and June fell to 4. A month one order short of the lowest older month is not a trend. A number written before July is: that is your answer to "what would change your mind?".

```text
Checked: Harbor's 7 a.m. orders in each month since April, against the older cafes'.
Showed: 7, 9 and 4, where the lowest older month is 10; May was one short.
Cannot show: why Harbor is quiet, or what July holds.
Would change my mind: two months in a row at 10 or more.
```

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Five parts, about a minute each: decision, chart, evidence, what could be wrong, what would change it.
- Expect three questions: is the number right, is the comparison fair, how sure are you.
- Answer "are you sure?" with what you checked, what it showed and what it cannot show.
- Write the number that would change your mind before the data arrives.

**Common trap:** answering "are you sure?" with a firmer yes.

</Aside>

## Finish it

<Exercise id="data-analyst-presenting-findings-1">

**Is the till even open at 7?** Sam: "Maybe Harbor does not really serve at 7." The editor counts the days since April 1 for each cafe. Add the days with at least one order at 7 a.m., then write the answer in three parts. Run unit 1 first. Expected: 3 rows, 3 columns.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT store,
       COUNT(DISTINCT CAST(ordered_at AS DATE)) AS days
FROM orders_done
WHERE ordered_at >= DATE '2026-04-01'
GROUP BY store
ORDER BY store;`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

`COUNT(DISTINCT CAST(ordered_at AS DATE)) FILTER (WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7)` counts the days that have such an order.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

```sql
SELECT store,
       COUNT(DISTINCT CAST(ordered_at AS DATE)) AS days,
       COUNT(DISTINCT CAST(ordered_at AS DATE)) FILTER (WHERE ...) AS days_with_7
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT store,
       COUNT(DISTINCT CAST(ordered_at AS DATE)) AS days,
       COUNT(DISTINCT CAST(ordered_at AS DATE)) FILTER (WHERE hour(ordered_at) = 7) AS days_with_7
FROM orders_done
WHERE ordered_at >= DATE '2026-04-01'
GROUP BY store
ORDER BY store;
```

Harbor took an order at 7 a.m. on 18 of 91 days, Campus on 33 and Old Town on 39. Checked: the days with an order at 7. Showed: Harbor does serve at 7, on about one day in five (20 percent), against 36 percent of days at Campus and 43 at Old Town. Cannot show: whether the till is staffed from 7:00, since the data holds orders, not opening hours.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<RunSQL query={`-- Write your query here, then press Run.`} />

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

`dayname(ordered_at)` gives the weekday's name. Order the rows by `isodow(ordered_at)` so that Monday comes first.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"Seven weekdays, small counts, and one of them far above the rest."*

```sql
SELECT dayname(ordered_at) AS weekday, COUNT(*) AS orders_at_7
FROM orders_done
WHERE store = 'Harbor' AND ...
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT dayname(ordered_at) AS weekday, COUNT(*) AS orders_at_7
FROM orders_done
WHERE store = 'Harbor' AND hour(ordered_at) = 7
GROUP BY weekday, isodow(ordered_at)
ORDER BY isodow(ordered_at);
```

Friday has 8 of the 20 orders; the other days have 1 to 3. Each weekday occurs 13 times in the 91 days. Spread evenly, 20 orders over 7 weekdays would give about 2.9 a weekday (20 / 7), so 8 on one weekday is far above an even spread, and chance alone is an unlikely explanation. Checked: the count for each weekday. Showed: Friday stands out, at 8 orders in 13 Fridays, about 0.6 a Friday. Cannot show: why, or whether it lasts. A number to change your mind: 5 or more orders at 7 a.m. across the five July Fridays, which would justify a Friday-only trial.

</Solution>

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague opens her readout with: "Harbor's 7 a.m. brings in almost nothing, so close the shop until 8." The editor counts Harbor's completed orders by hour.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT hour(ordered_at) AS hr, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders_done
WHERE store = 'Harbor'
GROUP BY hr
ORDER BY hr;`} />

Run unit 1 first. Expected: 11 rows, 2 columns. Run it, then say what the data supports and what it does not.

<Solution>

Harbor took 20 orders at 7 a.m. and 28 to 43 in every other hour. The hour is the quietest, by 8 orders (noon has 28), not empty. "Almost nothing" exaggerates how quiet it is, and "close the shop" asks for a decision that the file cannot support, because it holds orders and no costs. A line that matches the check: "7 a.m. is Harbor's quietest hour, 20 orders, eight fewer than the next quietest."

</Solution>
