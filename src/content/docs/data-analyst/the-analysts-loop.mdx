## The question

Dana owns the three cafes. She sends one line:

> Sales feel down lately. Can you look into it and tell me what to do?

That is a request, not a question: nothing in it can be computed. A *scoped question* is a question with its edges drawn: it says what to measure, what to compare it with, and which dates and cafes, so a query can answer it. Two more words from the title and the goals: an *assumption* is a choice you make where the request is silent, written down so that someone can say no to it, and to *wrangle* data is to clean it and join its tables until it is fit to count. The data is `cafe-sales`, three stores and six months of orders:

| store_id | store_name | city | opened_on |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Old Town | Oakland | 2023-05-12 |
| 2 | Campus | Berkeley | 2024-09-03 |
| 3 | Harbor | Alameda | 2026-04-01 |

## Learn by doing

About 40 minutes, 5 short ideas: the four slots, assumptions and data, the loop, the tool, the memo.

### 1. A request has four empty slots

**Idea.** A request becomes a question when four slots are filled: the *decision* it feeds, the *measure* (a metric as in C12), the *comparison* (against what) and the *window* (which rows and dates). Ask one question for each slot. A job posting from GitLab, a software company, for a Staff Data Analyst lists the same skill as translating ambiguous business questions into structured analytical frameworks.

Dana answers your four questions:

| Slot | You ask | Dana says |
|---|---|---|
| Decision | What will you do differently if sales are down? | "Run a July promotion, or not. I decide on Friday." |
| Measure | What number would show it? | "What the tills (cash registers) took: money from completed orders." |
| Comparison | Down compared with what? | "May." |
| Window | Which dates, which cafes? | "June, all three." |

**Predict:** write the scoped question as one sentence from these answers: a measure, two windows and a reference that tells a real drop from an ordinary month.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

> Did revenue per day, across the three cafes, fall in June compared with May by more than the usual swing between months?

</details>

*Why:* each phrase fills a slot. "Revenue per day" is the measure, per day because May has 31 days and June 30: windows of different length cannot be compared as totals. "The usual swing" is the reference: a fall matters only against how much months normally move. The decision, the promotion, is not in the sentence; it decides how sure you need to be.

### 2. Write down what you assumed and what you need

**Idea.** An *assumption* is a choice the request did not make for you. Write each one so that someone can say no to it. The *minimum data* is the fewest tables and columns that can answer the question; list it before you extract.

Two assumptions for Dana so far: sales are completed orders, quantity times price minus discount, and a day is a calendar day on the till's clock. The minimum data is four columns of `orders`, four of `order_items` and the price in `products`.

**Try it:** check that the data covers the window, one row per store.

<RunSQL query={`SELECT s.store_name,
       MIN(CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE)) AS first_day,
       MAX(CAST(o.ordered_at AS DATE)) AS last_day,
       COUNT(*) AS orders,
       SUM(CASE WHEN o.status = 'canceled' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS canceled
FROM orders o
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = o.store_id
GROUP BY s.store_name
ORDER BY first_day, s.store_name;`} />

Expected: 3 rows, 5 columns; only Harbor starts late, on 2026-04-01. Now probe a column the question depends on.

**Try it.**

<RunSQL query={`SELECT order_id, product_id, quantity
FROM order_items
WHERE quantity > 3;`} />

**Predict:** a till sells one to three of an item at a time. How many lines have a quantity above 3?

Expected: the columns `order_id`, `product_id` and `quantity`.

*Why:* one line has a quantity of 20, on order 2075, a Turkey Sandwich: a 2 typed with an extra zero, and it sits in Old Town's May bar of the first figure. Probing a column before you trust it is the cheapest check of the loop. It adds the third assumption: that line is shown as recorded and corrected.

### 3. Plan the loop before you run it

**Idea.** Each stage leaves one thing behind and ends with one check. A failed check sends you back one stage; a surprise at the end can send you back to the question.

| Stage | For Dana's question | Check before the next stage |
|---|---|---|
| Extract | revenue lines, January to June | rows per store and month look right |
| Wrangle | completed orders only; the 20-unit line marked | row count before and after each filter |
| Summarize | revenue per day by month, and the usual swing | one total reconciled a second way |
| Visualize | one chart of the months side by side | the title states the finding |
| Communicate | a memo that ends in a decision | the decision is the first line |

### 4. Pick the tool the question needs

**Idea.** Choose the tool by the shape of the work, not by habit; this is a rule of thumb.

| Stay in a spreadsheet when | Move to SQL when | Move to Python when |
|---|---|---|
| one file that fits a sheet, answered once | tables to join, or a rerun next month | long cleaning, a model or a custom chart |

A sheet holds 1,048,576 rows, so the 1,800 orders fit. Dana's question still goes to SQL: it joins three tables and will be asked again in July.

### 5. End in a memo

Before the work starts, send Dana the question, in the format of C15.

> **Decision:** approve this question by Wednesday, and the answer is ready on Friday: did revenue per day across the three cafes fall in June compared with May by more than the usual swing between months?
> **Context:** you asked whether sales are down before you decide on a July promotion.
> **Tension:** the months differ in length, Harbor opened in April, and one May line has a quantity of 20 where 2 was probably meant; each can make "down" look bigger or smaller.
> **Evidence:** the data covers January 1 to June 30 for all three cafes, 1,800 orders of which 1 was canceled, with every column the question needs.
> **Recommendation:** approve the question and its three assumptions (completed orders, revenue per day, the 20-unit line shown both ways), or name another comparison by Wednesday.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Four slots make a question: decision, measure, comparison, window.
- Write assumptions so that someone can refuse them, and list the minimum data.
- Five stages, one thing left behind and one check each.
- Spreadsheet for one file, SQL for joins and reruns, Python for long cleaning and models.
- Send the question as a memo before the work starts.

**Common trap:** answering as written ("sales by month") with a table no one can act on.

</Aside>

**Is the new store taking customers from the old ones?** Dana asks. The decision (keep Harbor's opening-week offer) and the measure (completed orders in a month at Old Town and Campus) are filled in. Write the *comparison* and the *window* as two comment lines in the editor, then run the query to see whether the data holds your window. Expected: 12 rows, 3 columns.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

`CAST(ordered_at AS TIME)` keeps only the time of day: the `TIME` type holds the hour, minute and second of a day. `MEDIAN` is an aggregate, like `AVG`, that returns the middle value of a group of values, and it works on times too. Take each store's first order of each day in a `WITH … AS (…)` block, a CTE as in [*Readable queries with CTEs*](/sql/ctes/) (S13), then `MIN` and `MEDIAN` of those times for each store.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"One row for each store: a store name and two times of day."*

```sql
WITH first_orders AS (
  SELECT store_id, CAST(ordered_at AS DATE) AS day, MIN(CAST(ordered_at AS TIME)) AS first_time
  ...
)
```

</details>

<Solution>

```sql
WITH first_orders AS (
  SELECT store_id, CAST(ordered_at AS DATE) AS day, MIN(CAST(ordered_at AS TIME)) AS first_time
  FROM orders
  GROUP BY store_id, day
)
SELECT s.store_name, MIN(f.first_time) AS earliest, MEDIAN(f.first_time) AS median_first
FROM first_orders f
JOIN stores s ON s.store_id = f.store_id
GROUP BY s.store_name
ORDER BY s.store_name;
```

The earliest order is at 07:00:00 at Campus, 07:01:00 at Old Town and 07:06:00 at Harbor, so no order is stamped before 07:00:00 and the data cannot show demand at 6:30. Campus's median first order of a day is 08:51:00, so ask what "first thing" means. The minimum data does not exist: say so, and propose a two-week trial that records the orders between 6:30 and 7:00.

</Solution>
