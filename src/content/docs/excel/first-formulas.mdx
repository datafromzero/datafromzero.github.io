A **formula** is an instruction you type into a cell. It starts with `=` and reads other cells by
their addresses. The cell shows the result; the formula stays in the formula bar. When a cell the
formula reads changes, the result changes by itself. That is the difference between a formula and a
number you typed.

A formula does arithmetic with `+`, `-`, `*` (times) and `/` (divided by). When it mixes them, Excel
works in the order you know from school: parentheses first, then multiplication and division, then
addition and subtraction, and steps of the same rank from left to right. Try to predict the result of
each formula below, then open the answers.

| Formula |
|---|
| `=5+2*3` |
| `=(5+2)*3` |
| `=10-4-3` |
| `=12/4*3` |

<details>
<summary>Reveal the results</summary>

| Formula | Result | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| `=5+2*3` | 11 | multiplication runs before addition |
| `=(5+2)*3` | 21 | parentheses run first |
| `=10-4-3` | 3 | same rank, left to right: (10 − 4) − 3 |
| `=12/4*3` | 9 | same rank, left to right: (12 / 4) × 3 |

</details>

Functions do the repetitive work. A <Term id="glossary-range-spreadsheet">**range**</Term> such as `F2:F8` names the first and the last cell, and
the function reads every cell between them.

| Function | What it gives |
|---|---|
| `SUM(F2:F8)` | the sum of the numbers |
| `COUNT(F2:F8)` | how many cells hold a number |
| `AVERAGE(F2:F8)` | the average of the numbers |
| `MIN(F2:F8)` | the smallest number |
| `MAX(F2:F8)` | the largest number |

All five skip empty cells and text inside the range. That matters for `AVERAGE`: an empty cell is left
out, it is not counted as a zero, so three numbers and one empty cell average over 3, not over 4.

## Worked example

Question: **does the till agree with the manager's 4 orders and \$36.75?** Open the practice file and
follow along.

1. **Copy the data to `calc`.** On `raw_oldtown_jan5`, select everything (**Ctrl+A**, on a Mac
   **Cmd+A**), copy it, click cell **A1** on `calc` and paste.
   *Why:* the raw sheet stays exactly as it arrived. You work on the copy.

   **On a phone (Google Sheets app):** tap the down arrow on the `raw_oldtown_jan5` tab and tap
   **Duplicate**. Wherever a step says `calc`, work on the copy instead.

2. **Say what one row is and what the new column holds.** One row is one line of an order. The new
   column, `line_usd`, is the money that line brought in: quantity times unit price, minus the
   discount. Type `line_usd` in **F1**.
   *Why:* write the expected result in words before you write the formula, so you know what to check
   it against. This is also how the dataset defines the amount of a line.

3. **Write the formula in the first row only.** Click **F2**, type `=C2*D2-E2` and press Enter. F2
   shows **4.25**.
   *Why each part:* `=` starts a formula. `C2`, `D2` and `E2` are the cells it reads: quantity, unit
   price and discount. `*` runs before `-`, so the line is (quantity × price) − discount. Instead of
   typing an address you can click the cell: the app writes the address for you.

4. **Predict, then fill down.** Before you copy it, say what F3 will contain. Then select **F2:F8** and
   press **Ctrl+D** (on a Mac **Cmd+D**), in Excel or in Google Sheets. In Excel you can also drag the
   fill handle, the small square at the corner of the selected cell. Click F3: it holds
   `=C3*D3-E3`.
   *Why:* an address in a formula is relative to the cell that holds the formula. Moving down one
   row moves every address down one row, so each line reads its own quantity, price and discount.
   The next lesson explains this and shows how to stop it when you need to.

   **On a phone (Google Sheets app):** tap **F2** and drag the corner of the selection down to **F8**.
   Tap the selected cells, then tap **Autofill** (tap **More** first if you do not see it).

5. **Summarize to the right of the data.** Keep a blank column between the data and your notes, and
   no totals inside the data. In **H1** type `measure` and in **I1** type `value`, then fill in
   these pairs:

   | H | I | Result |
   |---|---|---:|
   | `total_usd` | `=SUM(F2:F8)` | 36.75 |
   | `lines` | `=COUNT(F2:F8)` | 7 |
   | `average_line_usd` | `=AVERAGE(F2:F8)` | 5.25 |
   | `smallest_usd` | `=MIN(F2:F8)` | 3.25 |
   | `largest_usd` | `=MAX(F2:F8)` | 9.75 |

   *Why start at F2:* the header in F1 is text and would be skipped anyway, but a range that starts
   at the first record says exactly what you mean.

6. **Check it against something you did not calculate.**
   - The manager said **\$36.75**, and `=SUM(F2:F8)` gives **36.75**.
   - The manager said **4 orders**. `COUNT` gives 7, because it counts lines. Look at column A instead:
     the different `order_id` values are 1027, 1033, 1034 and 1035, so **4 orders**.
   - Add the seven line amounts by hand: 4.25 + 3.50 + 5.00 + 6.00 + 5.00 + 9.75 + 3.25 = 36.75.
   - Change **D2** to 5.25 and watch the total become **37.75** by itself. Then put 4.25 back.

The till agrees with the manager: **4 orders, \$36.75.**

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why type the formula once and fill it down, instead of typing the seven results?</summary>

Because the formula is the rule, and the rule keeps working. If a price or a quantity changes, or a
new line is added and filled, the result follows. Seven typed numbers would silently go stale, and a
typo in one of them would hide among the others.

</details>

<Aside type="note" title="Commas or semicolons?">
Arguments in this course are separated by commas, as in `=SUM(F2:F8)`. Which separator your Excel
expects follows your computer's regional settings: Microsoft notes that a comma as the decimal mark
makes Excel use a semicolon as the list separator, the character between a formula's arguments. If
your Excel rejects a comma, use a semicolon. It is the same formula.
</Aside>

<Exercise id="excel-first-formulas-3">

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague totals January 15 (`raw_oldtown_jan15`, eight lines). In F2 they write
`=C2*(D2-E2)` and fill it down. Their total is **51.25**. The till's closing report says **52.25**.
The colleague's file is the sheet `calc_ex3`. What went wrong, and how would you find the cause?

<Solution>

The real total is 52.25. The discount column holds the discount on the **whole line**, so the line is
(quantity × price) − discount. Their parentheses take the discount off each unit first, so a line of
two Mochas loses 2 × 0.50 = 1.00 instead of 0.50, and comes out at 9.00 instead of 9.50. Two lines
are like that (orders 1107 and 1109, rows 6 and 9), 0.50 each, so the total is 1.00 short.

The Cappuccino on order 1107 has quantity 1, so it comes out right with either formula. If you spot
check only that row, you will not see the problem. Check a discounted row whose quantity is above 1,
by hand: 2 × 5.00 − 0.50 = 9.50, but the sheet says 9.00. The fix is `=C2*D2-E2`.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
