This lesson works the same in Microsoft Excel and in Google Sheets. Where the two apps differ, both
ways are shown. It teaches no particular AI product: "the assistant" means any chatbot or in-sheet
assistant that drafts formulas for you. The replies shown here are examples written for this lesson,
not the output of a real product, and you can try the same prompts in whichever assistant you use.

**Your practice file:** download
[`excel-ai-draft-and-verify.xlsx`](https://github.com/datafromzero/datafromzero.github.io/raw/main/sheets/excel/excel-ai-draft-and-verify.xlsx)
and open it in Excel, or upload it to Google Drive and open it with Google Sheets.
It holds real-looking (but invented) data from **cafe-sales**, a small chain of three cafes that we
use through the whole roadmap. The main sheet, `raw_order_lines`, has one row per order line, as in the
pivot lessons. A second sheet, `raw_known_answer`, holds eleven of those lines, small enough to total by
hand.

## Warm-up

Answer from memory before you open anything. Then click to check.

1. On `raw_order_lines`, `store` is in column D and `status` in column K. Write the formula that counts
   the completed lines of the store Old Town.

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `=COUNTIFS(D:D,"Old Town",K:K,"completed")`. It gives 1,337.

   </details>

   calculation one part at a time.

   </details>

3. In one sentence: what is one row of `raw_order_lines`?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   One order line: one product in one order. An order has one or more lines, so a row is not an order.
   You will need that sentence today.

   </details>

## The question

The owner of the cafes asks:

> An assistant wrote me a formula for the average order value on Monday's screen, and it says 6.92.
> That looks reasonable. Can I trust it?

An assistant is good at a quick first draft, at naming the right function, and at explaining a
formula you inherited. It does not see your sheet, it does not know what *you* mean by "an order", and it
cannot see whether its answer matches your data. NIST, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, calls the failure *confabulation*: "the production of
confidently stated but erroneous or false content". A wrong formula arrives in the same calm tone as a
right one.

So the rule of this lesson is the one you would apply to a new junior colleague: **the draft is a
draft until it passes a check.** By the end you will have caught a plausible-looking wrong answer with
a number worked out by hand.

The loop has four steps: **ask** (say the task, the columns and the expected output), **draft** (the
assistant answers), **verify** (compare the draft with a known answer you worked out yourself), and
**decide** (accept it if they match; otherwise reject it, fix the prompt or the formula, and verify
again). The second picture is the *known answer*: seven orders, eleven lines, small enough to total on
paper, and chosen so that it contains the awkward case, a canceled order (1461).

## Worked example

Question: **what is the average order value of completed orders, and is the assistant's formula right?**
Open the practice file and follow along.

1. **Write the prompt: task, columns, expected output.** An example:

   > *My sheet is `raw_order_lines`. Columns: A order_line_id, B order_id, C date, D store, E product,
   > F category, G quantity, H unit_price_usd, I discount_usd, J amount_usd, K status. One row is one
   > order line, and an order has one or more lines. `status` is "completed" or "canceled". Write a
   > formula for the average order value of completed orders: total sales divided by the number of
   > orders. Expected output: one number, in US dollars.*

   *Why:* the assistant cannot see your sheet, so you say what is in it, what one row is, and what you
   expect back. A prompt that states the expected output also makes a wrong answer easier to spot. Notice
   what it contains: column names and a description, and no customer data.

2. **Read the draft before you use it.** The assistant replies (an example written for this lesson):

   > *`=AVERAGEIFS(J:J,K:K,"completed")` averages the amount over the rows where the status is
   > completed.*

   *Why read it first:* look at what it averages. `J` is the amount of one **line**, and the average runs
   over lines, not orders. You asked for orders. On the whole sheet it gives **6.92**, which looks
   like a perfectly reasonable average. Do not trust it yet.

3. **Work out a known answer by hand.** On `raw_known_answer`, total each completed order on paper. Order
   1458 is 6.50 + 3.50 + 4.25 = 14.25. The six completed orders come to 14.25, 14.00, 13.50, 13.50, 4.00
   and 17.00: **76.25**. The canceled order 1461 is left out. So the average order is
   76.25 ÷ 6 = **12.71**. *Why by hand, and why before you run the draft:* if you see the formula's
   number first, it will pull your own arithmetic toward it.

4. **Run the draft on the known answer.** Raw sheets are never edited, so on `calc` type the draft with the
   sheet name in front of each range:
   `=AVERAGEIFS(raw_known_answer!J:J,raw_known_answer!K:K,"completed")`. It shows **7.625**. That is not 12.71: **reject it**. Find why:
   76.25 ÷ 10 completed lines = 7.625. The draft averaged lines. *Why this check works:* on the whole
   sheet both numbers, 6.92 and 12.65, look plausible. Only a small case with a known answer shows
   which one is right.

5. **Fix it, and verify again.** Ask the assistant again with the grain stated, or write it yourself:

   `=SUMIFS(raw_known_answer!J:J,raw_known_answer!K:K,"completed")/COUNTA(UNIQUE(FILTER(raw_known_answer!B:B,raw_known_answer!K:K="completed")))`

   On `calc` it gives 76.25 ÷ 6 = **12.71**: a match. On the whole sheet:

   `=SUMIFS(raw_order_lines!J:J,raw_order_lines!K:K,"completed")/COUNTA(UNIQUE(FILTER(raw_order_lines!B:B,raw_order_lines!K:K="completed")))`

   gives **12.65**. Confirm with a second route: the pivot from the earlier lessons has a grand total of
   22,749.25, and the dataset has 1,799 completed orders (1,800 minus the canceled one);
   22,749.25 ÷ 1,799 = 12.6455. *Why a second route:* a formula that passes one check can still be
   wrong; two independent routes agreeing is the evidence.

6. **Keep a log.** On `calc`, write what happened: the prompt, the assistant you used, the date, the
   check you ran, and the result (*rejected: averaged lines; replaced by sales ÷ distinct orders;
   verified against the known answer and the pivot*). *Why:* when you share the file, the reader sees
   what was drafted by a machine and what was verified.

That answers the owner: **no, 6.92 is the average line. The average order is 12.65.**

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why did step 3 come before step 4?</summary>

Because a known answer only protects you if it is independent. If you compute it after seeing the draft's
number, you will tend to find the arithmetic that agrees. Worked out first, on paper, it can disagree, and
that is exactly the point of it.

</details>

<Aside type="caution" title="What you must never paste into an assistant">
An assistant is a service you send text to. NIST lists "data privacy" among the risks of generative AI:
"leakage and unauthorized use, disclosure, or de-anonymization of biometric, health, location, or other
personally identifiable information or sensitive data". So do not paste: names, emails, phone numbers or
addresses of real people; customer or employee lists; payment details; passwords, keys or access links;
confidential financial results or contracts; or anything your employer or school forbids. Your
organization's rule comes first. Paste column names and three invented rows instead, as in step 1. In this
course every row is invented, so nothing you paste here is private; in your job, it could be.
</Aside>

<Exercise id="excel-ai-draft-and-verify-1">

**An assistant drafts the total sales of Old Town. Do you accept it?** The draft is
`=SUMIFS(H:H,D:D,"Old Town",K:K,"completed")`. Work on the empty sheet `calc_ex1`, and put the sheet
name in front of each range (`raw_known_answer!H:H`, and so on). Expected output: accept or reject, with the
known answer and the draft's number side by side.

The first steps are done for you:

1. The prompt stated the columns, one row per order line, and "total sales of completed orders".
2. You read the draft: it adds column `H`, and `H` is `unit_price_usd`.

Now finish it: work out the known answer, run the draft on the known frame, and decide.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the Old Town orders in `raw_known_answer` (1458, 1460 and 1463). Add each one's amount by hand, then
see what the draft adds for the same lines.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

The Old Town orders are 14.25, 13.50 and 4.00 by hand. Point the draft at `raw_known_answer` from `calc_ex1` and compare. Then

</details>

<Solution>

1. **Known answer by hand:** 14.25 + 13.50 + 4.00 = **31.75** for Old Town on `raw_known_answer`.
2. **Run the draft there:** on `calc_ex1`,
   `=SUMIFS(raw_known_answer!H:H,raw_known_answer!D:D,"Old Town",raw_known_answer!K:K,"completed")` adds the
   unit prices of those five lines: 3.25 + 3.50 + 4.25 + 4.50 + 4.50 = **20.00**. It ignores quantity and discount.
3. **Decide: reject.** 20.00 is not 31.75. The cause is the wrong column: `H` is the price of *one unit*;
   the amount of the line is `J` (`amount_usd`), which is quantity × unit price − discount.
4. **Fixed:** `=SUMIFS(raw_known_answer!J:J,raw_known_answer!D:D,"Old Town",raw_known_answer!K:K,"completed")`
   gives 31.75 on the frame, and the same formula with `raw_order_lines!` in front of each range gives
   **9,144.25** on the whole sheet, the Old Town total from the pivot lessons. The rejected draft gives
   5,815.25 there: a believable number, and 3,329.00 short.

The lesson of the exercise: the wrong total is not absurd, so only a known answer exposes it.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## On your own

<Exercise id="excel-ai-draft-and-verify-2">

**Verify an assistant's summary of Harbor.** First, write the prompt you would send to get a short summary

below, claim by claim, and mark each one *true* or *false* with the number you checked it against:

> *Harbor sold 4,606.25 from 355 orders. Coffee was its biggest category. Its sales grew every month.
> The Latte was its best-selling product. The average order was about 13 dollars.*

Before you check anything, write one sentence saying what the result should look like. Work on the empty sheet `calc_ex2`;
do not change the raw sheets.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Five claims, five checks. A pivot with `store` = Harbor, `status` = completed, and `date` grouped by month

</details>

<details>

<Solution>

**A prompt that would do.** *"My sheet is `raw_order_lines` (columns A to K: order_line_id, order_id, date,
store, product, category, quantity, unit_price_usd, discount_usd, amount_usd, status; one row is one order
line; status is completed or canceled). Summarize the Harbor store's completed sales in five short sentences:
total sales, number of orders, biggest category, the trend by month, best product. Expected output: five
sentences, each with a number I can check."*

**The checks:**

| Claim | Checked against | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Sold 4,606.25 from 355 orders | pivot grand total 4,606.25; `UNIQUE`/`FILTER` count 355 | true |
| Coffee is the biggest category | coffee 2,164.50; food 1,041.25; bakery 729.00; tea 671.50 | true |
| Sales grew every month | April 1,596.50, May 1,546.50, June 1,463.25 | **false: they fell every month** |
| The Latte was the best-selling product | Latte 624.50 in sales and 140 units; next, Avocado Toast 475.00 | true |
| The average order was about 13 dollars | 4,606.25 ÷ 355 = 12.98 | true |

One claim in five is wrong, and it is the one that sounds like a story. A good summary would say: *"sales
fell each month: 1,596.50 in April, 1,546.50 in May and 1,463.25 in June."* Check each number the same way
before you pass a machine's summary on, and note in your log which claims you verified.

</Solution>

**Spot the flaw.** For Harbor's total sales, an assistant drafts `=SUMIF(D:D,"Harbor",J:J)`. A colleague
tests it: it gives **4,606.25**, which matches the Harbor pivot, so she accepts it. A week later she uses the same
formula with `"Campus"` on the owner's screen. What went wrong, and what check would have caught it?

<Solution>

The draft ignores `status`: it adds canceled lines as well as completed ones. The test passed only by luck:
Harbor has **no canceled lines**, so its total is the same with or without the condition. Campus has the
dataset's one canceled order (order 1461, a 5.00 Mocha), so the draft gives **9,003.75** for Campus while the
completed sales are **8,998.75**: 5.00 too much, and no one would notice.

A known-answer check must contain the trap. Pointed at `raw_known_answer`, which includes order 1461
(`=SUMIF(raw_known_answer!D:D,"Campus",raw_known_answer!J:J)`, typed on any sheet that is not a raw one), the
draft gives **49.50** for Campus, while the completed orders add up to 14.00 + 13.50 + 17.00 = **44.50** by hand.
Rejected at once. The fix is the condition the draft left out:
`=SUMIFS(J:J,D:D,"Campus",K:K,"completed")`.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
