This lesson works the same in Microsoft Excel and in Google Sheets. In Excel the tool is called a
PivotTable; in Google Sheets it is a pivot table. Where the two apps differ, both ways are shown.

**Your practice file:** download
[`excel-pivottables-build.xlsx`](https://github.com/datafromzero/datafromzero.github.io/raw/main/sheets/excel/excel-pivottables-build.xlsx)
and open it in Excel, or upload it to Google Drive and open it with Google Sheets.
It holds real-looking (but invented) data from **cafe-sales**, a small chain of three cafes that we
use through the whole roadmap. This time the main sheet, `raw_order_lines`, has one row per order
line: 3,287 rows, with the date, store, product, category, quantity, prices, amount and status of
each line.

## Warm-up

Answer from memory before you open anything. Then click to check.

1. On `raw_order_lines`, `store` is in column D and `amount_usd` in column J. Write the formula that
   totals `amount_usd` for the store Harbor.

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `=SUMIFS(J:J,D:D,"Harbor")`. The range to add comes first, then each range with its condition.
   It gives one number for one store. Today you will get that number for every store at once.

   </details>

2. How can you tell that a column of amounts is stored as text and not as numbers?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The cells line up on the left, `ISNUMBER` returns `FALSE`, and when you select the column the
   status bar shows a Count but no Sum.

   </details>

3. What does a single header row make possible when you turn a range into a Table?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Every column gets a name, and the Table grows when you add rows below it. Tools that read a table,
   such as a PivotTable, can use the names and the whole list.

   </details>

## The question

The owner of the cafes asks:

> Which category brings in the most money at each store, and do the three stores sell the same mix?

You could answer with twelve `SUMIFS` formulas: three stores times four categories. Here is the top of
the sheet the answer comes from (`raw_order_lines`):

| order_line_id | order_id | date | store | product | category | quantity | unit_price_usd | discount_usd | amount_usd | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 5001 | 1001 | 2026-01-01 | Campus | Croissant | bakery | 1 | 3.25 | 0.00 | 3.25 | completed |
| 5002 | 1001 | 2026-01-01 | Campus | Cold Brew | coffee | 1 | 4.75 | 0.00 | 4.75 | completed |
| 5003 | 1001 | 2026-01-01 | Campus | Espresso | coffee | 2 | 3.00 | 0.00 | 6.00 | completed |

By the end of this lesson you will build that table of twelve answers in under a minute, check that
it adds up to the source, and write what it shows in one sentence.

A **PivotTable** takes a table of records and shows a summary of it. You choose where each field goes:

- **Rows**: a field whose values become the row labels (here, `store`);
- **Columns**: a field whose values become the column labels (here, `category`);
- **Values**: the number to add up or count in each cell (here, `amount_usd`);
- **Filters**: a field that hides some rows before anything is added (here, `status`).

Each cell then holds the sum of the source rows that share its row label and its column label. The
pivot never changes the source. (In Excel it works from a snapshot of the source; step 8 shows what
that means.)

## Worked example

Question: **how much did each store sell in each category?** Open the practice file and follow along.

1. **Say what one row is, and what one cell will be.** One row of `raw_order_lines` is one order line.
   One cell of the pivot will be *the sum of `amount_usd` for the completed lines of one store and
   one category*. *Why:* the sentence tells you what to check later. If a cell cannot be said in one
   sentence, the pivot is not ready to build.

2. **Copy the data to `calc` and make it a Table.** On `raw_order_lines`, press **Ctrl+A**, then
   **Ctrl+C** (Mac: **Cmd+A**, **Cmd+C**). On `calc`, click **A1** and paste. In Excel, press
   **Ctrl+T** to make it a Table with a header row. *Why:* the raw sheet stays untouched. Excel Tables
   are the recommended pivot source, because new rows are picked up more easily.
   Then check the copy: click **J2**, press **Ctrl+Shift+Down arrow**. The status bar shows
   **Count: 3287** and **Sum: 22,754.25**. Write both down. *Why:* the Sum shows that `amount_usd`
   holds real numbers, and 22,754.25 is the control total for the whole lesson.

3. **Insert the pivot.**
   - **Excel:** click any cell in the data, then **Insert > PivotTable**. Choose **Existing
     Worksheet**, click cell **A3** on `output`, and select **OK**. The three-sheet habit again:
     `output` holds what you show.
   - **Google Sheets:** select the data including the header row (each column needs a header), then
     **Insert > Pivot table**. Sheets puts the pivot table on its own sheet; you can leave it there.

4. **Place the fields.**
   - **Excel:** in the field list on the right, drag `store` to **Rows**, `category` to **Columns**
     and `amount_usd` to **Values**.
   - **Google Sheets:** in the editor, click **Add** next to **Rows** and choose `store`; do the same
     next to **Columns** for `category` and next to **Values** for `amount_usd`.

   *Why this assignment:* rows and columns say *where* a number goes, values say *which* number. You
   should see three rows (Campus, Harbor, Old Town), four columns (bakery, coffee, food, tea) and a
   grand total. Check that the value says **Sum of amount_usd**.

5. **Reconcile the grand total.** The grand total is **22,754.25**, the same as the status-bar Sum
   in step 2. *Why:* a pivot can silently leave rows out. When the pivot total equals the source
   total, nothing was lost and nothing was counted twice.

6. **Take out the canceled order.**
   - **Excel:** drag `status` to **Filters**, open the filter above the pivot, choose
     **completed** and select **OK**.
   - **Google Sheets:** click **Add** next to **Filters**, choose `status`, then use the dropdown to
     filter by values and keep only **completed**.

   The grand total becomes **22,749.25**. *Why:* the owner asked what was sold, and a canceled order
   is not a sale. The difference, 22,754.25 − 22,749.25 = **5.00**, should be exactly the canceled
   line. Check it: set the filter to **canceled** only, and a single cell shows 5.00 (Campus, coffee:
   one Mocha, order 1461). Then set it back to **completed**.

7. **Change what the cell calculates.**
   - **Excel:** in the field list, click the arrow next to **Sum of amount_usd** in the Values area,
     choose **Value Field Settings** and, under **Summarize Values By**, pick **Count**. (In Excel for
     the web, right-click a value and choose **Summarize Values By**.)
   - **Google Sheets:** in the **Values** card, open the **Summarize by** list and choose a count
     option.

   The grand total is now **3,286**: the number of completed lines, which is the 3,287 rows of the
   source minus the canceled line. Choose **Average** and it reads **6.92**: the average amount of
   a line. *Why:* the grouping stays the same, and only the question changes: how much, how many, or
   how big on average. Switch back to **Sum** when you are done.

8. **See what refresh means.** On `calc`, change the first `amount_usd` (row 2) from 3.25 to 13.25.
   - **Excel:** the pivot still shows 22,749.25. PivotTables work on a snapshot of your data, called
     the cache, so they do not follow the source until you refresh. Right-click the pivot and choose
     **Refresh**: it now shows **22,759.25**. (**PivotTable Analyze > Refresh All** refreshes every
     pivot in the file.)
   - **Google Sheets:** the pivot table changes at once, because it refreshes any time you change the
     source data cells it is drawn from. If you add rows below the range, use **Select data range**
     in the editor to widen it.

   Change the value back to 3.25 and refresh. *Why:* a stale pivot is the most common reason for a
   report that does not match its data. Refresh is the first thing to try.

9. **Write the finding in one sentence.** Read the table, then say what it shows, with numbers:

   > *Coffee is the biggest category at all three stores: 4,507.75 of Old Town's 9,144.25, 4,129.75
   > of Campus's 8,998.75 and 2,164.50 of Harbor's 4,606.25. Harbor opened on April 1, so it has
   > three months of sales against six.*

That answers the owner's question: **coffee leads at every store**, and the mix is similar.

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why did step 5 come before step 6?</summary>

Because the reconciliation needs a total you already trust. Before the filter, the pivot should equal
the whole source column; that proves the pivot reads every row. Only then is it safe to remove rows
on purpose, and the 5.00 you removed is explained by one known line. If you filtered first, a
missing row and a removed row would look the same.

</details>

<Aside type="caution" title="A close result: look at the biggest line behind it">
Old Town (9,144.25) beats Campus (8,998.75) by only 145.50. But one Old Town line, on May 1, is
20 turkey sandwiches worth 185.00. The dataset notes say 2 were meant, an extra zero typed at the
till. With 2 sandwiches (18.50), Old Town would sell 8,977.75 and Campus would lead. A pivot adds
what is there; it cannot tell you a number is a typo. When a ranking is close, look at the largest
rows behind it.
</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="A pivot that counts instead of adds">
Microsoft's page on PivotTables says: "If Excel interprets your data as text, the data is displayed
as a COUNT." So if you drop `amount_usd` into Values and see **Count of amount_usd**, check the
column first: left-aligned cells and no Sum in the status bar mean the amounts are text. Fix the
source, not the pivot.
</Aside>
