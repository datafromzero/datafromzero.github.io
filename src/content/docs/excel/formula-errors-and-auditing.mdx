## Warm-up

Answer from memory before you open anything. Then click to check.

1. In `raw_products` the codes are in A2:A17 and the prices in D2:D17. Write the formula that brings the

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `=XLOOKUP(B2,raw_products!$A$2:$A$17,raw_products!$D$2:$D$17,"not on menu")`. The fourth argument is

   </details>

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `=B3*C3`. A reference that is not locked moves one row with the formula.

   </details>

3. In a lookup, what does `#N/A` mean?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   "Not found": the key you asked for is not in the list you searched.

   </details>

Every formula is a link in a chain. **Precedents** are the cells a formula reads from, **dependents** are
the cells that read from it. An error travels down the chain: one broken cell makes everything below it
show the same error. So the cell where you *see* the error is almost never the cell where it *starts*.
Auditing means walking back up the arrows until you reach a cell that shows an error even though all its
inputs are fine. That is the cause.

The error value itself tells you where to start looking:

| Error | What it says | First look |
|---|---|---|
| `#N/A` | The formula can't find what it was asked to look for. | A lookup: is the key in the list, with the same type and no stray spaces? |
| `#VALUE!` | Something is wrong in how the formula is typed or in the cells it reads. | Text or a stray space where a number should be. |
| `#REF!` | The formula points to a cell that is no longer valid. | A deleted row or column, or a column number beyond the table in a `VLOOKUP`. |
| `#DIV/0!` | The formula divides by zero. | The cell it divides by. |
| `#NAME?` | Excel does not know a name used in the formula. | A misspelled function name, or a name that was never defined. |

## Worked example

Question: **why does `total_usd` show `#N/A`, and what is the right total?** Open `raw_model` and follow
along. Do not change it: it is raw data.

1. **Write the expected output.** On `calc`, click **K1** and type: *"One total for 40 lines that equals
   the sum of 40 line amounts, with no error on the way."* *Why:* a repaired formula only counts if you
   knew beforehand what a good answer looks like.

2. **Read the error, not the formula.** `#N/A` means the formula can't find what it was asked to look for,
   and Microsoft's help says the most common cause is a lookup function. The total is a plain `SUM`,
   so the `SUM` is not the problem. The lookup is in column E. *Why:* the error type narrows the
   search before you click anything.

3. **Trace precedents.** Click **I2** (the total). On the **Formulas** tab, in the **Formula Auditing**
   group, click **Trace Precedents**: an arrow points from the range of amounts. Click a red amount, such
   as **F8**, and click **Trace Precedents** again: the arrows lead to C8, D8 and E8. Microsoft's help
   says that blue arrows point to cells with no errors and red arrows point to cells that cause errors.
   Click **E8** and again **Trace Precedents**: it reads from **B8** and the products table. B8 holds
   only a value, so the trail ends there. **Trace Dependents** does the same in the other direction: on
   B8 it first shows E8, and each further click goes one step down, to F8, then I2 and I3. Click
   **Remove Arrows** when you are done. *Why:* the cause is
   the first cell, going up, whose own inputs are fine. Pressing **F2** in a cell with a formula also
   shows its inputs in color.

4. **Evaluate the formula.** Click **E8**, then **Formulas**, **Formula Auditing**, **Evaluate
   Formula**. The part of the formula in the box that is underlined is the next to be worked out. Click
   **Evaluate**: `B8` turns into `"1"`, with quotation marks, which is how a text value is written. Keep
   clicking **Evaluate** until the last step: `#N/A`. *Why:* you see what the formula really receives.
   The code `1` is in the products table, but this `"1"` is text, and a lookup treats text and a
   number as different.

5. **Find every culprit.** The same typing problem may be elsewhere. On `calc`, in **A2**, type
   `=ISTEXT(raw_model!B2)` and fill down to row 41. Three rows show `TRUE`: rows **8**, **19** and **31**
   (order items 5007, 5018 and 5030). Row 19 is a different kind of text: `"11 "`, with a trailing
   space. *Why:* `#N/A` hides how many cells are wrong. The total fails if only one is.

6. **Repair on `calc`, never on the raw sheet.** In `calc`:
   - **B2:** `=VALUE(TRIM(raw_model!B2))`, the code as a real number (this works on text, on text
     with a trailing space and on numbers);
   - **C2:** `=XLOOKUP(B2,raw_products!$A$2:$A$17,raw_products!$D$2:$D$17)`;
   - **D2:** `=raw_model!C2*C2-raw_model!D2`;
   - fill all three down to row 41, then **D43:** `=SUM(D2:D41)`.

   The total is **247.75** and the average per line is 247.75 ÷ 40 = **6.19**.

7. **Verify.** `=COUNT(D2:D41)` gives **40**: every line has a number. And the sum has a second route.
   The three repaired lines are 5007 (an Espresso, 3.00), 5018 (a Blueberry Muffin, 3.50) and 5030
   (an Espresso, 3.00), together **9.50**. The other 37 lines add up to **238.25**, and 238.25 + 9.50 =
   **247.75**.

8. **Know what `IFERROR` would have done.** Wrap the price in `=IFERROR(...,0)` and the `#N/A` is gone,
   and the total reads **238.25**: no error, but **9.50** (nearly 4 percent) short, and nothing on the
   screen says so. `IFERROR` catches every error value, not only the one you expect. Microsoft's help
   says hiding errors is not recommended, because an error is often a sign that something needs to be
   fixed, not hidden.

**Google Sheets:** `IFERROR` and `IFNA` both exist in Sheets (`IFNA` catches only `#N/A`, which makes it
the safer one). Trace Precedents and Evaluate Formula are Excel tools, so in Sheets take the formula
apart yourself: put each piece in its own helper cell, and read the value of each piece.

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why did we trace the arrows back to B8 instead of fixing the formula in I2?</summary>

Because the formula in I2 was correct: it adds 40 amounts. It only showed the error it inherited. A fix
at the place where the error appears, such as hiding it, leaves the cause where it is and the number wrong.
The cause is upstream, in the first cell that shows an error although its own inputs are fine.

</details>

<Aside type="caution" title="Three more ways a copied formula fails">
A formula copied from a web page or a colleague can fail before any data is involved. Check these
three before you suspect the data:

| What you see | Likely cause | The check | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| A message "We found a problem with this formula" as soon as you press Enter | The separator between arguments. Excel expects the **list separator** of your computer's regional settings. Microsoft says the comma is the default for US English. | Retype the formula with the other separator, comma or semicolon. | Use the separator your Excel accepts, or change it in Windows Region settings or in **File**, **Options**, **Advanced**, **Editing options**, **Use system separators**. Same formula, other separator. |
| `#NAME?` on a formula copied from a page written for another language | A function name that your Excel does not know. Some languages of Excel use translated function names, such as `BUSCARV` for `VLOOKUP` in Spanish. | Read the function name: is it one you can find in an English list? | Retype it with the English name. |
| `0` or an empty result, and no error, on data that was pasted | A CSV that was never split into columns: each whole line sits in one cell. | Click a cell: if you see commas inside it, nothing was split. | Open the original file with **Data**, **From Text/CSV** and pick the delimiter, or split a copy of the pasted text into columns. |

The list separator also decides the delimiter Excel uses when it opens or saves a `.csv` file, so one
setting can cause both the first and the third problem on the same computer.
</Aside>

**Name the five errors.** The sheet `raw_error_zoo` has five cells in column B, each failing in a different

`calc_ex1` has the table with its first row done for you: **B2** shows `#N/A`. Its formula looks up code 99, which is not in the
products table: the key is not in the list. Fix: use a real code, or give the lookup a not-found result.

Now finish it for **B3**, **B4**, **B5** and **B6**.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Read each formula, not the message: what does it ask for, and what does the cell hand it? For B4 look at
the formula bar. What replaced one of the cell addresses?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

**B3** shows `#VALUE!`: the formula multiplies the cell C3 by 2, and C3 holds the text `n/a`, not a number.
Now do B4, B5 and B6 the same way: formula, input cells, cause.

</details>

<Solution>

| Cell | Error | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| B2 | `#N/A` | Code 99 is not in the products table. | Use a real code, or add a not-found result. |
| B3 | `#VALUE!` | `=C3*2` multiplies text (`n/a`) by a number. | Put a number in C3, or test first: `=IF(ISNUMBER(C3),C3*2,"no price")`. |
| B4 | `#REF!` | `=SUM(C4,#REF!,E4)`: the formula once used a cell in a column that has since been deleted, and its address was replaced by `#REF!`. | Undo the deletion right away (Ctrl+Z). If it is too late, rebuild the formula from the cells that are left, `=SUM(C4,E4)`, and prefer a range such as `=SUM(C4:E4)`, which shrinks when a column is deleted. |
| B5 | `#DIV/0!` | `=C5/D5` divides by D5, which is 0. | Decide what zero lines should show: `=IF(D5=0,"no lines",C5/D5)`. |
| B6 | `#NAME?` | `BUSCARV` is not an English Excel function name. | Retype it as `VLOOKUP` (or use `XLOOKUP`): `=VLOOKUP(2,raw_products!$A$2:$D$17,4,FALSE)` gives 3.5. |

Microsoft's help pages give the same causes: for `#REF!`, a deleted row or column, with Undo as the first
fix and a range such as `=SUM(B2:D2)` as protection; for `#NAME?`, mostly a typo in a function name.
Note that none of the fixes is `IFERROR`.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## On your own

<Exercise id="excel-formula-errors-and-auditing-2">

**Triage three failing formulas.** On `calc_ex2`, try each of these. Do not change the raw sheets.

Before you start, write one sentence on `calc_ex2` saying what you will hand back: for each of the three, the

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look at what each one produces: a message when you press Enter, an error value, or a number with no
error. They are three different causes from the table in *Three more ways a copied formula fails*. For the
third, click a cell of the pasted data.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output: *"Three lines, one per formula: its cause and its fix, and the number of Campus orders in the

</details>

<Solution>

1. **Separators.** In an Excel that expects commas, pressing Enter shows "We found a problem with this
   formula", because the semicolon is not its list separator (Microsoft's page: an error occurs when you
   use a symbol that is not the list separator of the Windows regional settings). Fix: retype with commas,
   `=XLOOKUP(5002,raw_model!A2:A41,raw_model!F2:F41)`, which returns **4.75**. If Excel accepted the
   semicolons, your Excel expects semicolons: same formula, and commas would be the ones to fail.
2. **A function name from another language.** `#NAME?`: Excel does not know `BUSCARV`. Fix: retype with the
   English name, `=VLOOKUP(5002,raw_model!A2:F41,6,FALSE)`, which returns **4.75**.
3. **A CSV that was never split.** Each pasted line, such as
   `1001,15,2,2026-01-01 08:02:00,completed,mobile`, sits whole in column A, so column C is empty and

   original file, **Data**, **From Text/CSV** and the comma as delimiter; with only the pasted lines, copy
   them to `calc_ex2!K1` and split them there, never on the raw sheet: Text to Columns in Excel, **Data**,

   `=COUNTIF(M2:M13,2)` gives **7**:
   orders 1001, 1003, 1005, 1008, 1010, 1011 and 1012. The other 5 are Old Town.

Only the third one gave no error, which makes it the most dangerous.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague opens `raw_model`, sees `#N/A`, and changes the price formula in all 40
rows to `=IFERROR(VLOOKUP(B2,raw_products!$A$2:$D$17,4,FALSE),0)` (their file is `calc_ex3`). "Fixed. The total is
238.25 and there are no errors anywhere." What went wrong?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The total no longer shows an error. How many of its 40 prices came back as 0 instead of a price?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Compare 238.25 with the right total from the worked example, and find out what the difference is made of.

<Solution>

The right total is **247.75**. The `#N/A` was a symptom of three product codes typed as text (rows 8, 19
and 31): the lookup could not find them. `IFERROR` replaced the three missing prices with 0, so the
total silently left out **9.50**, nearly 4 percent, and the average per line came out as 5.96 instead of
6.19. The message went away, and the problem stayed.

The honest fix is upstream: turn the three codes into numbers (`=VALUE(TRIM(...))`). If a not-found result
is really wanted, use a visible flag, such as the fourth argument of `XLOOKUP`, and count the flags.
`IFERROR` catches all error values, so use it only when you have already found and accepted the cause, and
prefer `IFNA`, which catches only `#N/A`.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

The pattern to remember: **read the error, walk back to the first bad cell, fix it there, then reconcile.**
The same habit works in SQL and in pandas, where a wrong number travels down a query or a pipeline the
same way.
