## Warm-up

Answer from memory before you open anything. Then click to check.

1. The quantity is in cell G2. Write a formula that shows "bulk" when it is 3 or more and "single"
   otherwise.

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `=IF(G2>=3,"bulk","single")`. The test comes first, then what to show when it is true, then what to
   show when it is false. Text goes in quotation marks.

   </details>

2. You wrote `=B1*C2` in D2 and filled it down. B1 holds a tax rate and must not move. What do you
   change?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Lock B1 with dollar signs: `=$B$1*C2`. Now the filled-down copies still point at B1, while C2 moves
   to C3, C4 and so on.

   </details>

3. After you filter a long list, which function totals only the rows you can still see?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `SUBTOTAL`, for example `=SUBTOTAL(109,J2:J3288)`. A plain `SUM` would add the hidden rows too.

   </details>

## The question

The owner of the cafes asks:

> How much coffee did Campus sell in March, and how many coffee lines is that? Count completed
> orders only. And what was the average line?

Here is the top of `raw_sales_lines`, the order list from the till. One row is one **product line**:

A **conditional total** looks at every row, keeps only the rows that pass **every** condition, and then
counts, adds or averages what is left:

- `COUNTIFS` counts the rows that pass.
- `SUMIFS` adds the cells of one column, for the rows that pass.
- `AVERAGEIFS` averages those cells.

Each condition is a **pair**: one column to look at and one test for it. A row counts only when it
passes all the pairs. The second picture shows the same idea from the other side: one bucket per
category, each with its own count and sum, and the buckets add up to the whole.

## Worked example

Question: **how many coffee lines did Campus sell in March (completed orders only), what do they add
up to, and what is the average line?** Open the practice file and follow along on the `calc` sheet.

1. **Say what one row is, and write the expected output.** Click cell **A1** and type: *"Three
   numbers for Campus, coffee, March, completed orders: how many lines, their total in dollars, the
   average line."* *Why:* one row is one product line, so we are counting lines, not orders. A
   formula with a typo still returns a number, so the sentence is what you check the number against.

2. **Type the conditions in cells.** In **A3:B7** type these pairs:

   | A | B |
   |---|---|
   | store | Campus |
   | category | coffee |
   | status | completed |
   | from (included) | 2026-03-01 |
   | until (not included) | 2026-04-01 |

   *Why:* to answer the same question for another store or month you change a cell, not a formula.
   Check that the two dates sit on the right side of the cell: that means they are real dates. Each
   order's `ordered_at` has a time of day, and "until April 1, not included" keeps all of March 31.

3. **Count the lines with COUNTIFS.** In **B9** type:

   ```text
   =COUNTIFS(raw_sales_lines!C:C,$B$3,raw_sales_lines!E:E,$B$4,raw_sales_lines!D:D,$B$5,raw_sales_lines!B:B,">="&$B$6,raw_sales_lines!B:B,"<"&$B$7)
   ```

   Read it in pairs: store column is `B3`; category column is `B4`; status column is `B5`; time
   column is at or after `B6`; time column is before `B7`. The result is **112**.
   *Why:* the `>=` and `<` sit inside quotation marks and are joined to the date cell with `&`. Whole
   columns such as `C:C` work because row 1 holds the headers, which never pass the tests. The dollar
   signs lock the condition cells, so you can copy the formula to another cell without the references
   sliding.

4. **Add the amounts with SUMIFS.** In **B10** type the same pairs, with the column to add in front:

   ```text
   =SUMIFS(raw_sales_lines!J:J,raw_sales_lines!C:C,$B$3,raw_sales_lines!E:E,$B$4,raw_sales_lines!D:D,$B$5,raw_sales_lines!B:B,">="&$B$6,raw_sales_lines!B:B,"<"&$B$7)
   ```

   The result is **706.25**. *Why the column to add comes first:* in `SUMIFS` the sum range is the
   first argument, then the pairs. Older workbooks use `SUMIF`, which takes one condition and puts the
   sum range last. Microsoft's help page warns about exactly this difference. For one condition:

   | Function | One condition, summing `line_amount_usd` for coffee |
   |---|---|
   | `SUMIF` (range, criteria, sum range) | `=SUMIF(raw_sales_lines!E:E,"coffee",raw_sales_lines!J:J)` |
   | `SUMIFS` (sum range, range, criteria) | `=SUMIFS(raw_sales_lines!J:J,raw_sales_lines!E:E,"coffee")` |

   Both return **10,807** for all coffee lines in the file. Only the position of the column to add
   changed. `AVERAGEIFS` follows the `SUMIFS` order: the column to average comes first.

5. **Average with AVERAGEIFS.** In **B11** use the same pairs, starting with `raw_sales_lines!J:J`
   again. The result is **6.3058**, which is **\$6.31** with two decimals.
   *Why not divide B10 by B9?* You can, and it must give the same number. `AVERAGEIFS` is one formula
   instead of two, and it cannot be broken by someone moving B9. If no row passes, Excel returns
   `#DIV/0!` (Microsoft's help page says so): an average of nothing has no value.

6. **Try three other kinds of criteria** on the whole list, in any empty cell:

   | Criteria | Formula | Result |
   |---|---|---:|
   | A comparison | `=COUNTIFS(raw_sales_lines!G:G,">=3")` lines with 3 or more units | 482 |
   | A comparison on money | `=COUNTIFS(raw_sales_lines!I:I,">0")` lines with a discount | 168 |
   | A wildcard | `=COUNTIFS(raw_sales_lines!F:F,"*Latte*")` product names that contain "Latte" | 635 |
   | Exact text | `=COUNTIFS(raw_sales_lines!F:F,"Latte")` product named exactly "Latte" | 420 |

   *Why:* `*` stands for any run of characters and `?` for exactly one, so `"*Latte*"` finds both
   Latte (420 lines) and Chai Latte (215 lines). To search for a real `*` or `?`, put `~` in front of it.
   - **Excel:** the wildcards are documented on the `SUMIFS` and `COUNTIFS` pages.
   - **Google Sheets:** Google documents the wildcards on its `SUMIF` page, not on `SUMIFS`. Use them
     in `SUMIFS` and `COUNTIFS` only after you have checked the result, as in the next two steps.

7. **Verify with buckets.** A conditional total is right only if the pieces add up to the whole. Copy
   the `SUMIFS` from step 4 into three more cells and, in each copy, type the category text instead of
   `$B$4`: `"tea"`, `"bakery"` and `"food"`. With coffee from step 4, the four sums are **706.25**,
   **185.75**, **260.00** and **288.75**, and they add up to **1,440.75**. Now remove the category pair
   and run it once more for the whole store: **1,440.75**.
   The buckets add up to the whole, so no category was lost or counted twice.

8. **Verify with a filtered list.** Make a copy of `raw_sales_lines` and work on the copy (**Excel:**
   right-click the tab, **Move or Copy**, **Create a copy**; **Google Sheets:** right-click the tab,
   **Duplicate**). Never filter the raw sheet. On the copy, filter `store_name` to Campus, `category`
   to coffee, `status` to completed and `ordered_at` to March 2026
   (**Excel:** **Data**, **Filter**; **Google Sheets:** **Data**, **Create a filter**). In a cell below the
   list, type `=SUBTOTAL(109,J2:J3288)`: it shows **706.25**, the same as step 4. `SUBTOTAL(103,A2:A3288)`
   counts the visible lines: **112**, the same as step 3. Both apps leave filtered-out rows out of
   `SUBTOTAL` whatever the function number; 109 also leaves out rows you hid by hand.
   *Why a second route:* `SUMIFS` never complains. If a condition does not match, you get a smaller
   number, or zero, and nothing on the screen tells you.

   Check the three results against your sentence in A1: **112 lines, \$706.25, \$6.31 average.**

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why did we run two checks in steps 7 and 8, when the formulas in steps 3 to 5 already gave numbers?</summary>

Because a conditional formula does not fail loudly. A wrong spelling in a condition, or a label that
is not exactly the same, makes some rows drop out and the total comes out smaller, but it is still a
perfectly normal-looking number. Only a second route, such as buckets that must add up or a filtered
list that must show the same total, can tell you it is wrong.

</details>

<Aside type="tip" title="One more look at the data">
The canceled order is a good example of why the `status` pair matters. Campus has one canceled coffee
line in March, a Mocha for \$5.00. Without the status pair, step 3 gives 113 and step 4 gives 711.25:
a number that looks fine and is \$5.00 too high.
</Aside>

<Solution>

1. **First try.** `COUNTIFS` for Harbor, bakery, completed gives **41** and `SUMIFS` gives **238.75**.
   No error, but both are wrong.
2. **Buckets check.** The four category sums for Harbor in June add up to **1,436.75**, while the same
   `SUMIFS` without the category pair gives **1,453.50**. A gap of **16.75** means some Harbor lines are in
   no bucket. Filter the category column on the raw sheet: next to `bakery` there is a second entry that
   looks the same: `bakery` followed by a stray space. It sits on 3 lines worth 16.75. (A wildcard
   `"bakery*"` would catch them, but fixing the label is cleaner.)
3. **Type check.** `=COUNT(raw_june_export!J2:J645)` gives **642**, but `=COUNTA(raw_june_export!J2:J645)`
   gives **644**. Two amounts are text (they sit on the left of their cells), 6.50 and 3.25. A text
   cell adds nothing to `SUMIFS`.
4. **Fix in `calc`.** In `calc!A2` type `=TRIM(raw_june_export!E2)` and in `calc!B2`
   `=VALUE(raw_june_export!J2)`, then fill both down to row 645. `TRIM` removes the stray space and
   `VALUE` turns text into a number (and leaves numbers alone). Rerun the formulas with
   `calc!A:A` as the category range and `calc!B:B` as the amount range, keeping the store, status and
   discount ranges on the raw sheet. All the ranges are whole columns, so they line up.
5. **Result:** **44 completed bakery lines**, **\$265.25**, average **\$6.03** (6.0284), **4** lines with a
   discount (`raw_june_export!I:I` with `">0"`).
6. **Check again.** The four categories now add up to **1,463.25**, which equals Harbor's June total
   (196 lines), and `COUNT` on `calc!B2:B645` gives **644**, equal to the number of lines.

The first try counted 3 lines too few and added 26.50 dollars too little (16.75 for the label,
9.75 for the two text amounts).

## Check yourself

1. Without looking back: in `SUMIFS`, which argument comes first, and how does `SUMIF` differ?

   <details>
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   In `SUMIFS` the range to add comes first, then one pair of range and criteria for each condition.
   `SUMIF` takes its single range and criteria first and the range to add last, and that last part is
   optional.

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague wants the completed Campus total for March and writes:

```text
=SUMIFS(raw_sales_lines!J:J,raw_sales_lines!C:C,"Campus",raw_sales_lines!D:D,"completed",raw_sales_lines!B:B,">="&DATE(2026,3,1),raw_sales_lines!B:B,"<="&DATE(2026,3,31))
```

It returns 1,376.00. "I checked the dates: the first of March to the thirty-first." What went wrong, and
how would you catch it?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look at the last pair of conditions. Which day does `DATE(2026,3,31)` stand for, and at what time of that day?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Every `ordered_at` has a time, for example 2026-03-31 08:15. Is that on or before 2026-03-31 at midnight? Then check how many lines the formula counts for March and compare with the filtered list.

</details>

<Solution>

The right answer is **1,440.75**. `ordered_at` is a date **and a time**. `DATE(2026,3,31)` means March 31
at midnight, so `"<="` throws out every order taken during the day on March 31: **11 lines worth
64.75** (1,440.75 − 64.75 = 1,376.00). The formula counts 207 lines instead of 218.

Fix: end the range before the next month starts, `"<"&DATE(2026,4,1)`, so the whole of March 31 is
inside. You catch it with a second route: the filtered list shows 218 completed Campus lines for March,
and a quick look at the last day shows March 31 lines that the formula never saw.

</Solution>
