The owner of the cafes asks:

> I need one chart for Monday's meeting: how have our monthly sales moved since January? And someone
> said May 1 at Old Town was a record day. Can we trust that?

Here are the monthly sales (completed orders), from a pivot you will build in a minute:

| month | sales_usd |
|---|---:|
| Jan | 3,109.75 |
| Feb | 2,670.25 |
| Mar | 2,995.25 |

Both charts draw the same six numbers. The first one lies without changing a single value: its axis
starts at 2,500, so the heights no longer match the values, and its title claims more than the data
does. The second one starts at 0, prints the values and says what happened.

Choose the chart from the **question**, not from the menu:

- **Which is bigger?** (compare categories) → a column or bar chart, sorted. Microsoft: column charts
  are "useful to show how data changes over time or to show comparisons among items".
- **How did it change over time?** → a line chart (Microsoft: ideal for "showing trends in data at
  equal intervals, like months, quarters, or fiscal years"), or columns when there are only a few periods.
- **Do two numbers move together?** → a scatter chart, which shows and compares numeric values
  without regard to time.
- **What is it made of?** → a pie chart only under Microsoft's conditions: one data series, no
  negative values, no more than seven categories. Even then, people read angles badly, and a bar chart
  is usually the clearer choice (From Data to Viz).

And three habits that keep a chart honest: a **title that states the takeaway**, a **value axis for
columns that starts at 0** (From Data to Viz says there is consensus on this for bar charts), and
**one measure per axis**.

## Worked example

Question: **how have monthly sales moved since January?** Open the practice file and follow along.

1. **Say the question and pick the chart.** The question is about change over months, so time goes
   on the horizontal axis. There are only six months, so columns work well. *Why:* the question
   chooses the chart. If you start from "which chart looks nice", you will draw what you can, not
   what you were asked.

2. **Build the pivot the chart will draw.** Copy `raw_order_lines` to `calc` (in Excel, make it a
   Table). Insert a pivot on `output`: `date` in Rows, `amount_usd` in Values (Sum), `status` in
   Filters set to **completed**. Group the dates into months (right-click a date, then **Group** and
   **Months** in Excel; **Create pivot date group** in Sheets, as in the previous lesson).
   *Why:* a chart is a picture of a table, and a wrong table gives a wrong chart. Reconcile first: the
   six months must add up to the grand total, **22,749.25**, which is the status-bar Sum of the source
   column (22,754.25) less the 5.00 canceled line.

3. **Insert the chart from the pivot.**
   - **Excel:** click a cell in the pivot, choose **Insert > PivotChart**, and pick **Clustered
     Column**. A PivotChart follows the pivot's fields, filters and slicers.
   - **Google Sheets:** select the pivot table's cells, choose **Insert > Chart**, and pick a
     **Column chart** from the **Chart type** list in the **Setup** tab.

   *Why from the pivot:* the chart then follows the pivot's filters and refresh. A chart drawn from
   numbers you typed beside it goes stale without telling anyone.

4. **Check the axis before anything else.** Look at the first label on the vertical axis.
   - **Excel:** "By default, Excel determines the minimum and maximum scale values of the vertical
     (value) axis", says Microsoft, so do not assume it starts at 0. To set it, select the vertical axis,
     choose **Format > Format Selection**, then **Axis Options**, and type **0** in the **Minimum**
     box.
   - **Google Sheets:** double-click the chart, open the **Customize** tab, then **Vertical axis**, and
     type **0** in **Min**.

   Then test the picture against the numbers: February's column should be about **0.60** of June's,
   because 2,670.25 ÷ 4,480.00 = 0.596. *Why:* the eye reads height as value. If the bars do not
   keep the ratio of the numbers, the chart is exaggerating, as the first picture does.

5. **Write the title.** Replace the default title (such as "Sum of amount_usd") with the finding:
   **Sales rose in April, when Harbor opened.** *Why:* the title is the one sentence a reader keeps,
   and a label tells them nothing. Check that the title is true: Harbor's `opened_on` date in the
   dataset is 2026-04-01. Filter `store` to leave out Harbor: Old Town and Campus together sold
   **2,794.25** in April, less than the **2,995.25** in March. So the rise really comes from the new store.
   Then clear the filter.

6. **Remove what carries no data.** Delete the legend (one series needs none), turn off 3-D and
   heavy gridlines, and add the values as data labels. *Why:* every extra element is something the
   reader has to ignore. The labels let a reader check the chart against your table.

That answers the owner's first question: **sales rose in April and stayed higher**, and the chart says
why in its title.

<details>
<summary>Explain it in your own words: why did step 4 come before step 5?</summary>

Because a good title on a distorted chart still misleads. The title tells people what to see; the axis
decides what they actually see. Check that the picture keeps the proportions of the numbers first,
then write the sentence that describes it.

</details>

<Aside type="tip" title="A pie chart is allowed, sometimes">
The four categories add up to the whole, so a pie chart would meet Microsoft's conditions: one series,
no negative values, seven categories or fewer. A sorted column chart still makes the sizes easier to
compare. A pie of the 15 products that sold would break the seven-category limit; try it in the last
exercise.
</Aside>

<Exercise id="excel-charts-that-tell-the-truth-3">

**Spot the flaw.** A colleague's slide has two charts. On the left, a **pie chart** of sales by product
(15 slices, titled "Sales by product"). On the right, **monthly sales as columns on the left axis,
starting at 2,500, with the number of orders as a line on a right axis**, titled "Orders are catching
up with sales". List what is wrong with each chart, and what you would draw instead.

<Solution>

**Left, the pie.** 15 products is more than the seven categories Microsoft allows for a pie, and
the slices are too close in size to compare: Latte brings in 2,927.00, Green Tea 783.75, and most of
the others fall between 800 and 2,000. The title describes the chart without saying anything. Use a
**bar chart sorted from largest to smallest**, with the title **"Latte is our best seller: 2,927.00 in
six months"**.

**Right, the two axes.** Three flaws:

- The sales axis starts at 2,500, so the column heights exaggerate (as in *The picture*).
- With **two vertical axes**, each scale is chosen separately, so where the line crosses the columns means
  nothing. Change the maximum of either axis and the crossing moves: "catching up" is a result of
  the scales. Sales are in dollars and orders in counts: they should not share a plot.
- The title states a story the chart cannot support.

Draw instead **two small charts, one measure each, both axes from 0**: monthly orders (242, 218, 257,
349, 380, 353) and monthly average order value (12.85, 12.25, 11.65, 12.58, 13.43, 12.69). Together they
say something real: *April's jump came mostly from more orders (257 in March, 349 in April); the average
order moved only from 11.65 to 12.58.*

</Solution>

</Exercise>
