## The question

The retention team can contact a few people each week and wants the ones most likely to churn. In this lesson churn is rare, because a win-back campaign would mostly find people who stay. *Which model setting is best, how much better than a guess is it, and what do class weights, a cut and copies of rows do to the answer?* The sample, drawn from the table of lesson DS3:

## The big idea

Tuning is a loop of fit and score over settings on the same folds: the best score is the highest of several noisy ones, so report it next to a baseline and score the test part once.

### 1. A search is a loop over settings

**Idea.** A *search* tries each setting of a grid, scores it by cross-validation on the same folds, and keeps the best. `GridSearchCV` is the loop: a setting for a step of a pipeline is named `step__parameter`, and the grid can swap the model itself. The score is *average precision* (AP), one number between 0 and 1 built from the precision and the recall at every cut, higher is better; it rewards ranking the churners high, and it suits a rare outcome. At the end the winner is refitted on all the training rows.

**Try it:** search 8 settings, 4 of logistic regression and 4 of boosted trees, and compare the best with the plain logistic regression.

```python
grid = [{"model": [LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)], "model__C": [0.01, 1.0], "model__class_weight": [None, "balanced"]},
        {"model": [HistGradientBoostingClassifier(early_stopping=False, random_state=SEED)],
         "model__learning_rate": [0.05, 0.2], "model__max_depth": [2, 4]}]
search = GridSearchCV(build(LogisticRegression()), grid, scoring="average_precision", cv=folds)
search.fit(X_train, y_train)                                  # 8 settings x 5 folds = 40 fits, then one refit of the winner on all the training rows
results = pd.DataFrame(search.cv_results_)
best = results.loc[search.best_index_]
plain = results[(results["param_model__C"] == 1.0) & results["param_model__class_weight"].isna()].iloc[0]
print(len(results), type(search.best_estimator_["model"]).__name__)
print(round(float(best["mean_test_score"]), 3), round(float(best["std_test_score"]), 3), round(float(plain["mean_test_score"]), 3))
```

**Predict:** The grid has 8 settings: 4 of logistic regression (`C` of 0.01 or 1, with or without class weights) and 4 of boosted trees. Each is scored by average precision on the same five folds. Will the best setting beat the plain logistic regression (`C` of 1, no weights) by more than the spread of the best setting's own five fold scores (their standard deviation)?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
8 LogisticRegression
0.219 0.046 0.216
```

</details>

*Why:* The best setting is a logistic regression with a `C` of 1 and balanced class weights (the next unit explains the weights): a mean average precision of 0.219 and a standard deviation of 0.046 over the five folds. The plain logistic regression scores 0.216: a lead of 0.003, about a fifteenth of the spread. The search always returns a winner, and here the winner is a tie with the default. The four boosted settings did not win, which agrees with the Data Scientist track: the columns, not the algorithm, set the ceiling.

### 2. A baseline, and a test part scored once

**Idea.** A score needs a yardstick. `DummyClassifier(strategy="prior")` gives every person the share of churners in the training rows. Report the tuned model next to it, and score the test part once, after the search is over: the search has already used the training rows for every choice.

**Try it:** score the flat guess and the tuned model on the test part.

```python
flat = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(X_train, y_train)
tuned = search.best_estimator_                                # already refitted on all the training rows
for label, model in (("flat guess", flat), ("tuned", tuned)):
    print(label, round(float(average_precision_score(y_test, model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1])), 3))
print(round(float(y_test.mean()), 3))
```

**Predict:** The flat guess gives every person the same chance. What average precision will `DummyClassifier(strategy="prior")` score on the test part, and about what will the tuned model score there?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
flat guess 0.102
tuned 0.214
0.102
```

</details>

*Why:* The flat guess scores 0.102, exactly the share of churners in the test part (the last line). The scikit-learn page says the same: with random predictions, the AP is the fraction of positive samples. The tuned model scores 0.214, twice the baseline. That is the claim the model can make: it ranks churners about twice as well as chance, and a score of 0.214 means little without the 0.102 beside it.

### 3. Class weights or a cut

**Idea.** A model flags a person when the chance is at least a *cut*, 0.5 unless you say otherwise. `class_weight="balanced"` makes each churner count more in the fit, in proportion to how rare churners are. *Recall* is the share of the churners that are flagged, *precision* the share of the flagged that churned. Moving the cut changes both without a new fit. The units choose on a validation part cut from the training rows, not on the test part.

**Try it:** fit the plain and the balanced model on 75 percent of the training rows, flag at 0.5, and then move the plain model's cut until its recall equals the balanced model's.

```python
X_fit, X_val, y_fit, y_val = train_test_split(X_train, y_train, test_size=0.25, random_state=SEED, stratify=y_train)
plain_lr = build(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(X_fit, y_fit)
balanced_lr = build(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, class_weight="balanced")).fit(X_fit, y_fit)
chances = {"plain": plain_lr.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1], "balanced": balanced_lr.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1]}
for label, p in chances.items():
    flagged = p >= 0.5
    print(label, round(float(recall_score(y_val, flagged)), 3), round(float(precision_score(y_val, flagged, zero_division=0)), 3))
target = recall_score(y_val, chances["balanced"] >= 0.5)       # the recall the balanced model has at 0.5
cut = max(t for t in np.unique(chances["plain"]) if recall_score(y_val, chances["plain"] >= t) >= target)
print(round(float(cut), 3), round(float(recall_score(y_val, chances["plain"] >= cut)), 3), round(float(precision_score(y_val, chances["plain"] >= cut)), 3))
```

**Predict:** A plain logistic regression and the same model with `class_weight="balanced"` are scored on a validation part cut from the training rows, at the usual cut of 0.5 (a person is flagged when the chance is at least 0.5). What share of the churners will each catch (recall)? And when the plain model's cut is moved until its recall equals the balanced model's, will its precision be clearly lower than, about the same as, or clearly higher than the balanced model's?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
plain 0.0 0.0
balanced 0.566 0.16
0.104 0.566 0.167
```

</details>

*Why:* Each line prints recall and precision. The plain model flags nobody at 0.5, so its recall and precision are both 0.0: no person has a chance of churning that high when 10 in 100 do. The balanced model catches 56.6 percent of the churners, with a precision of 0.16. The plain model with its cut moved to 0.104 catches the same 56.6 percent with a precision of 0.167: about the same. Weights and a moved cut are two routes to one trade, recall for precision; the cut is the one you can change after the fit, and the team's capacity decides where to stand.

### 4. Resample inside the folds

**Idea.** Resampling changes the rows a model is fitted on. *Oversampling* copies the churners, here 5 times each. The function `copies` below makes the copies. The two routes differ only in when it is called: before the five folds are cut, or on each fold's fitting rows after the cut.

**Try it:** score the plain logistic regression by average precision with copies made before the folds, and with copies made inside them.

```python
def copies(X_part, y_part, times=5):
    """Every churner `times` times, every person who stayed once."""
    extra = X_part[y_part == 1]
    return pd.concat([X_part] + [extra] * (times - 1)), np.concatenate([y_part] + [y_part[y_part == 1]] * (times - 1))


X_big, y_big = copies(X_train, y_train.to_numpy())              # copies made before the folds
before = cross_val_score(build(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)), X_big, y_big, cv=folds, scoring="average_precision")
inside = []
for fit_ix, score_ix in folds.split(X_train, y_train):         # copies made after the cut, in the fitting rows only
    X_f, y_f = copies(X_train.iloc[fit_ix], y_train.iloc[fit_ix].to_numpy())
    model = build(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(X_f, y_f)
    inside.append(average_precision_score(y_train.iloc[score_ix], model.predict_proba(X_train.iloc[score_ix])[:, 1]))
print(round(float(before.mean()), 3), round(float(np.mean(inside)), 3))
```

**Predict:** Each churner in the training rows is copied 5 times. In the first route the copies are made before the five folds are cut, so a person's copies can land in both a fitting part and a scoring part; in the second route each fold's fitting rows are copied after the cut and the scoring rows are left as they are. Will the second route score higher than, equal to or lower than the first on average precision?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.56 0.218
```

</details>

*Why:* Copies made first scored 0.56, and copies made inside the folds scored 0.218, no better than the 0.216 of the plain model in unit 1. With the copies made first, most scoring rows had a twin in the fitting part, so the model was scored on people it had already seen with their labels. Resampling can help a model that needs more weight on the rare class, but it did not improve the ranking here, and a score that jumps after resampling is a reason to look for the copies.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A search loops over settings on the same folds and refits the winner; its best score is the highest of several noisy ones.
- Report a tuned model next to the flat guess and the default setting; for a rare outcome the flat guess of average precision is the share of positives.
- Class weights and a moved cut both trade recall for precision; the cut can be changed after the fit.
- Copy or drop rows only in the fitting part of each fold.
- Score the test part once, after every choice is made.

**Common trap:** a score that jumps after balancing the classes. Look for copies of the same person on both sides of the split.

</Aside>
