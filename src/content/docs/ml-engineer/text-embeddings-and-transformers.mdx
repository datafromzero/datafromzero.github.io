## The question

A learner types a question into the help box of the site: *How do I keep rows that have no match?* The site has hundreds of paragraphs of lessons, and the words of the question are not the words of the answer. *How does a model turn text into numbers that compare by meaning, and is that better than counting words?* Three of the 220 paragraphs, with the lesson each comes from:

| lesson | one of its paragraphs |
|---|---|
| left-join-anti-join | A join pairs rows by key, and the join type decides what happens to a row that finds no partner. |
| null-handling | COALESCE(x, d) returns the first of its arguments that is not NULL, so it gives d when x is missing (the utility functions page). |
| window-functions | GROUP BY squeezes rows together. A window function does the same sums and counts but keeps every row: it writes the answer in a new column beside them. |

By the end you can say what a token is, compare two texts by cosine, say what a layer of attention does to a word's vector, and test a pretrained encoder against a baseline that counts words.

## The big idea

A model reads numbered pieces of text and gives each text a list of numbers, an embedding, where texts about the same thing end up pointing the same way.

### 1. Tokens, not words

**Idea.** A model cannot read letters. A *tokenizer* cuts text into *tokens*, pieces from a fixed list, and the model works with the number of each piece. Hugging Face's tokenizer summary says that common words stay whole as single tokens and that rare or unknown words decompose into subwords. This model uses WordPiece, the algorithm of the BERT family; in the output below, a piece that continues the word before it starts with `##`.

**Try it:** cut three sentences into tokens and print the words, the tokens and the pieces.

```python
tokenizer = model.tokenizer
for sentence in ["How many orders did store 2 take?", "COALESCE replaces a NULL with a default.", "date_trunc('month', ordered_at)"]:
    pieces = tokenizer.tokenize(sentence)
    print(len(sentence.split()), len(pieces), pieces)
```

**Predict:** How many tokens will the tokenizer make of each sentence, and which sentence gets the most tokens for its words?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
7 8 ['how', 'many', 'orders', 'did', 'store', '2', 'take', '?']
7 10 ['coal', '##es', '##ce', 'replaces', 'a', 'null', 'with', 'a', 'default', '.']
2 14 ['date', '_', 'tr', '##un', '##c', '(', "'", 'month', "'", ',', 'ordered', '_', 'at', ')']
```

</details>

*Why:* The columns are the words (split on spaces), the tokens, and the pieces. The question has 7 words and 8 tokens: the `?` is a token of its own, and the tokenizer wrote everything in lowercase. The sentence with `COALESCE` has 7 words and 10 tokens, because that rare word is cut into `coal`, `##es` and `##ce`. The line of code has 2 words and 14 tokens, 7 for each word, the most: underscores, brackets and quotes are tokens, and `date_trunc` and `ordered_at` break into pieces. What a model reads, and what its limits and prices count, is tokens, not words.

### 2. Embeddings and cosine similarity

**Idea.** An *embedding* is the list of numbers that a model gives a text; here there are 384. The model card says the model was trained on a dataset of above 1 billion sentence pairs with a contrastive objective: given one sentence of a pair, pick out the sentence it was paired with from a set of others. So texts that go together get vectors that point the same way. *Cosine similarity* measures how close two directions are: 1 for the same direction, 0 for a right angle, negative for opposite ones.

**Try it:** embed four sentences and print the cosine of the first with each of the others.

```python
sentences = ["How do I keep rows that have no match?", "Which join keeps unmatched rows?", "How do I keep my phone from losing its match history?", "What is the capital of Australia?"]
vectors = model.encode(sentences)
print(vectors.shape, [round(float(np.linalg.norm(v)), 3) for v in vectors])
print([round(float(score), 3) for score in vectors[0] @ vectors[1:].T])
```

**Predict:** Which of the other three sentences will be closest to the first, and will the one that shares the most words with it come first?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
(4, 384) [1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0]
[0.62, 0.342, 0.012]
```

</details>

*Why:* The first line holds the shape of the result, 4 vectors of 384 numbers, and their lengths, all 1.0 in this run (that comes from the code, not from the card: the last module of the model, which `print(model)` shows, scales every vector to length 1). The second line holds the cosines with the first sentence: 0.62 for the question in other words, 0.342 for the phone question that shares *how do I keep* and *match* with it, and 0.012 for the capital of Australia. The closest sentence shares one word, *rows*, with the first, and the one that shares most words is far less close. With vectors of length 1 the dot product `@` is the cosine; in general:

$$
\cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\text{length}(a) \times \text{length}(b)}
$$

### 3. What attention does to a word

**Idea.** A *transformer* is built from layers of *attention*. The paper that introduced it describes, in its section 3.2, an attention function as mapping a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output, a weighted sum of the values, where each weight comes from a compatibility function of the query with the matching key. In plain words, every token builds its next vector as a mix of the vectors of every token in the text, itself included, and takes more from the tokens that matter to it. The paper's transformer (its section 3) has two parts: an *encoder* that reads the whole input and gives a vector for each token, and a *decoder* that writes the output one element at a time, using what it has already written. The model here keeps only the reading side: it returns vectors and writes no text.

**Try it:** put the word `key` in two sentences at the same place, and compare its vectors at the input of the model and after each layer. Then print where `key` looks in the last layer.

```python
encoder = AutoModel.from_pretrained(MODEL_NAME, revision=REVISION, attn_implementation="eager").eval()    # the same network, set to report its attention
pair = ["A table needs a primary key.", "To run it press the key."]
batch = tokenizer(pair, return_tensors="pt", padding=True)
with torch.no_grad():
    result = encoder(**batch, output_hidden_states=True, output_attentions=True)
tokens = tokenizer.convert_ids_to_tokens(batch["input_ids"][0])
where = tokens.index("key")                                                   # the place of `key`: the same in both sentences
print(tokens, where, encoder.config.num_hidden_layers, encoder.config.num_attention_heads)
print([round(float(torch.cosine_similarity(layer[0, where], layer[1, where], dim=0)), 3) for layer in result.hidden_states])
looks = result.attentions[-1][0].mean(0)[where]                              # last layer, mean of the 12 heads: how much `key` looks at each token
print(round(float(looks.sum()), 3), [(t, round(float(w), 3)) for t, w in sorted(zip(tokens, looks.tolist()), key=lambda item: -item[1])[:3]])
```

**Predict:** How similar, by cosine, are the two vectors of `key` at the input and after each of the six layers? And which tokens does `key` look at most in the last layer?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
['[CLS]', 'a', 'table', 'needs', 'a', 'primary', 'key', '.', '[SEP]'] 6 6 12
[1.0, 0.925, 0.866, 0.842, 0.848, 0.824, 0.678]
1.0 [('[CLS]', 0.474), ('primary', 0.155), ('key', 0.105)]
```

</details>

*Why:* The first line shows the tokens, `key` at place 6, then 6 layers and 12 heads (a head is one attention that runs in parallel with the others). The second line has 7 cosines, the input and then the output of each layer: 1.0 at the input, because it is the same token at the same place; it ends at 0.678 after layer 6, with one small rise at layer 4. The two `key`s started as one vector and ended as two, because each took something from its own sentence. The third line is the attention of `key` in the last layer, the mean of the 12 heads: the weights add up to 1.0, and the largest are `[CLS]`, the marker the tokenizer puts first (0.474), `primary` (0.155) and `key` itself (0.105). That is one run on one sentence, not a rule about attention.

### 4. A baseline first, then a pretrained encoder

**Idea.** Before a pretrained model, fit the cheap baseline. *TF-IDF* gives each paragraph one number for each word or pair of words: a log-scaled count of the term in the paragraph (1 plus the log of the count, `sublinear_tf=True`) times a weight that is smaller the more paragraphs hold the term (scikit-learn: tf-idf(t, d) = tf(t, d) × idf(t)). The pretrained encoder is used as in lesson MLE6: each paragraph becomes 384 numbers once, and a logistic regression learns on top. Each head picks its `C` by 5-fold cross-validation on the training paragraphs only. The flat guess always names the most common lesson.

**Try it:** split the 220 paragraphs 75 to 25, fit both heads on the training paragraphs, and score them on the held-out paragraphs and on the 16 questions.

```python
train_text, test_text, train_lesson, test_lesson = train_test_split(texts, labels, test_size=0.25, random_state=SEED, stratify=labels)
questions, question_lesson = [q for q, _ in PROBE], [lesson for _, lesson in PROBE]
grid = {"C": [1, 10, 100]}                                                    # each head picks its C by 5-fold cross-validation on the training paragraphs

tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(sublinear_tf=True, ngram_range=(1, 2)).fit(train_text)
baseline = GridSearchCV(LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000), grid, cv=5).fit(tfidf.transform(train_text), train_lesson)
vectors_train, vectors_test, vectors_q = model.encode(train_text), model.encode(test_text), model.encode(questions)
encoder_head = GridSearchCV(LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000), grid, cv=5).fit(vectors_train, train_lesson)

flat = max(sorted(set(train_lesson)), key=train_lesson.count)       # ties go to the first name in alphabetical order
print(len(train_text), len(test_text), flat)
for kind, on_test, on_questions in [("flat guess", [flat] * len(test_text), [flat] * len(questions)),
                                    ("TF-IDF and logistic regression", baseline.predict(tfidf.transform(test_text)), baseline.predict(tfidf.transform(questions))),
                                    ("encoder and logistic regression", encoder_head.predict(vectors_test), encoder_head.predict(vectors_q))]:
    print(kind, round(float(np.mean(np.array(on_test) == np.array(test_lesson))), 3), int(np.sum(np.array(on_questions) == np.array(question_lesson))), "of", len(questions))
```

**Predict:** On the held-out paragraphs and on the 16 questions, which scores higher, the TF-IDF baseline or the encoder with a logistic regression?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
165 55 left-join-anti-join
flat guess 0.145 2 of 16
TF-IDF and logistic regression 0.691 15 of 16
encoder and logistic regression 0.691 14 of 16
```

</details>

*Why:* The first line is the training and held-out paragraphs (165 and 55) and the most common lesson of the training part, which the flat guess names. The flat guess is right for 0.145 of the paragraphs (8 of 55) and 2 of the 16 questions. TF-IDF and the encoder both get 0.691 of the paragraphs (38 of 55), and 15 and 14 of the 16 questions: a tie, one question apart. Over 20 other splits of the paragraphs (the optional cell) both heads have a median of 0.727 on the held-out paragraphs, 0.655 to 0.782 for TF-IDF and 0.655 to 0.818 for the encoder, and a median of 14 of 16 questions; the encoder was higher in 12 splits and lower in 7. So the headline run sits below both medians on the paragraphs (0.691 against 0.727), and on the questions TF-IDF's 15 is the top of its range of 13 to 15 while the encoder's 14 is its median. A tie means that the baseline, which needs no download and runs in a blink, is the one to keep until something beats it by more than the splits move. Unit 2 showed the encoder close a paraphrase that shares one word; unit 4 shows that, for naming the lesson of a paragraph, the words are enough. Whether embeddings win at finding the paragraph that answers a question is a test for the retrieval lesson.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A model reads tokens, not words: a rare word or a number is cut into several pieces, and a line of code costs many.
- An embedding is a list of numbers for a text; cosine similarity compares two of them, and with vectors of length 1 it is the dot product.
- Attention lets every token mix in the vectors of the others, so one word gets different vectors in different sentences.
- Fit the cheap baseline first; a pretrained encoder with a head is worth its download only if it beats the baseline on held-out text.
- Keep text the model never fitted (the 16 questions) for the last look, and read a one-question gap as a tie.

**Common trap:** a pretrained model is always better than counting words. On these 220 paragraphs it tied.

</Aside>
