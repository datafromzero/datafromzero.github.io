## The big idea

k-means cuts people into k groups around k centers, and it will cut any cloud, so the work is in checking what the cuts are worth.

### 1. Assign, move, and scale first

**Idea.** k-means picks k centers and repeats two steps: it assigns every person to the nearest center, then moves each center to the mean of its people (the two steps of the scikit-learn guide), until the centers stop moving. Distance adds up differences across columns. The setup cell's `Z` holds the counts after a log for the long tail, then each column with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. To compare two sets of groups use the *adjusted Rand index*: 1 for the same grouping and close to 0 for groups drawn at random.

**Try it:** cluster the people into four groups on the raw counts and on the scaled log counts, and compare the two groupings.

```python
raw = KMeans(4, n_init=10, random_state=SEED).fit(active[COUNTS])               # the counts as they are
scaled = KMeans(4, n_init=10, random_state=SEED).fit(Z)                         # the counts on a log scale, each with mean 0 and standard deviation 1
print(sorted(int(n) for n in np.bincount(raw.labels_)), sorted(int(n) for n in np.bincount(scaled.labels_)))
print(round(float(adjusted_rand_score(raw.labels_, scaled.labels_)), 3))
print(pd.DataFrame(raw.cluster_centers_, columns=COUNTS).round(1).to_string(index=False))
```

**Predict:** Will k-means on the raw counts find about the same four groups as on the scaled log counts (an adjusted Rand index near 1), partly the same (about 0.5) or unrelated (near 0)?

On the scaled log counts the groups hold 2,596, 2,728, 2,778, 5,183 people and every column has a say. Neither grouping is the truth; the scaled one asks about the pattern of a week.

### 2. Three rulers for k

**Idea.** Nothing in the data says what k is. *Inertia*, the sum of squared distances from each person to their center, falls every time k grows, so it cannot choose k alone (the guide: "lower values are better and zero is optimal"). The *silhouette* compares, for each person, the distance to their own group with the distance to the next nearest one: from -1 to 1, near +1 when the person is far from the neighboring groups. *Stability* is the median adjusted Rand index between fits on two random halves of the people (ten pairs, seeds 100 to 109). Read them against made-up data with three real groups.

**Try it:** print the rulers for k from 2 to 6, and the silhouette of made-up data with three real groups beside them.

```python
made_up, _ = make_blobs(len(Z), n_features=4, centers=3, cluster_std=1.0, random_state=SEED)       # made-up data with three real groups, as many rows as people
ruler_rows = []
for k in range(2, 7):
    km = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=SEED).fit(Z)
    km_made = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=SEED).fit(made_up)
    ruler_rows.append((k, round(float(km.inertia_)), round(float(silhouette_score(Z, km.labels_, sample_size=5000, random_state=SEED)), 3),
                 round(stability(Z, k), 3), round(float(silhouette_score(made_up, km_made.labels_, sample_size=5000, random_state=SEED)), 3)))
print(pd.DataFrame(ruler_rows, columns=["k", "inertia", "silhouette", "stability", "silhouette, made-up 3 groups"]).to_string(index=False))
```

**Predict:** On the made-up data the silhouette peaks sharply at k = 3. Will the people's silhouette peak as sharply at one value of k, or stay in a narrow band?

### 3. Describe a segment in words

**Idea.** Describe each segment by what its people do and how many they are, in words a teammate can use, and name it by something you can check, here how active it is. A table of sizes, means and the churn rate does it.

**Try it:** cluster the people into three segments, name them by activity, and print a profile of each, with the people who had no active day and a plain rule beside it.

```python
km3 = KMeans(3, n_init=10, random_state=SEED).fit(Z)
segments = active.assign(segment=km3.labels_)
order = segments.groupby("segment")["active_days_7d"].mean().sort_values().index                 # name the segments by activity, light to heavy
segments["segment"] = segments["segment"].map(dict(zip(order, ["light", "steady", "heavy"])))
profile = segments.groupby("segment").agg(people=("churned", "size"), sessions=("sessions_7d", "mean"), expenses=("expenses_7d", "mean"), active_days=("active_days_7d", "mean"),
                                           churned=("churned", "mean"), paid_social=("channel", lambda s: (s == "paid_social").mean())).loc[["light", "steady", "heavy"]].round(2)
print(profile.to_string())
quiet = table[table["active_days_7d"] == 0]
print("no active day:", len(quiet), "people, churned", round(float(quiet["churned"].mean()), 2))
print(active.groupby(active["active_days_7d"].clip(upper=3))["churned"].mean().round(2).to_dict())          # a plain rule: 1, 2 or 3 or more active days
```

**Predict:** How far apart will the churn rates of the light and heavy segments be: under 5 points, 5 to 15 points, or over 15 points?

*Why:* More than 15: the light segment (6,599 people) churns at 0.58 and the heavy one (2,120 people) at 0.31, 27 points apart. Paid social is 24 percent of the light segment and 2 percent of the heavy one.

### 4. What a cluster cannot prove

**Idea.** A clustering will cut any cloud into k pieces, so a clean result is no proof that groups exist. Stability is the check that k-means offers, so test it where the answer is known: on a random cloud with no groups by construction, with the same spread and correlations as the people.

**Try it:** print the stability of the people and of the cloud with no groups, for k from 2 to 4.

```python
cloud = np.random.default_rng(SEED).multivariate_normal(Z.mean(axis=0), np.cov(Z.T), size=len(Z))          # one cloud with no groups by construction, with the spread and correlations of the people
stab_rows = [(k, round(stability(Z, k), 3), round(stability(cloud, k), 3)) for k in (2, 3, 4)]
print(pd.DataFrame(stab_rows, columns=["k", "stability, people", "stability, cloud with no groups"]).to_string(index=False))
```

**Predict:** Will two fits on halves of a cloud with no groups at all agree (an adjusted Rand index above 0.9), agree partly or disagree?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
 k  stability, people  stability, cloud with no groups
 2              0.808                            0.964
 3              0.969                            0.933
 4              0.872                            0.810
```

</details>

*Why:* They agree: 0.933 at k = 3 and 0.964 at k = 2, and the people's 0.969 at k = 3 is not clearly more. Stability says that the cuts can be repeated, not that groups exist: halves of a smooth cloud get cut at nearly the same places. The dataset's rules say the same about the people: each person's activity is one log-normal draw times a factor for the channel and 1.10 for iOS, with no hidden groups of heavy or light users. The three segments are cut points on a continuum, and the paid-social share of 24 percent against 2 percent is the channel factor moving everyone's level. A cluster cannot prove that groups exist, why people differ, or that a message will work: nobody was given a message.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- k-means assigns each person to the nearest center, then moves each center to the mean of its people; log long-tailed counts and scale them first.
- Inertia always falls as k grows; silhouette and stability are rulers, and you read them against made-up data that has real groups.
- When the rulers are flat, k is a choice of use: say how many messages the team can write.
- Describe a segment by its size and behavior, and name it by something checkable.
- A stable clustering is repeatable, not real.

**Common trap:** taking a high silhouette or a repeatable clustering as proof that the groups are real.

</Aside>
