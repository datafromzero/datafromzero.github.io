### 1. Size a test on revenue

**Idea.** For a mean, the people needed in each arm grow with the *square* of the spread over the lift you want to catch: 2 times (spread times (z for the bar plus z for the power) divided by the lift) squared. The spread comes from a pilot, here the control arm of the last test. The formula assumes both arms have the pilot's spread; a simulation does not need to. Draw people from the pilot, scale the variant up to the lift, and count how often the test finds p below 0.05.

**Try it:** the pilot's mean and spread, the people the formula asks for to catch +$0.50 a person at power 0.80, and the simulated power at that size and at 2,700 people per arm.

```python
pilot = wt.loc[wt["variant"] == "control", "revenue_14d"].to_numpy()      # the control arm of the last test: a pilot for the next one
print(len(pilot), round(float(pilot.mean()), 3), round(float(pilot.std(ddof=1)), 3), round(float((pilot > 0).mean()), 3))


def n_per_arm(sd, delta, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """People needed in each arm to catch a true difference of `delta` in a mean whose spread is `sd`."""
    z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2) + stats.norm.ppf(power)
    return int(np.ceil(2 * (sd * z / delta) ** 2))


rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)


def power_at(n, delta, runs=2000):
    """Share of simulated tests with p < 0.05: draw n people per arm from the pilot; the variant's revenue is scaled up so its mean is `delta` higher."""
    scale = 1 + delta / pilot.mean()
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(runs):
        a, b = rng.choice(pilot, n), rng.choice(pilot, n) * scale
        hits += stats.ttest_ind(b, a, equal_var=False).pvalue < 0.05
    return hits / runs


n = n_per_arm(pilot.std(ddof=1), 0.50)
print(n, round(power_at(n, 0.50), 2), round(power_at(2700, 0.50), 2))        # the planned size, its simulated power, and the power of 2,700 per arm
```

**Predict:** Revenue per person in 14 days is $0 for most people, $7.99 or $59.99 for a buyer. To catch a lift of $0.50 a person, about how many people does each arm need: about 700, about 7,000 or about 70,000?

And 2,700 per arm, about the size of `paywall_v2`, catches +$0.50 in 0.34 of the tests. A formula is a start; when the metric has a heavy tail, simulate.

### 2. A bootstrap interval for any statistic

**Idea.** The *bootstrap* resamples the people of each arm with replacement, recomputes the statistic on every resample, and reads the interval from the middle 95 percent of the results. It works for any statistic you can compute: a mean, a ratio, a median. Resample the unit that the coin was tossed for, the person. SciPy has a ready-made `bootstrap`, whose default is the BCa method; this loop uses the plain percentile interval. The statistic must match the decision.

**Try it:** four statistics for `paywall_v2`, each with its interval, and the textbook interval for the mean beside them.

```python
ctrl, var = pw[pw["variant"] == "control"], pw[pw["variant"] == "variant"]
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)


def bootstrap(stat, variant, control, reps=2000):
    """95% percentile interval of stat(variant rows, control rows): resample the people of each arm, then recompute the statistic."""
    draws = [stat(variant.iloc[rng.integers(0, len(variant), len(variant))], control.iloc[rng.integers(0, len(control), len(control))]) for _ in range(reps)]
    return np.percentile(draws, [2.5, 97.5])


stats_ = {
    "mean revenue, difference": lambda v, c: v["revenue_14d"].mean() - c["revenue_14d"].mean(),
    "conversion, relative lift": lambda v, c: v["converted"].mean() / c["converted"].mean() - 1,
    "median revenue, difference": lambda v, c: v["revenue_14d"].median() - c["revenue_14d"].median(),
    "median hours to buy, difference": lambda v, c: v["hours_to_buy"].median() - c["hours_to_buy"].median(),
}
print(round(float(ctrl["revenue_14d"].median()), 2), round(float(var["revenue_14d"].median()), 2))
for name, stat in stats_.items():
    low, high = bootstrap(stat, var, ctrl)
    print(f"{name}: {stat(var, ctrl):.3f} ({low:.3f} to {high:.3f})")
t = stats.ttest_ind(var["revenue_14d"], ctrl["revenue_14d"], equal_var=False).confidence_interval()
print(round(float(t.low), 3), round(float(t.high), 3))                          # the textbook interval for the mean difference
```

**Predict:** What is the median revenue per person in each arm of `paywall_v2`: about $2.6, the average, $7.99, or $0?

*Why:* $0 in both: 87.3 percent of the control and 84.7 percent of the variant pay nothing, so the median sits on the zeros and ignores the lift; its interval is 0 to 0. The mean difference is +$0.627 per person (0.054 to 1.189), close to the textbook interval (0.042 to 1.212). The relative lift in conversion is +20.7 percent (5.1 to 38.6 percent), a ratio no simple formula gives an interval for. The median hours from assignment to purchase, among buyers, moved by 0.4 hours with an interval from -1.3 to 6.7: the new paywall changes whether people buy, not when. Pick the statistic from the decision, then bootstrap it.

### 3. Checks that need no outcome

**Idea.** Some checks look only at who is in the arms, before any outcome is read. The split against 50/50 is one. Another is *balance*: a coin cannot know a person's channel, platform or region, so each signup column should be spread evenly across the arms (a chi-square test of the column against the arm). Microsoft Research advises analyzing segments to see whether a mismatch is localized to some of them. An *A/A test* then checks the readout itself: split one arm at random, run the analysis, and count how often it finds a difference that cannot be there.

**Try it:** the checks for the three experiments, then 1,000 A/A splits of the control arm of `paywall_v2`.

```python
def two_prop_p(k0, n0, k1, n1):
    """Two-sided p of a z test for two shares, with the pooled standard error (as in lesson C14)."""
    p = (k0 + k1) / (n0 + n1)
    z = (k1 / n1 - k0 / n0) / np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) * (1 / n0 + 1 / n1))
    return 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))


def sanity(g):
    """Checks that need no outcome: the split against 50/50, and each signup column against the arm."""
    n = g["variant"].value_counts()
    row = {"control": int(n["control"]), "variant": int(n["variant"]), "ratio": stats.chisquare([n["control"], n["variant"]]).pvalue}
    for col in ["channel", "platform", "region"]:
        row[col] = stats.chi2_contingency(pd.crosstab(g[col], g["variant"]))[1]
    return row


checks = pd.DataFrame({name: sanity(g) for name, g in exp.groupby("experiment")}).T
shown = checks.assign(**{c: checks[c].map(lambda v: f"{v:.4f}") for c in ["ratio", "channel", "platform", "region"]}, control=checks["control"].astype(int), variant=checks["variant"].astype(int))
print(shown.to_string())
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
c = pw.loc[pw["variant"] == "control", "converted"].to_numpy()                   # 2,706 people who all got the same paywall
hits = 0
for _ in range(1000):                                                            # an A/A test: split them at random and run the readout
    i = rng.permutation(len(c))
    a, b = c[i[: len(c) // 2]], c[i[len(c) // 2:]]
    hits += two_prop_p(a.sum(), len(a), b.sum(), len(b)) < 0.05
print(round(hits / 1000, 2))
```

**Predict:** Which experiment fails which check? And in 1,000 random A/A splits of one arm, will about 1, about 5 or about 20 percent come out with p below 0.05?

welcome_tips       2449     2519  0.3206  0.6965   0.5058  0.8963

*Why:* Only `signup_flow_v2` fails: its split (2,878 and 2,603, p = 0.0002) and its platform column (p below 0.00005); channel and region pass, so the problem is localized to platform before any outcome is read, as C14 later traced it to Android. The other two experiments pass every check (every p is 0.32 or more). The A/A share is 0.05, as a bar of 0.05 promises, so the readout does not invent differences (1,000 splits, so the second decimal can move). A share far above 0.05 would mean that the code, the data or the randomization is broken.

### 4. Reading a causal claim

**Idea.** A claim that something *causes* a change compares two groups, so ask four questions. How did people get into the groups? What else differs between them? Does the gap survive when that is held fixed? How big and how sure is it? A coin balances every column in expectation, measured or not. Holding a column fixed balances only that column.

**Try it:** the claim "people who finished onboarding churn less", from the churn table of lesson DS3: the raw gap, the gap inside each channel averaged with the channel sizes and an interval for it, and the paid-social share in each group.

```python
by_channel = table.groupby(["channel", "onboarded"])["churned"].mean().unstack()
raw = table.groupby("onboarded")["churned"].mean()
weights = table["channel"].value_counts(normalize=True).reindex(by_channel.index)
adjusted = float(((by_channel[1] - by_channel[0]) * weights).sum())               # the gap inside each channel, averaged with the channel sizes
print(round(float(raw[1] - raw[0]), 3), round(adjusted, 3))
code_, o, y = table["channel"].astype("category").cat.codes.to_numpy(), table["onboarded"].to_numpy(), table["churned"].to_numpy()
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
draws = []
for _ in range(2000):                                                            # resample the people, recompute the adjusted gap
    i = rng.integers(0, len(y), len(y))
    cell = code_[i] * 2 + o[i]
    rate = np.bincount(cell, weights=y[i], minlength=10) / np.bincount(cell, minlength=10)
    draws.append(((rate[1::2] - rate[0::2]) * np.bincount(code_[i], minlength=5) / len(i)).sum())
print(np.round(np.percentile(draws, [2.5, 97.5]), 3))
share = pd.crosstab(table["onboarded"], table["channel"], normalize="index")["paid_social"]
arms = pd.crosstab(pw["variant"], pw["channel"], normalize="index")["paid_social"]
print(share.round(3).to_dict(), arms.round(3).to_dict())                          # the paid-social share, by onboarding and by experiment arm
```

**Predict:** People who finished onboarding churned 10.8 points less. Inside each channel, will the gap be about the same, about half, or about zero?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
-0.108 -0.011
[-0.028  0.005]
{0: 0.404, 1: 0.139} {'control': 0.101, 'variant': 0.102}
```

</details>

*Why:* About zero: -0.011, with an interval of -0.028 to 0.005 (2,000 resamples, seed 2026). Paid social is 40.4 percent of the people who did not finish onboarding and 13.9 percent of those who did, and paid-social people churn most, so the raw gap is mostly the channel. The dataset's rules make channel decide both how likely a person is to finish onboarding and how active they are, and give onboarding no effect on activity. Where people are put in groups by a coin, as in `paywall_v2`, the paid-social share is 0.101 in the control and 0.102 in the variant. The adjustment worked here because the channel was measured; it would not have balanced a column nobody recorded.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- For a mean, people per arm grow with spread squared over lift squared; simulate when the tail is heavy.
- Bootstrap the person, and pick the statistic from the decision: a median of mostly zeros hides the lift.
- Check the split, the balance and an A/A test before reading an outcome.
- Ask how people got into the groups, what else differs, whether the gap survives, and how sure it is.
- A coin balances every column; holding a column fixed balances only that one.

**Common trap:** a segment check at a bar of 0.05, with a few segments. One passing test in twenty fails by chance, so read the one that fails by a lot.

</Aside>

## Check yourself

1. Without looking back: why does a bootstrap resample people and not the rows of their events?

   <details>
   <summary>answer</summary>

   The coin was tossed for the person, so the person is the unit that varies between repeats of the test; a person's events belong together.
