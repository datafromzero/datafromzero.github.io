### 1. Shuffle a column and see what the model loses

**Idea.** *Permutation importance* shuffles the values of one column among the test people and measures how much the score drops. A column the model leans on costs a lot; a column it ignores costs nothing. It needs only the fitted model and the test people, so it works on a logistic regression and on a tree alike. The function below shuffles one column or a block of columns together.

**Try it:** the loss in ROC AUC for each column, then the four activity columns added up one by one and shuffled together.

```python
def group_loss(m, cols, repeats=30):
    """Mean drop in ROC AUC on the test people when the rows of `cols` are shuffled together, as one block."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
    base = roc_auc_score(yt, m.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1])
    drops = []
    for _ in range(repeats):
        shuffled = X_test.copy()
        shuffled[cols] = X_test[cols].iloc[rng.permutation(len(X_test))].to_numpy()
        drops.append(base - roc_auc_score(yt, m.predict_proba(shuffled)[:, 1]))
    return float(np.mean(drops))


one_by_one = permutation_importance(model, X_test, y_test, scoring="roc_auc", n_repeats=30, random_state=SEED)
loss = pd.Series(one_by_one.importances_mean, index=X_test.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)
print(loss.round(4).to_string())
print(round(float(loss[ACTIVITY].sum()), 4), round(group_loss(model, ACTIVITY), 4))       # the four activity columns: one by one added up, and shuffled together
```

**Predict:** `sessions_7d`, `expenses_7d`, `recent_3d` and `active_days_7d` all count how active a person was in week 1. Shuffled one at a time, the biggest loss is under 0.03. Shuffled together, will the loss be about the same as the biggest one, about the sum of the four, or more than the sum?

### 2. What the model says when one column moves

**Idea.** *Partial dependence* sets one column to a value for every test person, keeps the other columns as they are, and averages the model's chance. Repeat for a few values and you have a curve. It answers "what would the model say if everybody had this value?", and it assumes the column can move on its own: scikit-learn's page says this assumption is often violated.

**Try it:** the curve for `expenses_7d` by hand, checked against scikit-learn's own `partial_dependence`, beside the share who really churned.

```python
values = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6]


def average_chance(col, values):
    """Partial dependence by hand: set the column to each value for every test person, and average the model's chance."""
    out = []
    for v in values:
        shifted = X_test.copy()
        shifted[col] = v
        out.append(float(model.predict_proba(shifted)[:, 1].mean()))
    return out


by_hand = average_chance("expenses_7d", values)
sk = partial_dependence(model, X_test.astype({"expenses_7d": float}), ["expenses_7d"], kind="average", grid_resolution=50)       # scikit-learn picks its own grid of values
agrees = float(np.abs(np.array(average_chance("expenses_7d", sk["grid_values"][0])) - sk["average"][0]).max()) < 1e-9
print(np.round(by_hand, 3), agrees)
print(np.round([float(y_test[X_test["expenses_7d"] == v].mean()) for v in values], 3))      # the share who really churned among the test people with that many expenses
print([int((X_test["expenses_7d"] == v).sum()) for v in values])
```

**Predict:** At 0 expenses in week 1 the model's average chance of churning is about 0.60. Set every test person to 6 expenses and leave the other columns as they are: will the average be about 0.55, about 0.42 or about 0.20? Then compare it with the share who really churned among the people who have 6 expenses.

<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[1421, 1205, 748, 386, 267, 82]
```

*Why:* About 0.42: the curve falls by about 0.03 for each expense. The 82 test people who really have 6 expenses churned at 0.293, lower than the curve, because they differ in more than expenses, such as the channel they came from. The curve moves one column and holds the rest; the raw share moves everything that goes with it. Neither is a cause, and the curve also visits people nobody has seen, such as 6 expenses and no visit.

### 3. Where it fails: the same model, group by group

**Idea.** A score for everyone hides the groups. For each group, ask three separate questions: are the chances right (mean chance against the share who churned), does the model rank well (ROC AUC), and who gets the offer (the share offered, and the *recall*, the share of the leavers offered). Give a gap an interval: resample the test people and recompute it. Hardt, Price and Srebro's *equal opportunity* asks that people who have the outcome are offered at equal rates in every group.

**Try it:** the table by platform at the DS4 threshold, then the gap in recall between the app people and the web people, with 2,000 resamples.

```python
THRESHOLD = 2 / 3                    # lesson DS4's rule: an offer costs $2 and a missed leaver $1, so offer when the chance is at least 2 / (2 + 1)
flag = p_test >= THRESHOLD


def slice_table(col, threshold=THRESHOLD):
    """For each level of `col`: people, share who churned, mean chance, ROC AUC, share offered, share of the leavers offered (recall)."""
    flagged = p_test >= threshold
    rows = {}
    for level in sorted(X_test[col].unique()):
        m = (X_test[col] == level).to_numpy()
        rows[level] = {"people": int(m.sum()), "churned": round(float(yt[m].mean()), 3), "mean chance": round(float(p_test[m].mean()), 3),
                       "ROC AUC": round(float(roc_auc_score(yt[m], p_test[m])), 3), "offered": round(float(flagged[m].mean()), 3),
                       "recall": round(float(flagged[m & (yt == 1)].mean()), 3)}
    return pd.DataFrame(rows).T


print(slice_table("platform").to_string())
web = (X_test["platform"] == "web").to_numpy()
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
gaps = []
for _ in range(2000):                              # resample the test people; recall of the app people minus recall of the web people
    i = rng.integers(0, len(yt), len(yt))
    f, yy, w = flag[i], yt[i], web[i]
    gaps.append(f[~w & (yy == 1)].mean() - f[w & (yy == 1)].mean())
print(round(float(flag[~web & (yt == 1)].mean() - flag[web & (yt == 1)].mean()), 3), np.round(np.percentile(gaps, [2.5, 97.5]), 3))
```

**Predict:** The rule offers the credit to 22.4 percent of all test people. Among the web people, will the share offered be about the same as among the iOS people, about half, or about a tenth?

*Why:* About a tenth: 0.028 of the web people against 0.313 of the iOS people. Of the 634 web leavers 30 are offered (recall 0.047), of the 1,790 app leavers 754 (0.421): a gap of 0.374, interval 0.345 to 0.403 (seed 2026; its last digit can move on another NumPy). The chances are right in every platform (mean chance within about 0.02 of the share who churned), so this is not a calibration failure. It is the rule: almost only paid-social people pass 2 / 3, and 98 percent of them are offered. Only 39 of the 1,387 web people come from paid social, against about a third of the app people. Platform cost the model almost nothing in unit 1 (0.0023), yet the offers split by platform: a model can treat groups differently without looking at them.

*The risk to name:* **unequal opportunity**, the credit reaching 4.7 percent of the web leavers and 42.1 percent of the app leavers. Chouldechova shows that several fairness criteria cannot all hold when the outcome's rate differs across groups (here 0.457 churn on web, 0.615 on Android), so closing the gap costs something else, such as more wasted offers. The owner of the offer decides which cost to accept; the card records it.

### 4. Write the card and run the checklist

**Idea.** A *model card* is a short document that travels with the model. Mitchell and colleagues give it nine sections: model details, intended use, factors, metrics, evaluation data, training data, quantitative analysis, ethical considerations, and caveats and recommendations. Build it from the run, so a number in the card is a number the notebook computed. A *release checklist* then asks one question for each duty: is there evidence on the card? An item with no evidence stays open, and one open item stops the release.

**Try it:** write the card, with an interval for the test AUC, then run the checklist against it.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
lo, hi = np.percentile([roc_auc_score(yt[i], p_test[i]) for i in (rng.integers(0, len(yt), len(yt)) for _ in range(2000))], [2.5, 97.5])      # 2,000 resamples of the test people
by_platform = slice_table("platform")
web_row, app_row = by_platform.loc["web"], by_platform.loc[["android", "ios"]]
card = {
    "Model details": "Logistic regression on week-1 columns; 13,362 training people; scikit-learn, seed 2026.",
    "Intended use": "Rank people by their chance of leaving in days 8 to 30, to pick who gets a win-back offer.",
    "Factors": "Channel, platform, region. The data has no protected attribute; a real card names the groups affected.",
    "Metrics": f"ROC AUC {roc_auc_score(yt, p_test):.3f} (95% interval {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}); offer when the chance is at least {THRESHOLD:.3f}.",
```

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Shuffle a column and read the drop in score on held-out people; shuffle correlated columns together.
- A zero loss means unused by this model on these people, not unimportant in the world.
- Partial dependence moves one column for everybody; it assumes the column can move alone, and it is no cause.
- Check chances, ranking and who is offered for each group, and put an interval on the gap.
- A card is built from the run; a checklist item with no evidence stays open.

**Common trap:** removing the column and calling the model fair. The offers still split by platform without the column, because other columns carry it.
