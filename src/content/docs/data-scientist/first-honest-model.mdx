### 1. Two rungs below the model

**Idea.** A *baseline* is a rule the model has to beat, and one rung is not enough. Rung 1 is the flat guess: every person gets the share of churners in the training part. Rung 2 is the best rule you could have built before day 7: a model that knows only the three columns known at signup. A model is judged against the highest rung below it.

**Try it:** split the people once, with the seed of the track, then fit both rungs on the training part and score them on the held-out part. Accuracy is the share of people called right when a chance of 0.5 or more means "churns". ROC AUC scores the ranking: 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 is a perfect ranking.

```python
STATIC = ["channel", "platform", "region"]                    # known at signup
WEEK1 = ["sessions_7d", "expenses_7d", "recent_3d", "active_days_7d", "onboarded", "saw_paywall", "bought_plus"]    # known at day 7
X, y = table[STATIC + WEEK1], table["churned"]
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=SEED, stratify=y)
print(len(X_train), len(X_test), round(float(y_train.mean()), 4))

rung1 = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(X_train, y_train)     # the flat guess: everyone gets the training share of churners
rung2 = make_pipeline(ColumnTransformer([("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), STATIC)]), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
rung2.fit(X_train, y_train)                                         # the day-0 rule: the three signup columns only
for name, m in [("rung 1", rung1), ("rung 2", rung2)]:
    print(name, round(accuracy_score(y_test, m.predict(X_test)), 3), round(roc_auc_score(y_test, m.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]), 3))
```

**Predict:** Rung 1 gives everyone the same chance, 0.544. Rung 2 learns from channel, platform and region. How many more people out of 100 does rung 2 call right than rung 1: about 1, about 7 or about 20?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
13362 4454 0.5442
rung 1 0.544 0.5
rung 2 0.611 0.668
```

</details>

*Why:* Both rungs learned from 13,362 people and were scored on 4,454 others; the stratified split keeps the share of churners at 54.4 percent in both parts. The flat guess calls 54.4 of 100 right, simply the share of churners, and its ROC AUC is 0.5 because everyone gets the same chance. Rung 2 calls 61.1 of 100 right, about 7 more, and ranks with an AUC of 0.668. Signup columns alone already beat the flat guess, so that is the number the next model has to beat.

### 2. One object, three verbs

**Idea.** A scikit-learn model is an object with verbs. `fit` learns from the training part; `predict_proba` gives a chance for each person; `predict` gives a 0 or a 1 for each; `score` gives one number. Put the preparation steps and the model in one `Pipeline`, so that every verb runs every step in order and a step that learns, the encoder or the scaler, learns from the training part only.

**Try it:** build the day-7 model with the week-1 columns added, and look at what each verb returns for the held-out people.

```python
model = make_pipeline(
    ColumnTransformer([("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), STATIC),      # text columns: one 0/1 column per value
                       ("num", StandardScaler(), WEEK1)]),                           # number columns: centered and scaled
    LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000),
)
model.fit(X_train, y_train)                       # learns from the training part only
chance = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]        # a chance of churning for every test person
call = model.predict(X_test)                      # a 0 or a 1 for every test person
print(np.round(chance[:3], 3), call[:3])
print(round(model.score(X_test, y_test), 4), round(accuracy_score(y_test, call), 4), round(roc_auc_score(y_test, chance), 4))
print(bool(((chance > 0.5).astype(int) == call).all()))
```

**Predict:** `model.score(X_test, y_test)` is one number. Which is it: the accuracy, the ROC AUC or the share of churners? And is `predict` the same as asking whether the chance is above 0.5?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
[0.322 0.359 0.381] [0 0 0]
0.6322 0.6322 0.6898
True
```

</details>

*Why:* The first three held-out people get chances of 0.32, 0.36 and 0.38, all under 0.5, so `predict` calls each of them a 0, someone who stays. `score` is the accuracy, 0.6322: the same number as `accuracy_score` on the labels, as the documentation of the class says ("Return accuracy on provided data and labels"). The ROC AUC of 0.6898 needs the chances, not the 0 and 1 calls, because it scores the ranking. On every held-out person `predict` agreed with "chance above 0.5", so that is the cut it uses. It is a default and not a decision: lesson DS4 chooses the cut from what each kind of mistake costs.

### 3. An interval on the gain

**Idea.** A score from one split is one draw. To see how far it could move, resample the held-out people with replacement, score both models on the same draw, and repeat 2,000 times: a bootstrap, as in [*Confidence intervals*](/core/confidence-intervals/) (C7). The middle 95 percent of the 2,000 gains is the interval. Use the same resample for both models, so that each gain is measured on the same people.

**Try it:** bootstrap the gain in ROC AUC of the day-7 model over rung 2.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
y_arr = y_test.to_numpy()
p_day0 = rung2.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
gain = []
for _ in range(2000):
    i = rng.integers(0, len(y_arr), len(y_arr))                # one resample of the test people, used for both models
    gain.append(roc_auc_score(y_arr[i], chance[i]) - roc_auc_score(y_arr[i], p_day0[i]))
print(round(roc_auc_score(y_arr, chance) - roc_auc_score(y_arr, p_day0), 3))
print(np.round(np.percentile(gain, [2.5, 97.5]), 3))
```

**Predict:** The two models were scored on the same 4,454 people. Will the 95 percent interval of the gain in ROC AUC include 0, and about how wide will it be?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.022
[0.012 0.033]
```

</details>

*Why:* The gain is 0.022 and its 95 percent interval runs from 0.012 to 0.033: it does not include 0, and it is about 0.010 either side. On these people the week-1 columns add to what the signup columns knew. The interval is for this table and these 4,454 held-out people; it does not promise the same gain in June. It comes from the seed 2026: with seed 1 it is 0.012 to 0.033, so the ends move by a thousandth or so.

### 4. Which columns carry the gain

**Idea.** Refit the same recipe with a group of columns removed and score it on the same held-out people. A group whose removal costs nothing adds nothing to the verdict, and every column is one more thing to define, collect and check.

**Try it:** refit without the week-1 columns, without the signup columns, and without the three flags (`onboarded`, `saw_paywall`, `bought_plus`).

```python
COUNTS, FLAGS = WEEK1[:4], WEEK1[4:]


def auc_with(cat, num):
    """Fit the same recipe on some of the columns and give its test AUC."""
    parts = ([("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat)] if cat else []) + ([("num", StandardScaler(), num)] if num else [])
    m = make_pipeline(ColumnTransformer(parts), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(X_train[cat + num], y_train)
    return round(float(roc_auc_score(y_test, m.predict_proba(X_test[cat + num])[:, 1])), 3)


print({"all columns": auc_with(STATIC, WEEK1), "without the week-1 columns": auc_with(STATIC, []),
       "without the signup columns": auc_with([], WEEK1), "without the three flags": auc_with(STATIC, COUNTS)})
```

**Predict:** Which removal costs more ROC AUC: the week-1 columns or the signup columns? And what do the three flags add?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
{'all columns': 0.69, 'without the week-1 columns': 0.668, 'without the signup columns': 0.648, 'without the three flags': 0.691}
```

</details>

*Why:* Without the week-1 columns the AUC is 0.668, a loss of 0.022; without the signup columns it is 0.648, a loss of 0.042. So the signup columns carry more of the ranking, and the week-1 columns add a smaller, real part on top. Without the three flags the AUC is 0.691, a thousandth higher than with them: they add nothing here, and a lean recipe would leave them out. The truth is on the side of this reading: in the rules that made the data, the channel sets how active a person is (a factor from 1.80 for referral down to 0.35 for paid social), and activity is what keeps people coming back.

### 5. Is it memorizing, and the verdict

**Idea.** Score the model on the rows it learned from as well. A big gap between that score and the held-out score is a warning of overfitting; a small gap is only the absence of that warning, not proof that the model will hold up.

**Try it:** compare the model's training score with its held-out score, then write the verdict.

```python
print(round(model.score(X_train, y_train), 4), round(model.score(X_test, y_test), 4))
print(round(roc_auc_score(y_train, model.predict_proba(X_train)[:, 1]), 4), round(roc_auc_score(y_test, chance), 4))
```

**Predict:** Will the model's ROC AUC on the training part be more than 0.03 above its held-out AUC?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.6399 0.6322
0.6942 0.6898
```

</details>

*Why:* The training AUC is 0.6942 and the held-out AUC 0.6898, a gap of 0.0044; accuracy is 64.0 percent on the training part and 63.2 on the held-out part. Ten columns and 13,362 people leave little room to memorize. The verdict is the part you write:

> **Verdict.** On 4,454 held-out people the day-7 model has a ROC AUC of 0.690 and calls 63.2 of 100 right. A rule that knew only the signup columns scores 0.668 and 61.1, and the flat guess 0.5 and 54.4. The gain over the signup rule is 0.022 AUC, 95 percent interval 0.012 to 0.033.
>
> **Limits.** One split of one five-month stretch of a synthetic app. The share who churn rose from 50 percent of the January signups to 61 percent of the May signups, and this split mixes them, so it does not say how the model does on later people (lesson DS5 splits by time). The model ranks people; it does not say why anyone leaves.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A model is judged against the best simpler rule below it, not only the flat guess.
- `fit` learns, `predict_proba` gives chances, `predict` gives 0 or 1, and `score` is the accuracy for a classifier.
- Put an interval on the gain by resampling the same held-out people for both models.
- Remove a group of columns to see what carries the gain; a group that adds nothing can go.
- A verdict names the numbers with the baselines beside them, and a limits line says what they cannot tell.

**Common trap:** a score alone. 63 of 100 means nothing until the rule below it and the interval of the gain stand beside it.

</Aside>
