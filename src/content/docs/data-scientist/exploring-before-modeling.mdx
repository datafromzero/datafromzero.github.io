### 1. Grain, keys and gaps

**Idea.** Profile a table before you use it: its *grain* (what one row is), its *key* (the column that should be unique), its empty cells and its gaps between tables. `isna()` finds empty cells. A missing row has no cell to be empty, so find it by asking whether every key of one table exists in the other, with `isin`.

**Try it:** check the shape, the keys and the empty cells of `users` and `events`, then look for keys that have no match in the other table.

```python
print(users.shape, events.shape)
print(bool(users["user_id"].is_unique), bool(events["event_id"].is_unique))
print(int(users.isna().sum().sum()), int(events.isna().sum().sum()))
no_user = ~events["user_id"].isin(users["user_id"])
no_event = ~users["user_id"].isin(events["user_id"])
orphan_events, orphan_people, idle_users = int(no_user.sum()), events.loc[no_user, "user_id"].nunique(), int(no_event.sum())
print(orphan_events, orphan_people, idle_users)
print(users.loc[no_event, "signup_date"].min().date(), users.loc[no_event, "signup_date"].max().date())
```

**Predict:** How many empty cells are there in `users` and `events` together? How many events belong to a person with no row in `users`, and how many users have no event at all?

*Why:* No empty cell anywhere: 0. Yet 393 events belong to 40 people who have no row in `users`, and 10 users have no event; they signed up between June 12 and June 28. `isna()` cannot see either group. The second group falls outside the window of this track's table (lesson DS3).

### 2. Copies and impossible values

**Idea.** Two kinds of rows should not count as they stand. A *copy* is one event written twice. Define it by the columns that say what happened: who, when, what and where. An *impossible value* breaks a rule of the data, such as an event that arrives before it happened. `duplicated(subset=...)` marks every row after the first of its kind.

**Try it:** mark the copies, look at one pair, and look for events dated after they arrived.

```python
print(int(events.duplicated().sum()), int(events.duplicated(subset=KEY).sum()))
pair = events[events.duplicated(subset=KEY, keep=False)].sort_values(KEY + ["event_id"]).head(2)
print(pair[["event_id", "event_ts", "event_name", "received_at"]].to_string(index=False))
copies = int(events.duplicated(subset=KEY).sum())
print(sorted(set(events["event_name"]) - set(events.loc[events.duplicated(subset=KEY), "event_name"])))
future = int((events["event_ts"] > events["received_at"]).sum())
print(future)
```

**Predict:** `events.duplicated()` with no arguments, and `events.duplicated(subset=KEY)`: how many rows does each mark?

*Why:* None, and 835. With no arguments pandas compares every column, including `event_id`, which is different on each row, so nothing can repeat; the key `KEY` (person, time, name, platform) finds 835 extra rows. The pair shows the pattern: one person, one second, one name, and a `received_at` a few seconds apart, as if the app retried the send. Four names never have a copy: `cancel_subscription`, `onboarding_complete`, `purchase` and `signup_complete`, the events that count people and money. Another 30 events carry an `event_ts` later than their `received_at`, which is impossible: a phone clock ran ahead.

### 3. Two clocks

**Idea.** A log has two clocks: when an event happened (`event_ts`) and when the collector stored it (`received_at`). Profile both, by hour or by day, because the totals of one clock hide what the other shows. What an app knew at a moment is what had arrived by then, not what had happened.

**Try it:** count the events of March 17 by the hour they happened and by the hour they arrived.

```python
day = pd.Timestamp("2026-03-17")
by_hour = pd.DataFrame({
    "happened": events[events["event_ts"].dt.normalize() == day].groupby(events["event_ts"].dt.hour).size(),
    "arrived": events[events["received_at"].dt.normalize() == day].groupby(events["received_at"].dt.hour).size(),
}).fillna(0).astype(int)
print(by_hour.loc[13:21].T.to_string())
wait = events["received_at"] - events["event_ts"]
late = int((wait >= pd.Timedelta(hours=1)).sum())
print(late, int((wait >= pd.Timedelta(days=1)).sum()))
```

**Predict:** On March 17 the collector was down for part of the afternoon. For the hours 14 to 19, what will the row "arrived" show, and what will it show for hour 20?

### 4. A name that changed

**Idea.** A category can change meaning over time. Count the values of an event or category column by time and by platform, not only in total, before you build a definition on a name.

**Try it:** count the two paywall event names before and from May 18, by platform.

```python
paywall = events[events["event_name"].isin(["view_paywall", "paywall_viewed"])]
from_may_18 = paywall["event_ts"] >= pd.Timestamp("2026-05-18")
print(pd.crosstab([from_may_18.rename("from May 18"), paywall["platform"]], paywall["event_name"]).to_string())
renamed = int((events["event_name"] == "paywall_viewed").sum())
print(renamed)
```

**Predict:** From May 18 the iOS app sends `paywall_viewed` for the paywall. Which platforms still send `view_paywall` from May 18, and does any platform send both names?

```text
            ios                    0          2804
```

*Why:* Android and web keep `view_paywall`; iOS sends only `paywall_viewed`, 1,303 rows, and no platform sends both. Nothing about behavior changed, only a name. The fix is to treat the two names as one event before any count.

### 5. The problem list, and the split

**Idea.** A problem list has three columns: the problem, the rows it touches, and a decision. *Fix* a problem that changes a number the model will read and that you can correct. *Flag* one you cannot correct but a reader must know. *Leave* one that falls outside the table or changes nothing. Write the list from profiles that never use the target. A decision that does use the target, such as which rows are noise, is made on the training part only, after the split. the page of scikit-learn (the Python library that fits the models of this track) on common pitfalls, the mistakes that are easy to make, says that test data "should never be used to make choices about the model" and gives the general rule "to never call fit on the test data". Keeping the target out of the problem list, and looking at it on the training part only, is this lesson's own reading of that rule, not the page's wording.

**Try it:** collect the evidence of units 1 to 4 and of the signups with a short follow-up, then split the people with the seed of the track and look at the target on the training part only.

```python
incomplete = int((users["signup_date"] >= pd.Timestamp("2026-06-01")).sum())     # signed up from June 1 on: fewer than 30 days of follow-up before the export
problems = pd.DataFrame([
    ("events of people with no user row", orphan_events, "flag"),
    ("users with no event", idle_users, "leave"),
    ("retry copies of events", copies, "fix"),
    ("events dated after they arrived", future, "flag"),
    ("events that arrived an hour or more late", late, "fix"),
    ("paywall_viewed, the renamed event", renamed, "fix"),
    ("signups with under 30 days of follow-up", incomplete, "fix"),
], columns=["problem", "rows", "decision"])
print(problems.to_string(index=False))
train, test = train_test_split(table, test_size=0.25, random_state=SEED, stratify=table["churned"])      # the split of the track, made before the target is used
```

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Profile the grain, the key and the gaps; `isna()` finds empty cells, `isin` finds missing rows.
- A copy is defined by the columns that say what happened, never by the row id.
- A log has two clocks; what the app knew is what had arrived.
- Count a category by time and by platform before you build a definition on its name.
- Write the problem list from profiles that never use the target; decide with the target on the training part only.

**Common trap:** a clean-looking check. `isna()` found no empty cell and the table still had two kinds of gaps.

</Aside>
