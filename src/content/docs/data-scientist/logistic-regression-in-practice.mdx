### 1. A line for the odds

**Idea.** Logistic regression fits a line to the *log of the odds* of the outcome. The odds are the chance divided by one minus the chance (a chance of 0.75 is odds of 3 to 1). A coefficient is the amount one more unit of its column adds to the log odds, so `exp(coefficient)`, the *odds ratio*, is the factor it multiplies the odds by.

**Try it:** fit a model on one column, the active days of the first week, and read its coefficient.

```python
one = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X_fit[["active_days_7d"]], y_fit)       # one column: active days in the first week
w, w0 = float(one.coef_[0][0]), float(one.intercept_[0])
print(round(w, 3), round(float(np.exp(w)), 3))
days = pd.DataFrame({"active_days_7d": [0, 1, 2, 3]})
chance = one.predict_proba(days)[:, 1]
print(np.round(chance, 3))
print(np.round(chance[1:] / (1 - chance[1:]) / (chance[:-1] / (1 - chance[:-1])), 3))       # odds at 1, 2, 3 days over the odds a day earlier
print(np.round(y_fit.groupby(X_fit["active_days_7d"]).mean().loc[[0, 1, 2, 3]].to_numpy(), 3))
```

**Predict:** Each extra active day multiplies the odds of churning by a factor. Is it about 0.2, about 0.6 or about 1.0?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
-0.475 0.622
[0.685 0.575 0.457 0.343]
[0.622 0.622 0.622]
[0.699 0.566 0.445 0.369]
```

</details>

*Why:* The coefficient is -0.475, so the odds ratio is 0.622: each extra active day takes about 38 percent off the odds of churning, and the same factor holds from 0 to 1 day, 1 to 2 and 2 to 3. The chances it gives, 0.685, 0.575, 0.457 and 0.343, sit close to the shares that really churned in each group of the fitting part, 0.699, 0.566, 0.445 and 0.369. In symbols, with $$\hat{p}$$ the chance, $$x$$ the active days, $$w$$ the coefficient and $$w_0$$ the intercept, the formula of scikit-learn's documentation for $$\hat{p}$$ means

$$
\frac{\hat{p}}{1-\hat{p}} = e^{w_0 + w\,x}
$$

so one more day multiplies the right side by $$e^{w}$$.

### 2. Coefficients on one scale, with an interval

**Idea.** A coefficient is per unit of its own column, so columns in different units cannot be compared as they stand. Scale each number column to a standard deviation, and its odds ratio is per one standard deviation. A text column becomes 0/1 columns with one level left out (`drop`), and each odds ratio is against that reference level. An interval from refitting on resamples says how far each odds ratio could move. One more thing is built in: `LogisticRegression` applies an L2 penalty by default (`C=1.0`; the scikit-learn page says that "regularization is applied by default"), which shrinks the coefficients a little toward zero, and on columns scaled like these the shrinkage is slight. *Regularization* (DS6) turns the penalty up and chooses its strength.

**Try it:** fit the model with all the week-1 columns and refit it on 300 resamples of the fitting people.

```python
def recipe(num):
    """One-hot channel, platform and region (one level left out of each), the number columns scaled, then the model."""
    cat = OneHotEncoder(drop=["organic_search", "web", "north_america"])
    return make_pipeline(ColumnTransformer([("cat", cat, STATIC), ("num", StandardScaler(), num)]), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))


def odds_ratios(m):
    return pd.Series(np.exp(m[-1].coef_[0]), index=[n.split("__")[1] for n in m[0].get_feature_names_out()])


full = recipe(WEEK1).fit(X_fit, y_fit)
point = odds_ratios(full)
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
boot = []
for _ in range(300):                                           # refit on 300 resamples of the fitting people
    i = rng.integers(0, len(X_fit), len(X_fit))
    boot.append(odds_ratios(recipe(WEEK1).fit(X_fit.iloc[i], y_fit.iloc[i])))
low, high = pd.concat(boot, axis=1).quantile(0.025, axis=1), pd.concat(boot, axis=1).quantile(0.975, axis=1)
show = ["channel_paid_social", "channel_referral", "expenses_7d", "active_days_7d", "region_europe"]
print(pd.DataFrame({"odds ratio": point, "low": low, "high": high}).loc[show].round(3).to_string())
```

**Predict:** In unit 1 one extra active day gave an odds ratio of 0.62. Now `active_days_7d` sits in a model with the other week-1 columns, per standard deviation (about one day). Will its odds ratio be about the same, much closer to 1, or below 0.3?

### 3. Can the chances be read as chances?

**Idea.** A model is *calibrated* when, among the people it gives a chance of about 0.8, about 80 percent have the outcome. A cost threshold reads the chances as chances, so check before you use it: bin the chances of the validation part and compare each bin's mean chance with its share of real churners.

**Try it:** bin the validation chances and compare.

```python
p_val = full.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1]
bins = pd.cut(p_val, [0, 0.3, 0.45, 0.6, 0.75, 1.0])
check_table = pd.DataFrame({"chance": p_val, "churned": y_val.to_numpy(), "bin": bins})
print(check_table.groupby("bin", observed=True).agg(people=("churned", "size"), mean_chance=("chance", "mean"), share_churned=("churned", "mean")).round(3).to_string())
```

**Predict:** In the bin from 0.60 to 0.75, will the share of people who really churned be clearly below the mean chance the model gave them, near it, or clearly above it?

Neither condition was checked on this table, so the bins are the evidence, and here they agree; a model that was not calibrated would need its chances corrected before any threshold is read from them.

### 4. A threshold from error costs, chosen on the validation part

**Idea.** Cost the two wrong calls as regrets, what each costs against the right call, and every threshold has a cost for each 1,000 people. With calibrated chances, flag a person when the chance is at least $$c_{FP} / (c_{FP} + c_{FN})$$, the rule of [*Choosing a metric*](/core/metrics-and-error-costs/) (C21). Read the cheapest threshold off the validation part, then score it once on the test part, and report precision (the share of flagged people who are leavers, those who churn) and recall (the share of leavers who are flagged).

**Try it:** choose the threshold on the validation part, then score it on the test part, beside messaging nobody and messaging everybody.

```python
FP, FN = 2.0, 1.0                  # assumptions of this example: a wasted offer costs $2; a leaver left without an offer forgoes $1
grid = np.round(np.arange(0.05, 0.96, 0.01), 2)


def cost_per_1000(p, y, t):
    """Cost of flagging everyone whose chance is at least t, for every 1,000 people."""
    flag = p >= t
    return 1000 * (FP * (flag & (y == 0)).sum() + FN * (~flag & (y == 1)).sum()) / len(y)


yv, yt = y_val.to_numpy(), y_test.to_numpy()
best = float(grid[int(np.argmin([cost_per_1000(p_val, yv, t) for t in grid]))])       # chosen on the validation part
print(round(FP / (FP + FN), 3), best)
p_test = full.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]                                              # the test part is used once, here
flag = p_test >= best
print(round(cost_per_1000(p_test, yt, best)), round(cost_per_1000(p_test, yt, 0.0)), round(cost_per_1000(p_test, yt, 1.01)))     # model, everybody, nobody
print(round(float(flag.mean()), 3), round(float(precision_score(yt, flag)), 3), round(float(recall_score(yt, flag)), 3))
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
spread = []
for _ in range(300):                                           # how far does the cheapest threshold move when the validation people are resampled?
    i = rng.integers(0, len(yv), len(yv))
    spread.append(float(grid[int(np.argmin([cost_per_1000(p_val[i], yv[i], t) for t in grid]))]))
print(np.percentile(spread, [2.5, 50, 97.5]))
```

**Predict:** With these costs, will the rule beat both "message nobody" and "message everybody" on the test part, and about what share of people will it flag: a quarter, a half or most?

*Why:* The assumptions are the example's, not the data's: a win-back offer costs $2; one in four leavers who get it comes back, worth $12, so an offer to a leaver gains $3 and to a person who would have stayed it only costs $2. A wasted offer costs $2 and a leaver left without one misses out on $1 ($3 gained, less the $2 offer). The formula gives 0.667; the cheapest threshold on the validation part is 0.65. On the test part, for every 1,000 people, the rule costs $464, messaging nobody $544 and messaging everybody $912: it saves $80 against the better of the two, and flags 23.8 percent of people. Precision is 0.78: of every 100 offers, 78 go to people who would have left. Recall is 0.34: the offers reach about a third of the leavers. The cheapest threshold moves when the validation people are resampled, from 0.59 to 0.75 for the middle 95 percent (the middle is 0.65), so a threshold is a region and not a point. A single validation part is one draw; lesson DS5 replaces it with folds.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- The coefficient is a change in the log odds; `exp(coefficient)` is the odds ratio.
- Scale number columns before comparing them, and read a text column against its reference level; an interval says how far each could move.
- An odds ratio belongs to the model it sits in.
- Check calibration in bins before a threshold reads the chances as chances.
- Choose the threshold on a validation part, score it once on the test part, and report precision and recall there.

**Common trap:** reading an odds ratio of 3 as "three times as likely". It multiplies the odds, and the chance rises by less.

</Aside>

**A threshold for other costs.** Before you start, say in one sentence what the result looks like: a threshold well under 0.5, and a cost of a few hundred dollars. Now a wasted offer costs $1 and a leaver left without one forgoes $3. Use the validation chances `p_val` and the test chances `p_test` of unit 4 and its `grid`: find the cheapest threshold on the validation part, and put the list [that threshold, its cost for every 1,000 test people, rounded to a whole dollar] in `answer_2`. Write the code in the empty cell of the notebook. Expected: `answer_2` is a list of two numbers.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Copy `cost_per_1000` with the new costs in it (give them names of their own, so that unit 4 still works), take the `argmin` over `grid` on the validation part, and score that threshold on the test part.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Half-solution</summary>

Expected output in words: *"A threshold near the formula's 0.25, and a cost a little under that of messaging everybody ($456 for every 1,000 test people)."*

```python
best2 = float(grid[int(np.argmin([cost2(p_val, yv, t) for t in grid]))])
answer_2 = [best2, round(float(cost2(p_test, yt, best2)))]
```

</details>

<Solution>

```python
FP2, FN2 = 1.0, 3.0
def cost2(p, y, t):
    flag = p >= t
    return 1000 * (FP2 * (flag & (y == 0)).sum() + FN2 * (~flag & (y == 1)).sum()) / len(y)
best2 = float(grid[int(np.argmin([cost2(p_val, yv, t) for t in grid]))])
answer_2 = [best2, round(float(cost2(p_test, yt, best2)))]
```

The cheapest threshold on the validation part is 0.27, close to the formula's 0.25, and it costs $441 for every 1,000 test people. Messaging nobody costs $1,633 for every 1,000 test people and messaging everybody $456, so the rule saves $15 against the better of the two. When a missed leaver costs three times a wasted offer, the rule flags more people: the costs move the threshold, and the model stays the same.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
