## The big idea

A tree asks a chain of yes/no questions about single columns; a forest averages many such trees, so that the luck of one tree does not decide.

### 1. How a tree picks its questions

**Idea.** A tree asks one yes/no question about one column, sends each person left or right, and asks again inside each group. It picks the question that makes the two groups purest. For two outcomes, the *Gini impurity* of a group with a share $$p$$ of churners is $$2p(1-p)$$: 0 when everyone in the group has the same outcome, 0.5 when the group is split evenly. This is the form $$\sum_k p_k(1-p_k)$$ of the scikit-learn guide, for two classes. The impurity after a question is the average of the two groups' impurities, each weighted by its number of people.

**Try it:** score one question for every column, a stump of depth 1, and print a tree of depth 2.

```python
names = list(prep().fit(X_fit).get_feature_names_out())
Xf = pd.DataFrame(prep().fit_transform(X_fit), columns=names)         # the fitting people as 0/1 and number columns
p = y_fit.mean()
print("impurity of all the fitting people:", round(float(2 * p * (1 - p)), 3))
rows = []
for col in names:
    stump = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1, random_state=SEED).fit(Xf[[col]], y_fit)       # one question about one column
    t = stump.tree_
    after = (t.n_node_samples[1] * t.impurity[1] + t.n_node_samples[2] * t.impurity[2]) / t.n_node_samples[0]
    rows.append((col, round(float(t.threshold[0]), 1), round(float(after), 3)))
print(pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["column", "split at", "impurity after"]).sort_values("impurity after").head(4).to_string(index=False))
two = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, min_samples_leaf=50, random_state=SEED).fit(Xf, y_fit)
print(export_text(two, feature_names=names))
```

**Predict:** The fitting people start at an impurity of 0.496. Which single question will lower it most: one about `channel_paid_social`, `expenses_7d` or `active_days_7d`?

A split is chosen for the group in front of it alone, so the tree is built question by question and never plans ahead.

### 2. Leaf size is the stronger brake

**Idea.** With no limit a tree keeps asking until each leaf is pure, and memorizes, as in lesson DS5. Two settings hold it back: `max_depth`, the most questions in a row, and `min_samples_leaf`, the fewest people a leaf may hold. The scikit-learn guide says that a very small leaf size "will usually mean the tree will overfit". Try both on the five folds, with `GridSearchCV` as in lesson DS6.

**Try it:** score 42 combinations of depth and leaf size on the five folds, and print three depths against three leaf sizes.

```python
grid = {"decisiontreeclassifier__max_depth": [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10], "decisiontreeclassifier__min_samples_leaf": [1, 10, 50, 100, 200, 400]}
tree_search = GridSearchCV(tree(), grid, cv=folds, scoring="roc_auc").fit(X_train, y_train)
cv_table = pd.DataFrame(tree_search.cv_results_)
cv_table = cv_table.pivot(index="param_decisiontreeclassifier__max_depth", columns="param_decisiontreeclassifier__min_samples_leaf", values="mean_test_score")
cv_table.index.name, cv_table.columns.name = "depth", "leaf"
print(cv_table.loc[[3, 6, 10], [1, 50, 400]].round(3).to_string())
print(tree_search.best_params_, round(float(tree_search.best_score_), 3), round(float(cross_val_score(logit(), X_train, y_train, cv=folds, scoring="roc_auc").mean()), 3))
```

**Predict:** A tree of depth 10 can have leaves of one person or leaves of at least 400 people. Will the two score about the same on the folds?

*Why:* No. At depth 10 the leaves of one person score 0.645 and the leaves of at least 400 score 0.688, a gap of 0.043. With big leaves the depth hardly matters (0.688 at depth 6, 0.688 at depth 10), because a tree whose leaves must hold 400 of a fold's 10,689 fitting people has at most 26 leaves, and extra depth has nowhere to go. The best of the 42 is depth 6 with leaves of at least 400 people, 0.688 on the folds, below the 0.692 of the day-7 model on the same folds. A leaf size of 400 is the edge of the grid, so a larger one might score a little higher; the lesson does not try.

### 3. Many trees, averaged

**Idea.** A *random forest* grows many trees. Each is built from a bootstrap sample of the people, drawn with replacement, and each split looks at a random subset of the columns (`max_features` sets how many). The forest's chance for a person is the average of its trees' chances; the scikit-learn guide describes the same three steps. Averaging calms the luck of any one tree. `forest(trees, leaf)` in the setup cell builds one.

**Try it:** score forests of 1 to 300 trees on the validation part, then forests with three leaf sizes on the folds.

```python
forest_curve = []
for trees in [1, 5, 25, 100, 300]:
    m = forest(trees).fit(X_fit, y_fit)
    forest_curve.append((trees, round(float(roc_auc_score(y_val, m.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1])), 3)))
print(forest_curve)
leaf_rows = []
for leaf in [1, 20, 100]:
    s = cross_val_score(forest(200, leaf), X_train, y_train, cv=folds, scoring="roc_auc")
    leaf_rows.append((leaf, round(float(s.mean()), 3), round(float(s.min()), 3), round(float(s.max()), 3)))
    print(*leaf_rows[-1])
```

**Predict:** How many trees until the validation AUC stops improving: about 5, about 25 or about 300? And will a forest with leaves of one person score the same as one with leaves of 100?

*Why:* One tree scores 0.661, five 0.695, 25 0.698, 100 0.699 and 300 0.698: after about 25 trees the validation part sees no more gain. The leaf size still matters: leaves of one person score 0.626 on the folds, 20 people 0.687 and 100 people 0.691 (folds from 0.680 to 0.698). Averaging does not cure a tree that memorizes: each tree of the first forest still memorizes the rows of its own sample.

### 4. Importances with caution

**Idea.** A fitted forest reports an importance for every column: the share of the impurity drop that the column's questions produced, over all trees, with the shares adding up to 1. *Permutation importance* asks another question: shuffle one column of held-out people, score the fitted model again, and read the drop in score. To test both, add a column of random numbers, which cannot predict churn.

**Try it:** add a random column, fit forests with two leaf sizes, and compare the built-in importance with the permutation importance on the validation people.

```python
Xn = X_fit.assign(noise=np.random.default_rng(SEED).normal(size=len(X_fit)))                # a column of random numbers: nothing in it predicts churn
Xnv = X_val.assign(noise=np.random.default_rng(SEED + 1).normal(size=len(X_val)))
imp_rank, built_in_by = {}, {}
for leaf in (1, 100):
    m = make_pipeline(prep(WEEK1 + ["noise"]), RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=leaf, n_jobs=-1, random_state=SEED)).fit(Xn, y_fit)
    built_in = pd.Series(m[-1].feature_importances_, index=m[0].get_feature_names_out()).sort_values(ascending=False)       # the importance that comes with the fitted forest
    built_in_by[leaf] = built_in
    imp_rank[leaf] = (list(built_in.index).index("noise") + 1, round(float(built_in["noise"]), 3))
    print(f"leaf {leaf}: random column ranks {list(built_in.index).index('noise') + 1} of {len(built_in)} with share {built_in['noise']:.3f}; first is {built_in.index[0]} with {built_in.iloc[0]:.3f}")
held = permutation_importance(m, Xnv, y_val, scoring="roc_auc", n_repeats=10, random_state=SEED, n_jobs=-1)      # m is the forest with leaves of 100: shuffle one column of the validation people at a time
perm = pd.Series(held.importances_mean, index=Xnv.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)
print(perm.round(4).to_string())
```

**Predict:** In a forest with leaves of one person (the setting scikit-learn starts from), where will the built-in importance rank the random column among the 20 columns: last, in the middle, or first? And what will shuffling it do to the score of held-out people?

The scikit-learn guide says that impurity-based importance for trees is "strongly biased" and favors "high cardinality features" such as numbers, and an abstract of Strobl and colleagues reports that such measures can be misleading because "suboptimal predictor variables may be artificially preferred". A random number has a different value for nearly every person, so a tree can always find a cut that happens to fit some of them, and that luck is counted as importance.

*Why:* No. The day-7 model scores 0.690, the forest 0.688 and the tuned tree 0.684. The forest's gain is -0.001, with a 95 percent interval of -0.004 to 0.002: it includes 0, so the forest ties. The tree's gain is -0.006, -0.010 to -0.001: the single tree is worse. A tree can bend where a straight line cannot, and on this table the trees found no bend. The ladder reads 0.5 for the flat guess, 0.668 for the signup columns, and 0.690, 0.688 and 0.684 for three kinds of model.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A tree asks one yes/no question about one column at a time, and picks the question that makes the two groups purest.
- Limit a tree with leaf size and depth, chosen on folds; leaves of one person memorize.
- A forest averages trees grown on bootstrap samples with random columns; after a few dozen trees, more add little, and leaf size still matters.
- The importance that comes with a forest is computed on the fitting rows and can reward random numbers; shuffle columns of held-out people instead.
- Put every model beside the simpler one, on the same people, with an interval.

**Common trap:** reading the built-in importance list of a forest as the columns worth keeping.

</Aside>

The biggest single drop is 0.020 and the four columns shuffled together cost 0.077, about 4 times as much. The four columns count the same week of activity in different ways, so when one is shuffled the forest reads the rest from the other three. The scikit-learn guide says this about correlated features: the model "still has access to the latter through its correlated feature". Shuffle a group of correlated columns together to see what the group is worth.
