## The question

The team of lesson DS2 has one score for its model, 0.690, from one split. Before it goes to anyone, they ask the questions a reviewer will ask: *is that number luck, would it hold on people who signed up later, and could the model have peeked?* The three questions are the three halves of this lesson: how far a score moves, how to cut the folds so that they look like the use, and how to keep the model from seeing what it is scored on. ## The big idea

A score is only as honest as the rows it was measured on: cross-validation measures it several times on rows the model never saw, and shows how far it moves.

### 1. Overfitting is a gap

**Idea.** *Overfitting* is learning the rows instead of the pattern: the model scores well on the rows it learned from and worse on rows it has not seen. Make the gap visible with a model whose flexibility is one number. A decision tree splits the rows again and again by a question about one column, and `max_depth` is the maximum depth of the tree; with no limit it keeps splitting until its leaves are pure or too small to split. Score it twice at each depth: on the fitting rows, and on a validation part held back from them.

**Try it:** fit a tree at eight depths on part of the training people and score each on the fitting rows and on the validation rows.

```python
X_fit, X_val, y_fit, y_val = train_test_split(X_train, y_train, test_size=0.25, random_state=SEED, stratify=y_train)   # a validation part, cut from the training part
curve = []
for depth in [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, None]:                                   # None: let the tree grow until it cannot split any more
    m = tree(depth).fit(X_fit, y_fit)
    curve.append(("no limit" if depth is None else depth, round(float(roc_auc_score(y_fit, m.predict_proba(X_fit)[:, 1])), 3), round(float(roc_auc_score(y_val, m.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1])), 3)))
print(pd.DataFrame(curve, columns=["max_depth", "AUC on the fitting rows", "AUC on the validation rows"]).to_string(index=False))
```

**Predict:** As the depth grows, the AUC on the fitting rows keeps rising. Will the AUC on the validation rows keep rising too, rise and then fall, or stay flat? At about what depth does it stop improving?

### 2. K-fold: a score and its spread

**Idea.** A validation part is one draw. *K-fold cross-validation* cuts the training rows into k folds, fits on k − 1 of them and scores on the one left out, and repeats until every fold has been the one left out, so each score is on rows its model never saw. Report the mean and the range of the fold scores. To compare two models, give both the same folds (an integer seed in the splitter) and compare fold by fold.

**Try it:** score the day-7 model on five stratified folds of the training part, and score a depth-3 tree on the same five folds.

```python
scores = cross_val_score(logit(), X_train, y_train, cv=folds, scoring="roc_auc")      # five fits, each scored on the fold it did not learn from
print(np.round(scores, 3), round(float(scores.mean()), 3), round(float(scores.std()), 4))
tree_scores = cross_val_score(tree(3), X_train, y_train, cv=folds, scoring="roc_auc")  # the same five folds for a second model
print(round(float(tree_scores.mean()), 3), np.round(scores - tree_scores, 3))
```

**Predict:** Will the five fold scores of the day-7 model all lie within 0.01 of each other, within 0.03, or further apart? And will it beat the depth-3 tree on every fold?

[0.685 0.687 0.701 0.702 0.683] 0.692 0.0082
0.676 [0.014 0.012 0.017 0.022 0.015]

*Why:* The five scores run from 0.683 to 0.702, a range of 0.019: within 0.03 but not within 0.01. Their mean is 0.692, and the single split of lesson DS2 (0.690) was one draw of this kind. The model beats the tree on all five folds, by 0.012 to 0.022, so the comparison is clear even though each score moves by about 0.008: the same folds make the fold-to-fold differences small. This lesson has scored nothing on the test part yet.

### 3. Folds that fit the question

**Idea.** Folds should look like the use. *Stratified* folds keep the share of each class equal in every fold (`StratifiedKFold`); it matters when the classes are unequal or the rows come in blocks. A model that will be used on later people needs *time-based* folds: `TimeSeriesSplit` trains on the past and tests on what comes next, and each training set holds the one before it.

**Try it:** compare the churn share of blocks cut in time order with that of stratified folds, then score the day-7 model on four time-based folds.

```python
Xs, ys = X_train.sort_index(), y_train.sort_index()              # the training people in signup order (user_id rises with the signup time)
print([round(float(ys.iloc[test].mean()), 3) for _, test in KFold(5).split(Xs)])        # five blocks in time order, no shuffling
print([round(float(ys.iloc[test].mean()), 3) for _, test in folds.split(Xs, ys)])       # stratified folds
rows_ = []
for train_ix, test_ix in TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=4).split(Xs):                          # each fold trains on the past and tests on what comes next
    m = logit().fit(Xs.iloc[train_ix], ys.iloc[train_ix])
    p = m.predict_proba(Xs.iloc[test_ix])[:, 1]
    rows_.append((len(train_ix), round(float(ys.iloc[test_ix].mean()), 3), round(float(p.mean()), 3), round(float(roc_auc_score(ys.iloc[test_ix], p)), 3)))
print(pd.DataFrame(rows_, columns=["trained on", "churned in test block", "mean chance", "AUC"]).to_string(index=False))
print(round(float(np.mean([r[3] for r in rows_])), 3))
gapped = []
for train_ix, test_ix in TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=4, gap=1500).split(Xs):                # the same folds with 1,500 people left out between train and test
    gapped.append(round(float(roc_auc_score(ys.iloc[test_ix], logit().fit(Xs.iloc[train_ix], ys.iloc[train_ix]).predict_proba(Xs.iloc[test_ix])[:, 1])), 3))
print(gapped, round(float(np.mean(gapped)), 3))
```

**Predict:** The model will be used on later signups. Will its mean AUC on four time-based folds be lower than the 0.692 of the random folds, about the same, or higher?

*Why:* Cut in time order without shuffling, the five blocks have churn shares from 0.500 to 0.611: the last two blocks come after the paid-social campaign began on April 13 (the README's dates). The stratified folds hold 0.544 each. The time-based AUC is 0.651, 0.667, 0.708 and 0.728, a mean of 0.688: about the same as the random folds. The first fold is the lowest, and it trained on only 2,674 people. These folds also leave no gap: a label reads as far as 30 days past signup, so the last people of a training block have labels that reach 30 days past the start of the test block. `TimeSeriesSplit` has a `gap` argument that leaves people out between the two; with a gap of 1,500 people the AUCs are 0.654, 0.665, 0.709 and 0.726 (mean 0.689): they hardly move, though a gap of this size covers only part of the 30 days. The mean chance follows the real share in every block (0.499 against 0.507 in the first, 0.599 against 0.611 in the last), because the channel columns carry the shift. For this data the model holds up on later people; June, after the campaign ended on June 7, is not in the table.

### 4. Learning inside each fold

**Idea.** Every step that learns from the rows, a scaler, an encoder or a feature selector, is part of the model, and in cross-validation it must be fitted inside each fold on that fold's training rows only. Put the steps in a `Pipeline` and give the pipeline to `cross_val_score`: it refits every step in every fold. A step fitted before the folds has already seen the labels of the rows it will be scored on.

**Try it:** pick the 20 best of 300 random columns outside the folds, and then inside them.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
pilot = X_train.sample(400, random_state=SEED)
y_pilot = y_train.loc[pilot.index].to_numpy()
noise = rng.normal(size=(400, 300))                   # 300 columns of random numbers: nothing in them predicts churn
five = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=SEED)
top20 = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20).fit(noise, y_pilot)             # outside the folds: it looks at every label first
leaky = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000), top20.transform(noise), y_pilot, cv=five, scoring="roc_auc")
honest = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)), noise, y_pilot, cv=five, scoring="roc_auc")
print(round(float(leaky.mean()), 3), round(float(honest.mean()), 3))
```

**Predict:** The 300 columns are random numbers: nothing in them predicts churn. The first route picks the 20 columns most related to churn from all 400 people and then cross-validates; the second puts the selection inside the folds. What AUC will each report: near 0.5, a little above, or well above?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
0.718 0.489
```

</details>

*Why:* The first route reports 0.718 for columns that carry no information, and the second 0.489, near 0.5, which is the truth. Outside the folds the selector had already seen every label, so the rows that later played the part of unseen helped to choose the columns. scikit-learn's pitfalls page shows the same with 10,000 random features: an accuracy of 0.76 on a target that is pure chance. The scaler of lesson C20 is the mild case, because only means leak; a selector that reads the labels is the strong one.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- Overfitting is a gap: a high score on the fitting rows and a lower one on unseen rows.
- Cross-validation scores a model k times on rows it never saw; report the mean and the range, and give two models the same folds.
- Stratified folds keep the class shares equal; time-based folds train on the past and test on what comes next.
- A step that learns from the rows, above all one that reads the labels, belongs inside the folds: use a pipeline.
- Score the test part once, after every choice is made: in this lesson, Exercise 3.

**Common trap:** a cross-validated score that looks too good. Look for a step that learned before the folds were cut.

</Aside>
