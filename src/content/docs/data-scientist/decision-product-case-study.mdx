## The question

Maya Okafor, head of growth at the budgeting app, writes: *On day 7 after signup we could send a $2 win-back offer to the people likely to leave. Should we, and to whom? I need a yes or a no I can defend to finance, with the risk written next to it.* Maya and her question are invented for this exercise; the data are the synthetic `app-events` data.

You have built the pieces over the track. This capstone puts them in one memo she can use, and asks you to show in a log that you checked what an AI told you.

There is no single right memo. Several decisions earn full marks, and a number you cannot trace earns none.

## The brief

**The decision.** Send the day-7 win-back offer or not, and to whom, for people who sign up from July. You write the success definition: what number, under what rule, would make Maya say the offer worked.

**Treat these as facts.**

1. The table is the track's: 17,816 people who signed up from January 1 to May 31, 2026; the target `churned` is 1 for the 9,696 (54.4 percent) with no visit and no expense in days 8 to 30; the prediction moment is signup plus 7 days. The parts are the track's: 10,021 fitting people, 3,341 validation, 4,454 held out.
2. The offer economics are assumptions of *Logistic regression: predicting yes or no* (DS4), not facts about the app: an offer costs \$2; a leaver who gets one returns with chance 0.25, worth \$12. Nobody has measured the return rate.
3. Finance funds the offer only if it earns more than sending nothing, and wants to know what would make that wrong.
4. The held-out people are used once, here, for the final numbers.

## Rubric

Eight items, each scored 2 (full), 1 (partial) or 0 (none): 16 in all. The rubric grades what you shipped and how well it holds up, not the decision you chose: any decision that your reasoning supports can score 16.

**1. Framing page.** Count six things: a named stakeholder; the decision in one sentence; a success definition with a number and a rule; the target and the prediction moment as a time; every assumption listed; each marked as an assumption.

- **Full:** all six.
- **Partial:** four or five.
- **None:** three or fewer.

**2. Baselines and evaluation.**

- **Full:** a flat guess and a simpler rung or rule beside the model; the held-out people used once, after every choice; each gain with an interval; the value of the rule for every 1,000 people next to doing nothing and offering everybody, with an interval.
- **Partial:** baselines but no intervals, or no value of the rule.
- **None:** the model alone, or a choice made on the held-out people.

**3. Error analysis.**

- **Full:** at least two slices with the people counted in each; who the rule misses and who it offers to without need, with numbers; one cause tied to a rule of the data; one thing the analysis cannot say.
- **Partial:** one slice, or slices with no counts.
- **None:** an overall score only.

**4. Limits.**

- **Full:** at least three limits, each tied to a number or a rule of the data, including the assumption the decision rests on and the value at which it flips.
- **Partial:** one or two tied limits.
- **None:** generic limits, such as "the data is small", or none.

**5. The plan: make and undo.**

- **Full:** when and from what a score is made; where it is stored with its model version and time; who reads it; what happens when a run fails; a rollback; an owner.
- **Partial:** four or five of the six.
- **None:** three or fewer.

**6. The plan: watch and retrain.**

- **Full:** at least three named checks covering inputs, outputs and the outcome, each with a threshold, an owner and an action; a retraining trigger stated as a number and tried on past data, with how often it would have fired; one thing the checks cannot catch.
- **Partial:** checks without thresholds, or a trigger never tried on the past.
- **None:** "watch for drift" and nothing more.

**7. Decision memo.**

- **Full:** the decision first; for whom; three numbers, each with its rule (which people, which money, which assumption); the flip value; the risk; the next check and its owner; at most 300 words.
- **Partial:** four of those parts, or over 300 words.
- **None:** the model first and the decision missing, or a decision the numbers do not support.

**8. AI log and explain-back.**

- **Full:** a context pack with its four parts in order; a row for every AI output that fed a result, each with output, checks run (by number or name), verdict and reason; at least one revise or reject, or a sentence on why none; your name, the date and what you did not check; an explain-back that names one number and one check.
- **Partial:** a log with verdicts but no reasons or no named checks, or an explain-back that could describe any project.
- **None:** no log, or a list of prompts with no checks.

A total of 13 or more is strong. From 8 to 12 is solid with gaps: redo the items that scored 0 or 1. Below 8, rebuild the evaluation before you write the memo.

### Error analysis

```python
# Error analysis: who is offered, and which leavers does the rule miss?
errors = pd.DataFrame({"channel": X_test["channel"].to_numpy(), "platform": X_test["platform"].to_numpy(), "churned": yt, "offered": model_rule})


def slice_table(frame, col):
    """For each level of `col`: people, churn rate, share offered and share of leavers offered (recall)."""
    rows = {}
    for level, s in frame.groupby(col):
        rows[level] = [len(s), s["churned"].mean(), s["offered"].mean(), s.loc[s["churned"] == 1, "offered"].mean()]
    return pd.DataFrame.from_dict(rows, orient="index", columns=["people", "churn rate", "offered", "leavers offered"]).round(3)


print(slice_table(errors, "channel").to_string())
print(slice_table(errors, "platform").to_string())
print(slice_table(errors[errors["channel"] == "paid_social"], "platform").to_string())
```

### The plan on paper

**Make.** Version 1 ships the rule: a filter on the channel in the nightly job that finds the people whose day 7 passed that night, so there is no model to go stale. The same job scores the day-7 model beside it and stores `user_id`, the chance, the model version and the time, so the two can be compared. **Undo:** if a run fails or a check below fires, the job sends nothing, which gives up the \$81 and costs nothing else; the last model file is kept.

**Watch.** The notebook tries monitoring on the past: it fits the model on the signups before March 2 and scores each of the 13 full weeks after it, once its 30 days are over.

The mean chance follows the real churn in every week, never more than 0.037 away, and the weekly AUC stays between 0.636 and 0.732, although the share of paid-social signups jumps from 0.096 to 0.299 in the week of April 13. So an input change is an alert to look, not to retrain. Three checks, each with an owner and an action. *Inputs:* the paid-social share of signups, and for each platform the share of people with each column filled, against the last four weeks (owner: the data scientist; action: look). *Outputs:* the mean chance against the real churn of weeks whose 30 days are over. *Outcome:* the return rate in the held-back fifth; below 0.214 stops the offer (owner: growth).

A silent change shows why the input check names the platform. From May 18 the iOS app sends `paywall_viewed` for `view_paywall`. In the notebook's input cell, a pipeline that listed only the old name sees the share of iOS people with a paywall event fall from 0.315 in the week of May 11 to 0.000 in the week of May 18 (0.357 with both names), and the model runs on without an error. Rule 10 of the Rules of Machine Learning is "Watch for silent failures".

**Retrain.** Rule 8 asks how much a model loses if it is a day, a week or a quarter old. Scored on the 5,077 May signups, the model fitted before March gives AUC 0.716 and the one fitted before May 0.717, so two months of age cost 0.001. No schedule is needed; the trigger is the check of the outputs: a gap of more than 0.05 between the mean chance and the real churn, or a weekly AUC under 0.60, two weeks in a row. On the 13 back-test weeks it fires 0 times, so it gave no false alarm; the back-test cannot say that it would catch real decay, because this data holds none. The label needs 30 days, so the trigger sees a problem a month late.
