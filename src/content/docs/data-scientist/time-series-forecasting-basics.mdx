## The question

The support team of the budgeting app staffs the chat desk a week at a time and asks: *how many people will sign up on each day of next week?* The first seven days of the data:

| day | weekday | signups |
|---|---|---:|
| 2026-01-01 | Thursday | 125 |
| 2026-01-02 | Friday | 98 |
| 2026-01-03 | Saturday | 94 |
| 2026-01-04 | Sunday | 62 |
| 2026-01-05 | Monday | 82 |
| 2026-01-06 | Tuesday | 95 |
| 2026-01-07 | Wednesday | 92 |

By the end you can forecast a week from the weeks before it, score the forecast the way it will be used, and say whether it beats copying last week.

<Aside type="note" title="You already know this">

**Match.** In [*Overfitting and cross-validation*](/data-scientist/cross-validation-and-overfitting/) (DS5) the time folds trained on the past and tested on what came next. A backtest does that for every week.

**Diverge.** In DS5 each row was a person. A day depends on the days around it, so a feature built from its neighbors can carry the answer. Unit 3 shows what that does.

</Aside>

## The big idea

A forecast is judged by repeating the real task: stand on a Sunday night with only the past, forecast the week, then compare.

### 1. Trend, weekday pattern and noise

**Idea.** A daily count is a slow *trend*, a weekday pattern that repeats every 7 days, and *noise*. A moving average over one whole week gives the trend, because every weekday counts once. Divide the series by it and what is left is the weekday pattern and the noise; the mean of that ratio for each weekday is the weekday factor. The README of the dataset states its own factors, so we can check ours.

**Try it:** the trend and the weekday factors of the whole series, with the README's factors beside them.

```python
trend = signups.rolling(7, center=True).mean()                  # a window of one whole week: every weekday counts once, so the weekday pattern cancels
ratio = signups / trend                                             # what is left is the weekday pattern and the noise
factor = ratio.groupby(signups.index.dayofweek).mean()
factor = factor / factor.mean()                                     # weekday factors with an average of 1
days = ["Mon", "Tue", "Wed", "Thu", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"]
readme = pd.Series([1.00, 1.05, 1.05, 1.00, 0.90, 0.75, 0.80], index=range(7))      # the weekday factors of the dataset README
readme = readme / readme.mean()
print(pd.DataFrame({"estimated": factor.round(3).to_numpy(), "README": readme.round(3).to_numpy()}, index=days).T.to_string())
noise = signups - trend * factor.reindex(signups.index.dayofweek).to_numpy()
print(round(float(noise.std()), 1), round(float(np.sqrt(signups.mean())), 1))      # the spread of what is left, and the square root of the average day
```

**Predict:** Every weekday has a factor against the average day, and the factors average 1. Which day is the quietest, and is its factor about 0.6, about 0.8 or about 0.95?

The split leaves two things out: the first three days of every month, which the README says run 1.3 times busier, and a campaign. Units 3 and 4 meet them.

### 2. A baseline, and a backtest that rolls

**Idea.** The *seasonal naive* forecast gives each day the value of the same weekday one week earlier; Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, the authors of the textbook *Forecasting: Principles and Practice*, define it as setting each forecast to the last observed value from the same season. Any model must beat it to be worth keeping. A *rolling-origin* backtest scores it as it will be used: at each Sunday night, forecast the next 7 days from the days before; their text says no future observation may be used in constructing a forecast. scikit-learn's `TimeSeriesSplit` does the same by rows; here the unit is a week, so the loop is written out.

**Try it:** the seasonal naive forecast and a flat forecast, the mean of the last 7 days, over the 17 forecast weeks.

```python
def seasonal_naive(past, days):
    return past["y"].reindex(days - pd.Timedelta(days=7)).to_numpy()            # each day gets the value of the same weekday one week earlier


def last_week_mean(past, days):
    return np.full(len(days), past["y"].iloc[-7:].mean())                       # no weekday pattern: the average of the last 7 days for every day


b_naive, b_flat = backtest(seasonal_naive), backtest(last_week_mean)
print(len(weekly_mae(b_naive)), round(float(weekly_mae(b_naive).mean()), 2), round(float(weekly_mae(b_flat).mean()), 2))
print(round(float(df["y"].loc["2026-03-02":].mean()), 1))                        # the average day of the forecast weeks, for scale
```

**Predict:** The series has a strong weekday pattern. Will the seasonal naive forecast beat the flat last-7-days mean by a lot, by a little or not at all?

### 3. Features that exist on Sunday night

**Idea.** A *lag feature* is a past value of the series moved forward, `shift(7)` for the same weekday last week. A *rolling feature* is a summary of a window. By default pandas labels a window at its right edge, so `rolling(7)` on a day includes the day itself. Before using a feature, ask: on the Sunday night before the week, could I compute it? The forecaster below leaves the days to forecast empty, so a feature built from them is empty and cannot slip in; `roll_now` is the careless exception, because it reads the whole series from outside.

**Try it:** a linear model of the weekday, the first-three-days flag, last week's value and the mean of the last 7 days, and the same model plus `roll_now`.

```python
def week_features(y):
    """Features that exist on the Sunday night before a day's week: the same weekday last week and the mean of the last 7 days, plus the calendar."""
    origin = y.index - pd.to_timedelta(y.index.dayofweek + 1, unit="D")        # the Sunday before each day's week
    out = pd.DataFrame(index=y.index)
    out["lag7"] = y.shift(7)
    out["mean7"] = y.rolling(7).mean().reindex(origin).to_numpy()
    out["weekday"] = y.index.dayofweek
    out["month_start"] = (y.index.day <= 3).astype(float)                      # the 1st to the 3rd of a month: known in advance
    return out


def linear_forecaster(cols):
    def forecast(past, days):
        known = past["y"].reindex(past.index.union(days))                       # the days to forecast are empty here: nothing of theirs can leak in
        F = week_features(known)
        F["roll_now"] = df["y"].rolling(7).mean()                               # the careless feature: it reads the whole series, today included
        X = pd.get_dummies(F["weekday"].astype(int).astype(str), prefix="wd", dtype=float).reindex(columns=[f"wd_{i}" for i in range(7)], fill_value=0.0)
        X[["month_start"] + cols] = F[["month_start"] + cols]
        rows = X.index.isin(past.index) & ~X.isna().any(axis=1).to_numpy()
        model = LinearRegression().fit(X[rows], past["y"].reindex(X.index[rows]))
        return model.predict(X.loc[days])
    return forecast


b_model = backtest(linear_forecaster(["lag7", "mean7"]))
b_leaky = backtest(linear_forecaster(["lag7", "mean7", "roll_now"]))
print(round(float(weekly_mae(b_model).mean()), 2), round(float(weekly_mae(b_leaky).mean()), 2))
```

**Predict:** The careless model adds `roll_now`, the mean of the 7 days that end on the day itself. Will its backtest error be above, about the same as or well below the honest model's?

*Why:* Well below: 13.07 against 18.62. The careless feature has the day's own count inside it, so it looks like skill and is not; on a real Sunday night it does not exist. The honest model, 18.62, is 2.30 below the seasonal naive 20.92. Whether that is more than luck is the next unit.

### 4. Where the error comes from

**Idea.** A weekly error is an average of 7 noisy days, and a backtest is only 17 weeks, so look at the weeks one by one and put an interval on a difference by resampling whole weeks, never single days. Because the data is synthetic there is a reference no real series has: the README's expected number of signups for each day. It is not a forecast anyone could have made, but its error is the noise floor, the miss of a forecast that knows the truth.

**Try it:** the weekly errors of the three forecasts, the worst weeks of the model, and an interval for how much better the model is than the seasonal naive forecast.

```python
README_DAY = [1.00, 1.05, 1.05, 1.00, 0.90, 0.75, 0.80]


def expected(past, days):
    """The dataset's own expected signups for each day: a straight line from 90 to 125, the weekday factor, 1.3 on days 1 to 3, and the paid-social campaign."""
    out = []
    for d in days:
        level = 90 + 35 * (d - df.index[0]).days / 180
        level *= README_DAY[d.weekday()] * (1.3 if d.day <= 3 else 1)
        if pd.Timestamp("2026-04-13") <= d <= pd.Timestamp("2026-06-07"):
            level += 55 * min(1.0, 0.4 + 0.6 * (d - pd.Timestamp("2026-04-13")).days / 7) * README_DAY[d.weekday()]
        out.append(level)
    return out


b_truth = backtest(expected)
weeks = pd.DataFrame({"seasonal naive": weekly_mae(b_naive), "model": weekly_mae(b_model), "expected": weekly_mae(b_truth)})
print(weeks.mean().round(2).to_string())
worst = weeks["model"].sort_values(ascending=False).head(3)
print(worst.round(1).to_string())
print(round(float(worst.sum() / weeks["model"].sum()), 3))
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
diff = (weeks["seasonal naive"] - weeks["model"]).to_numpy()
means = [diff[rng.integers(0, len(diff), len(diff))].mean() for _ in range(2000)]      # resample whole weeks, not days
print(round(float(diff.mean()), 2), np.round(np.percentile(means, [2.5, 97.5]), 2))
```

**Predict:** The paid-social campaign starts in the week of April 13. Will the model's error in that week be about its average, about twice it or under 10?

*Why:* About twice: 36.9 against the average 18.62. No earlier week holds a campaign, so no forecast learned from the past can see it coming. The worst week, June 8 (67.7), is the first after the campaign stopped; May 4 (42.5) follows a week with the busy first three days of May, which the 7-day mean mistakes for the level. Three weeks of 17 hold 46.4 percent of the model's error. The expected values miss by 8.63 a day. That is about the floor: the real counts stray that far from their own expected value, so a forecast that cannot see the noise does not do much better on average. The model is 2.3 better than the seasonal naive forecast, with an interval of -1.94 to 6.16 (2,000 resamples of the 17 weeks, seed 2026): it includes 0. Seventeen weeks cannot say which of the two forecasts is better, and the honest sentence says so.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A series is trend, a repeating pattern and noise; check the pattern against what you know.
- Backtest by repeating the task: forecast each week from the days before it, then score.
- Beat the seasonal naive forecast first, and say so when an interval includes 0.
- Ask of every feature: could I compute it on Sunday night?
- Resample whole weeks, not days; look at the worst weeks.

**Common trap:** a rolling mean that includes the day being forecast. The backtest error falls and nothing was learned.

</Aside>
