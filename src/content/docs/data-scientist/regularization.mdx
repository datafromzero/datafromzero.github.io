## The big idea

A penalty makes every unit of coefficient cost something, so the model keeps only the weight that its people can support.

## Learn by doing

About 25 minutes, 4 new ideas: the penalty and C, scaling before the penalty, choosing C with folds, and where the penalty pays. `recipe` is the day-7 model with a penalty you can change. Everything runs on the 13,362 training people.

### 1. A penalty on big coefficients

**Idea.** A model fits by making its cost small. A *penalty* adds the size of the coefficients to the cost, so a large coefficient has to earn its place:

$$
\text{mean log-loss} + \frac{r(w)}{n\,C}
$$

Here $$w$$ holds the coefficients, $$n$$ is the number of people and $$C$$ is a setting you choose. *Ridge* (an L2 penalty) sets $$r(w)$$ to half the sum of the squared coefficients, *lasso* (an L1 penalty) to the sum of their absolute values. A smaller C means a bigger penalty.

**Try it:** fit a ridge and a lasso at six values of C on the fitting part, and count the coefficients that are not exactly zero.

```python
rows = []
for C in [1.0, 0.1, 0.03, 0.01, 0.003, 0.001]:
    ridge_coef = recipe(C).fit(X_fit, y_fit)[-1].coef_[0]                   # 19 coefficients: 12 text-column levels and 7 number columns
    lasso_coef = recipe(C, lasso=True).fit(X_fit, y_fit)[-1].coef_[0]
    rows.append((C, round(float(np.abs(ridge_coef).max()), 2), int((ridge_coef != 0).sum()), round(float(np.abs(lasso_coef).max()), 2), int((lasso_coef != 0).sum())))
print(pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["C", "ridge: largest", "ridge: not zero", "lasso: largest", "lasso: not zero"]).to_string(index=False))
```

**Predict:** As C falls from 1 to 0.001, the penalty grows a thousand times. Will the coefficients of the ridge reach exactly zero, or shrink and stay above it? And the lasso's?

### 2. Scale before you penalize

**Idea.** A penalty adds up coefficients, and the size of a coefficient depends on the units of its column. So the number columns can go in as they are, or scaled with `StandardScaler` (mean 0, standard deviation 1) inside the pipeline, as in lesson DS5. scikit-learn's guide warns that the l1 and l2 regularizers of linear models "may assume that all features are centered around zero or have variance in the same order".

**Try it:** fit a lasso at C = 0.01 on the number columns as they are and on the scaled columns, and list the columns it keeps.

```python
kept_by = {}
for scaled in (True, False):
    kept_by[scaled] = kept(recipe(0.01, lasso=True, scaled=scaled).fit(X_fit, y_fit))
print("scaled:  ", kept_by[True])
print("unscaled:", kept_by[False])
```

**Predict:** Will the lasso keep the same columns on the raw number columns as on the scaled ones?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the result</summary>

```text
scaled:   ['channel_paid_social', 'channel_referral', 'sessions_7d', 'expenses_7d', 'active_days_7d', 'onboarded', 'bought_plus']
unscaled: ['channel_paid_social', 'channel_referral', 'sessions_7d', 'expenses_7d', 'active_days_7d']
```

</details>

*Why:* No. Scaled, it keeps 7 columns, including `onboarded` and `bought_plus`, which are 0 or 1. Unscaled, it keeps 5, and both flags are gone. A flag runs from 0 to 1, so its coefficient must be large to matter and the penalty charges for it; a count such as `expenses_7d` runs to 34, so a small coefficient costs little. The units, not the people, made the lists differ.

### 3. Choose C on the folds

**Idea.** Choose C like any setting of a model: try values on a log grid, score each on the same folds, and keep the one with the best mean. `GridSearchCV` runs the loop over the folds you give it and keeps the best model as `best_estimator_`. The test part stays outside the search. Report the range of the folds beside the mean, as in lesson DS5.

**Try it:** choose C for the ridge and for the lasso on the five folds of the training part.

```python
grid = {"logisticregression__C": [0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 100]}
searches = {}
for lasso in (False, True):
    searches[lasso] = GridSearchCV(recipe(lasso=lasso), grid, cv=folds, scoring="roc_auc").fit(X_train, y_train)     # the same five folds for every C
table_cv = pd.DataFrame({"C": grid["logisticregression__C"], "ridge": searches[False].cv_results_["mean_test_score"].round(4), "lasso": searches[True].cv_results_["mean_test_score"].round(4)})
print(table_cv.to_string(index=False))
for lasso in (False, True):
    best_row = int(searches[lasso].best_index_)
    scores = [searches[lasso].cv_results_[f"split{k}_test_score"][best_row] for k in range(5)]
    print("lasso" if lasso else "ridge", searches[lasso].best_params_["logisticregression__C"], round(float(min(scores)), 3), round(float(max(scores)), 3))
```

**Predict:** How much will the best C gain over C = 1 in the mean ROC AUC: about 0.0005, about 0.005 or about 0.02?

### 4. Where the penalty pays: few people, many columns

**Idea.** A penalty earns its keep when a model has more columns than its people can pin down. Give a pilot of 500 people 60 extra columns of random numbers, which carry nothing about churn, and fit three models: almost no penalty (C = 1,000,000), a ridge and a lasso, the last two with C chosen on the pilot's five folds. Score each on the 3,341 validation people.

**Try it:** fit the three models on the pilot and score them on the validation people.

### 5. The verdict on the test part

**Idea.** Score the two models of unit 3 on the 4,454 test people, once, beside the day-7 model, with a 95 percent interval on each gain from 2,000 resamples (seed 2026, as in lesson DS2), and count the columns each keeps.

The ridge's gain is 0.000 with a 95 percent interval of -0.001 to 0.000; the lasso's is -0.001, -0.003 to 0.001. Both include 0. The penalty gave no better ranking here; the lasso scores 0.689 with 12 columns not zero, where the ridge has 19.

<Aside type="tip" title="Remember">

- A penalty adds the size of the coefficients to the cost; a smaller C is a stronger brake.
- Ridge shrinks every coefficient; lasso can set some to exactly zero.
- Scale the number columns first, inside the pipeline, and choose C on the same folds for every value.
- A penalty helps when people are few for the columns; with plenty of people it changes little.

**Common trap:** choosing C by the score on the test part. The test part is for the one honest score at the end.

</Aside>
